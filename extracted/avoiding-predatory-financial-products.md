## Start here

[paragraph] A financial offer should not require the decision speed of a game-show buzzer, especially when it wants access to your bank account, your vehicle or the next 104 Wednesdays.

[paragraph] Urgency is a reason to investigate, not a reason to skip the investigation.

[paragraph] In this Red-Flag Investigation, you will examine payday loans, title loans, rent-to-own plans, buy now/pay later financing and credit-repair guarantees. Your job is to uncover three facts behind every promise: the full cost, the collateral or account access, and the consequences if the plan goes wrong.

[heading] WHAT “PREDATORY” MEANS HERE

[paragraph] A pattern of high pressure, hidden or confusing cost, unfair leverage, deceptive claims or unsuitable risk.

[paragraph] It is not a legal verdict on every product or provider. Investigate the actual agreement and the rules where you live.

[spotlight] If an offer becomes less attractive when you ask for the total repayment in writing, the question did valuable work.

[heading] The Three-Part Investigation

[cards]
  - LAYER 1 — COST — Find the amount received or retail price, every fee, the APR when applicable, the payment count, the due dates and the total dollars paid.
  - LAYER 2 — COLLATERAL OR ACCESS — Identify any lien, item ownership condition, pre-authorized debit or card charge. Also note any account permission, security device or personal information the provider receives.
  - LAYER 3 — CONSEQUENCE — Write what happens after a missed payment, failed debit, cancellation or dispute. Include fees, collections, credit reporting, repossession or loss of a promotional rate.

[myths]
  - “If a product is legal or the provider says it is approved, it must be affordable.” — Rules may limit certain charges or require disclosure, but they do not decide whether the repayment fits your cash flow. Verify the provider where required, then test the agreement against your own budget and alternatives.

## Evidence Room One: Translate the Price Into Total Dollars

[paragraph] Small daily, weekly or instalment prices make an offer feel manageable. Translate them before comparing.

[paragraph] For a loan, add principal, interest and every fee. For a purchase plan, multiply the payment by the number of payments, then add setup, delivery, late, processing, buyout or cancellation charges that apply.

[cards]
  - FILE A — PAYDAY LOAN — $600 + $84 = $684 — Using FCAC’s current $14-per-$100 illustration, borrowing $600 creates an $84 borrowing cost. The short deadline matters as much as the fee because the repayment must coexist with rent, food and other bills.
  - FILE B — RENT-TO-OWN — $22 × 104 = $2,288 — FCAC’s sofa example compares an $800 retail price with $22 each week for 104 weeks. The multiplication reveals $2,288 before tax, which is $1,488 above the retail price.

  url: https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/loans/payday-loans.html

[source] FCAC: Rent-to-own plans
  url: https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/loans/rent-to-own.html

[heading] The Cost Questions That Slow the Sales Pitch

[ordered]
  - How much money or value do I receive? — Use the cash advanced or the comparable retail price, not the advertised payment.
  - How much will I pay in total? — Include every scheduled payment and fee, plus the amount required to own the item or end the contract.
  - When does each payment leave? — Place the dates beside income and essential bills. Affordable in total can still be impossible on the due date.
  - What changes after one missed payment? — Check late and NSF fees, interest changes, collection rights and whether earlier payments are lost.

[myths]
  - “A low weekly payment proves the product is inexpensive.” — Payment size shows timing, not total cost. Multiply payment by payment count, add all fees and compare the result with the cash received or ordinary retail price.

[spotlight] The most important price is often the number the advertisement does not headline: total dollars paid from start to finish.

  ¶ Asha calculates $684 instead of focusing on the $84 fee alone. Repaying the full amount in 12 days would push groceries and insurance into the next shortage. The loan solves Monday while quietly creating a second Monday. She writes “timing failure” across the investigation file and keeps looking.
  ¶ The title lender offers Asha $2,000, more than the repair requires, using the car as security. The larger approval sounds generous until she writes “vehicle and route to work” under collateral. The BNPL button is less dramatic, but its debit date lands one day before insurance. Convenience has discovered calendar conflict.
  ¶ Asha verifies Ontario payday rules, title-loan collateral and the BNPL schedule through official sources. A credit-repair message promises a “guaranteed reset” for an upfront fee. It fails the independent-source test, so it joins her evidence file, not her bank statement.
  ¶ Asha asks the repair shop for a written split: $300 now and $300 on payday, with no added fee in this fictional scenario. She uses $250 from emergency savings and $50 from current cash for the first payment, then schedules the second. She declines the larger loans, keeps the car out of a lien and starts a modest repair sinking fund. Monday remains inconvenient; it no longer owns the next six months.

## Evidence Room Two: Follow the Collateral and Account Access

[cards]
  - FILE C — TITLE LOAN — THE VEHICLE SECURES THE DEBT — The borrower may keep driving, but missed payments may lead to fees, credit damage or seizure. Some lenders may use GPS or immobilizer devices where permitted. Losing transport can also threaten income and care routines.
  - FILE D — BUY NOW, PAY LATER — BNPL IS CREDIT — Even when the promotion says 0%, agreements may use automatic bank debits or card charges. A failed payment may create provider fees, an NSF fee, a higher rate or credit risk depending on the plan.

[heading] Six Red Flags for the Investigation Board

[cards]
  - 1. SMALL-PRICE FRAMING — Daily, weekly or instalment cost appears without the total repayment.
  - 2. MISSING DISCLOSURE — APR, fees, payment count, due dates, ownership conditions or cancellation terms are unclear.
  - 3. MONEY BEFORE CLARITY — An upfront payment is demanded before the loan arrives or before a complete written service agreement is provided.
  - 4. PRESSURE — The seller discourages comparison, creates a false deadline or asks for a signature before questions are answered.
  - 5. UNCLEAR ACCESS — Automatic debits, card charges, liens, device permissions or cancellation steps are buried or incomplete.
  - 6. GUARANTEES OR THREATS — The provider promises a result it cannot control or claims normal consumer rights vanish immediately.

[myths]
  - “No credit check means there is no risk to my credit or property.” — Approval method and consequence are different questions. A product may still use a lien, automatic account access, collections or credit reporting. Read what secures the agreement and what follows a missed payment.

[spotlight] Collateral changes the question from “Can I make the payment?” to “What essential part of my life is exposed if I cannot?”

## Evidence Room Three: Verify Outside the Sales Screen

[paragraph] A polished website or seller-supplied licence number is not independent evidence. Leave the sales link.

[paragraph] Use official regulator or consumer-protection pages. Confirm the legal business name, contact details and required licence or registration.

[heading] FILE E — CREDIT-REPAIR GUARANTEE

[paragraph] Accurate information cannot be erased just because a company charges a fee.

[paragraph] Credit bureaus must correct errors for free, while rebuilding accurate history takes time and better payment behaviour. A guaranteed fast score increase is a claim to investigate, not a result to purchase.

[source] FCAC: Debt and credit-repair alert
  url: https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/debt/debt-help/alert-debt-credit-repair.html

[heading] The Independent Verification Route

[cards]
  - LAYER 1 — PAUSE AND PRESERVE — Save the advertisement, quote, full agreement, payment schedule and messages. Do not send credentials, remote-access permission or money while material facts are missing.
  - LAYER 2 — CONFIRM THE PROVIDER — Use the official provincial, territorial or federal route that applies. Match the business name, address and contact details; do not rely on a link supplied only by the seller.
  - LAYER 3 — COMPARE THE EXIT — Ask how to cancel, repay early, remove a lien, stop future debits, return an item or complain. Write the procedure and cost before entering.

## The Safer-Alternative Ladder

[ordered]
  - FIRST — Ask the biller, repair shop, landlord, service provider or current lender about extra time, a split payment or another hardship option. Get any agreement in writing.
  - THEN — Use an available emergency or sinking fund, or delay or reduce the purchase. Sell an unused item, or test support from a trusted person if safe and appropriate.
  - ONLY AFTER COMPARISON — Check the total cost and due-date fit of existing overdraft, credit, line-of-credit or small-loan options. Availability, fees and suitability vary; borrowing is not automatically the answer.

[myths]
  - “A professional website or official-looking badge proves the provider is legitimate.” — A website is evidence of a website. Confirm the legal business and any required licence through an official source, independently verify contact details and stop if information does not match.

[spotlight] Real verification happens outside the seller’s page. The provider does not get to grade its own identity check.

[heading] Province and Territory Lens

[paragraph] Rules for these products, cancellations, collections and licensing differ across Canada. This applies in Alberta, British Columbia, New Brunswick, Ontario, Prince Edward Island, and Newfoundland and Labrador. Verify fee limits, cooling-off periods, licence checks and complaint routes through your official consumer affairs office.

[source] Office of Consumer Affairs: Consumer affairs offices
  url: https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/office-consumer-affairs/en/federal-provincial-and-territorial-consumer-affairs-offices

[heading] Close the Investigation With a Decision, Not a Feeling

[myths]
  - “A credit-repair company can remove accurate negative information if I pay enough.” — Only inaccurate information can be corrected through a dispute. Accurate history generally improves through time and responsible credit behaviour. No company controls a lender’s approval or can guarantee a specific score increase.

[spotlight] A guarantee is strongest when the seller controls the result. A credit-repair seller does not control the credit bureaus, scoring models or future lender decisions.

[heading] Red-Flag Investigation Closing Checklist

[ordered]
  - The advertised price was translated. — Total repayment includes payment count, interest and every disclosed fee.
  - Collateral and access are visible. — Liens, ownership conditions, automatic debits and account permissions are understood.
  - The bad-month outcome is written. — Missed-payment, cancellation, collection, credit and repossession consequences are not assumed.
  - The provider was independently checked. — Official sources, not seller-supplied badges, confirm the relevant identity or licence facts.
  - At least two alternatives were compared. — Total cost, payment timing and exposed essentials support the final decision.

[takeaway] Do not judge a financial product by approval speed or the size of one payment.

[spotlight] Walking away is not a failed application. It is a completed investigation that found the risk before the risk found your account.

## Glossary of Terms

[glossary]
  - Predatory pattern — Pressure, hidden or confusing cost, unfair leverage or deceptive claims that can create unsuitable risk.
  - Total repayment — All principal, interest, scheduled payments and fees paid from the start to the end of an agreement.
  - APR — Annual percentage rate, a yearly measure used to describe and compare borrowing cost when applicable.
  - Collateral — Property pledged to support repayment; the lender may have rights against it after default.
  - Lien — A registered claim against property, such as a vehicle, that remains until the secured obligation is resolved.
  - Pre-authorized debit — Permission for an organization to withdraw scheduled payments from a bank account.
  - Payday loan — A short-term, high-cost loan generally intended to be repaid around the borrower’s next payday.
  - Buy now, pay later — Credit that defers a purchase balance or divides it into scheduled payments.
  - Rent-to-own — A rental arrangement in which ownership occurs only after the agreement’s conditions are met.
  - Credit-repair guarantee — A promise of fast or assured credit improvement that the seller cannot independently control.

## Check-in

1. THE PROMISE — What does the advertisement say the product will solve, how quickly, and what payment does it headline?
2. COST — Record the amount received or retail price, APR when applicable, every fee, payment count and total repayment. Show the calculation.
3. COLLATERAL OR ACCESS — List any lien, item ownership condition, automatic debit, card charge, device permission or personal information requested.
4. CONSEQUENCES AND EXIT — What happens after a missed payment, failed debit, cancellation, early repayment, return or dispute? What does the exit cost?
5. RED-FLAG TEST — Mark small-price framing, missing disclosure, money before clarity, pressure, unclear access, guarantees or threats. Which facts remain missing?
6. INDEPENDENT VERIFICATION — Which official office or registry applies? Record the legal business name, licence result where required and any mismatch.
7. ALTERNATIVE LADDER — Compare at least two options by total cost, due-date fit and exposed collateral. Include a non-borrowing option where possible.
8. DECISION RECORD — Choose decline, pause, modify or proceed. State the evidence, next action, owner and review date. If facts are incomplete, the decision is pause.
