## Start here

[paragraph] Starting with little or no Canadian credit history can feel like being asked for job experience before anyone will give you the first job.

[paragraph] Mildly inconvenient? Yes. Permanent problem? No. The point of this lesson is to build useful credit history without turning your wallet into a science experiment.

[paragraph] Having little Canadian credit history is not the same as having a bad one. It only means lenders and bureaus have less activity to look at. FCAC notes that a thin or poor history can make borrowing harder, and that your score shifts over time as lenders update your file.

[source] FCAC: Credit report and score basics
  url: https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/credit-reports-score/credit-report-score-basics.html

[spotlight] No Canadian credit history is not the same thing as bad credit. You are not repairing a broken record here. You are starting one from scratch, which is a much easier job.

## The First Credit Ladder

[paragraph] Your goal is not to race to the top. Build one rung that can safely hold your weight, then repeat the behaviour that makes the account useful.

[ordered]
  - Choose one suitable starting product — — Look for an account you can afford, understand and realistically manage every month.
  - Confirm reporting — — Ask whether the provider reports the account to Equifax, TransUnion or both. A product cannot build your bureau file if nothing about it ever reaches a bureau.
  - Plan light use — — Give the account one or two small jobs that are already in your budget, instead of inventing new spending to make the card feel important.
  - Pay reliably — — Protect the due date above everything else. Paying the whole statement balance is the cleaner habit whenever you can manage it.
  - Check the result — — Once the account has had time to report, read your credit files and check that every detail is right.

[spotlight] TransUnion’s advice for building from scratch begins with “start slow.” One card you can manage beats several rushed applications, each one bringing a hard inquiry and another payment to remember.

[source] TransUnion Canada: 5 Ways to Build Your Credit from Scratch
  url: https://www.transunion.ca/build-credit


## Choose Your First Rung, Not Your Forever Product

[paragraph] A starter product only has to do a few things well: fit your budget, report the account, keep its costs clear, and leave you with a payment routine you can keep up. Here are the usual routes worth considering:

[cards]
  - Student or newcomer credit card. — Some banks offer cards for people with little Canadian credit history. The terms vary a lot, so compare the actual agreement rather than the label on the ad.
  - Secured credit card. — You put down a deposit and receive a credit limit set by the issuer’s terms. FCAC says this can be an option when you have no credit history yet. Course 6 covers secured cards in full.
  - Credit-builder or secured loan. — Check the total cost, when the money reaches you, how to cancel, and exactly what gets reported. Do not borrow to create debt when a cheaper route does the same job.
  - Additional cardholder / authorized user. — This gives you access to someone else’s card account. Do not assume it puts anything on your own Canadian credit file. Ask the issuer how they report it before you count on it.

[source] FCAC: Choosing a credit card — Secured credit cards
  url: https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/credit-cards/choose-credit-card.html

[source] FCAC: Joint credit cards — Additional cardholder and authorized user
  url: https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/credit-cards/joint-credit-card.html

[heading] DON’T BUY THE LABEL

[paragraph] A product called “credit builder” is still a financial product with fees, terms and risks. A large fee wearing a motivational slogan is still a large fee.

[heading] The Seven-Question Product Filter

[paragraph] Before you apply, make the product earn a place in your budget. Ask:

[ordered]
  - Reporting — — Which Canadian credit bureau or bureaus receive account information?
  - Total cost — — What are the annual, monthly and setup fees you cannot avoid? What interest rate applies if you carry a balance?
  - Deposit — — Is a deposit required, where is it held, and what does it take to get it back?
  - Payment — — Can you easily find the statement, the due date, the minimum payment and the ways to pay?
  - Limit — — Is the limit small enough to manage without stress, but big enough for the expense you have in mind?
  - Exit or upgrade — — Can you close it, change it or graduate from it without a nasty surprise?
  - Purpose — — Does this solve a real need, or are you applying because the ad used words like “guaranteed” and a very confident font?

[spotlight] Reporting matters. TransUnion notes that companies do not have to report account information at all, so ask first, before you rely on a product to build your file.

[source] TransUnion Canada: Frequently Asked Questions — How do I build a good credit history?
  url: https://www.transunion.ca/customer-support/faq

[heading] Quick Myth Check

[myths]
  - “No score means I already have bad credit.” — A thin or new file just means there is not much history to look at yet. That is a different thing from a file full of missed payments.
  - “Opening several cards at once will build credit faster.” — More applications mean more hard inquiries and more accounts to keep track of. TransUnion recommends starting slowly, with one card you can manage.
  - “I need to carry a balance and pay interest so the score knows I am serious.” — You can build a payment record without paying a cent of purchase interest. FCAC encourages clearing the balance by the due date, because carrying one only costs you more.
  - “Being an authorized user automatically builds my Canadian credit file.” — Do not assume it does. An additional card is tied to the main cardholder’s account. Ask the issuer how it reports before you lean on it to build your own file.


## Build the Routine That Actually Does the Work

[paragraph] Once the account is open, the routine matters far more than the product. Treat the account as a small monthly reliability test, not a permission slip to spend more.

[cards]
  - Give it one planned job. — A phone bill, transit pass or one small grocery category is enough. The purchase should already exist in your budget.
  - Keep the balance well below the limit. — You do not need to chase a magic percentage. Just stay well clear of the limit, and never start treating the card as extra income.
  - Protect the due date. — Pay at least the minimum by the due date. When you can afford the whole statement balance, pay that instead. It keeps the routine simple and can avoid purchase interest under the card’s grace-period rules.
  - Turn on alerts. — Use payment-due and transaction alerts as guardrails, not as a replacement for reading the statement.
  - Check every statement. — Go through the purchases, the payment posting, the fees, and anything you do not recognize.
  - Verify the bureau file later. — Once the account has had time to report, check your credit report and make sure it shows up as yours, and shows up correctly.


## Partial Payment vs. Full Payment: What Changes?

[paragraph] Paying the minimum by the due date meets what the card requires of you, but whatever is left over can start earning interest. Paying the full statement balance usually avoids purchase interest, as long as the card’s grace-period rules apply.

[paragraph] The lesson here is not that paying in full earns secret score points. On-time payment protects your payment history, and a smaller carried balance keeps both the debt and the utilization easy to handle. The scoring formula is not a vending machine. Inserting $23 of interest does not dispense 15 bonus points.

[source] FCAC: Paying off your credit card
  url: https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/credit-cards/pay-off-credit-card.html

[source] FCAC: How credit cards work — Interest-free grace periods
  url: https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/credit-cards/credit-card-work.html

[spotlight] You do not need to carry a balance to prove you can handle credit. Paying in full can avoid purchase interest. Paying only the minimum makes the debt slower and dearer to clear.

  detail: SEAN’S EXTREMELY UNEXCITING SUCCESS STORY
  - Sean is 23 and wants to rent an apartment next year without asking his aunt to co-sign the lease. For that he needs a credit file. At the moment he does not have one.
  - His first Canadian credit card arrives with a $1,000 limit. He props it against the kettle for a day, the way you would a certificate.
  - There is a television he has been looking at for a while. It costs $997. Buying it would certainly make the card feel included.
  - He does not buy the television. He gives the card one job instead: his $55 phone bill.
  - The statement arrives. Sean checks the amount and the due date, then pays the whole $55 out of money already sitting in his budget for the phone bill. He does the same thing the next month, and the month after that.
  - Nothing dramatic happens for the rest of the year, which is exactly what is supposed to happen.
  - What Sean does not do is add shopping trips to “feed the score,” open three more cards because one looked lonely, or leave $11 unpaid to donate interest to the cause.
  - Eleven months later he pulls his credit report. The account is there, accurate and dull. He signed the lease on his own. The television, by then, was on sale.

[spotlight] Credit-building is evidence gathered over time. TransUnion says a good credit history takes a while to establish. No honest provider will promise you a particular score on a particular date.

## Glossary of Terms

[glossary]
  - Limited / thin credit file — A credit file with little history on it for lenders and scoring systems to judge.
  - Credit history — The record of how you have used and repaid credit over time.
  - Secured credit card — A credit card backed by a deposit you put down under the issuer’s terms.
  - Security deposit — Money you put down to secure a product, which is not the same thing as paying your monthly card bill.
  - Credit limit — The most you can borrow on a revolving account at any one time.
  - Statement balance — The balance shown on your card statement for a finished billing period.
  - Minimum payment — The smallest amount the issuer requires you to pay by the due date on that statement.
  - Due date — The date your required payment has to arrive, under your agreement.
  - Grace period — A window that can let eligible purchases avoid interest when you pay the required balance on the card’s terms.
  - Credit utilization — How much revolving credit you are using compared with the limit available.
  - Hard inquiry — A credit check tied to applying for new credit, which may affect your score.
  - Bureau reporting — A lender or provider sending your account information to Equifax, TransUnion or both.
  - Authorized user / additional cardholder — Someone allowed to use a card tied to the main cardholder’s account. Do not assume it creates a separate account on your own file.
