## Start here

[paragraph] Retirement planning is the work of turning future income, savings and benefits into a paycheque you can actually trust — not a contest to reach one dramatic account balance. A large number with no withdrawal plan can still produce a very confusing Tuesday.

[paragraph] This course helps you find where your retirement income will actually come from, price what it needs to cover, and get organized before you sit down with a real financial planner or tax professional. It is general Canadian education, not personal advice — pension rules and tax treatment shift, so verify anything time-sensitive with the right government office or professional before you rely on it.

[chips]
  - Identify the main layers of Canadian retirement income — government, workplace and personal.
  - Estimate retirement spending in plain, monthly, no-jargon terms.
  - Explain how your starting age changes CPP or QPP and OAS for life.
  - Recognize the different job each account does: RRSP, RRIF, TFSA, workplace pension and non-registered savings.
  - Spot major retirement risks before they turn into emergencies.
  - Build your own one-page retirement income map.

[myths]
  - “Retirement planning is mainly about choosing the right investments.” — Investments matter, but so do spending, taxes, pension dates, housing, insurance, benefit eligibility and estate instructions. A great portfolio cannot fix a plan that forgot about its bills.

## Start With the Life, Then Price It

[paragraph] A useful retirement plan starts with the life you expect to live — the account balance comes second. Picture an ordinary month: where will you live, is a mortgage or rent still due, are you driving, taking transit or somehow keeping two vehicles alive? Add travel and hobbies, but also prescriptions, dental care, home repairs, insurance, and the one appliance that always waits for a long weekend before it decides to retire before you do.

[paragraph] Split spending into three buckets. Essentials keep the household running. Flexible spending covers choices like dining, entertainment and travel. Irregular costs — property tax, vehicle repairs, insurance renewals, a new roof — show up less often but hit harder. Divide any yearly irregular cost by twelve so it stops ambushing you disguised as a surprise.

[paragraph] Do not assume every cost drops the day work ends. Commuting and payroll deductions may shrink, but health, home support, travel and leisure costs can grow — and taxes do not retire either. CPP or QPP, OAS, workplace pension payments, and RRSP or RRIF withdrawals can all create taxable income.

[spotlight] Retirement spending usually moves in stages. An active early retirement often means more travel; later years tend to lean toward health, accessibility and support at home. Build more than one version of the budget.

[paragraph] Inflation matters because retirement can run for decades. You do not need to predict the exact price of groceries in 2047 — you do need to check whether your income sources and savings can keep up as prices climb.

[source] FCAC: Planning and saving for retirement
  url: https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/retirement-planning.html

## Build Your Retirement Income Layers

[paragraph] Most Canadians will use several income sources at once, and each layer follows its own rules.

[cards]
  - GOVERNMENT PENSIONS & BENEFITS — CPP — QPP in Quebec — is built mainly from your contributions, earnings history and start age. OAS runs on age, legal status and Canadian residence. GIS tops up eligible low-income OAS recipients. The number in the news is a maximum, not a personal forecast — your own My Service Canada Account or Retraite Québec record is the only number worth trusting.
  - WORKPLACE PENSIONS & EMPLOYER PLANS — A defined benefit pension pays from a formula built on salary, service and retirement age. A defined contribution pension invests what goes in, so the payout depends on contributions, returns, fees and how you withdraw. Ask the plan administrator whether yours is indexed, whether survivor benefits apply, and whether early retirement changes the amount.
  - PERSONAL SAVINGS & OTHER RESOURCES — RRSPs, RRIFs, TFSAs, non-registered investments, annuities, business or rental income and home equity can all pitch in. Home equity is patient capital, not a direct deposit — selling, downsizing, borrowing against or renting part of it each carry their own costs and consequences.

[spotlight] Keep old pension statements. A short job from years ago may have left behind a pension entitlement or a locked-in account. A retirement filing cabinet occasionally contains money, not only warranties for appliances nobody owns anymore.

[source] Government of Canada: Canadian Retirement Income Calculator
  url: https://www.canada.ca/en/services/benefits/publicpensions/cpp/retirement-income-calculator.html

## Decide When Income Should Begin

[paragraph] The standard CPP starting age is 65, inside a window from 60 to 70. Start before 65 and the pension is permanently reduced by 0.6% for every month early — 36% lower at the earliest, age 60. Start after 65 and it climbs 0.7% for every month of delay, up to 42% higher at 70. Quebec's QPP runs on its own rules, opening between 60 and 72 with 65 still treated as normal; confirm current timing adjustments with Retraite Québec directly.

[paragraph] Eligible Canadians can generally start OAS at 65 or delay to 70, gaining 0.6% for every month of delay — up to 36% more at 70, with no further increase after that. Delaying needs extra care if GIS might apply: you cannot collect GIS while OAS is deferred, and GIS does not get the same delay bonus.

[spotlight] CPP or QPP and OAS are two separate decisions. Starting one does not force you to start the other on the same day.

[myths]
  - “Everyone should start CPP at the same age.” — Starting age changes the payment permanently, but the better choice depends on health, cash needs, taxes, other income and how long you expect to need the money.

[heading] Use scenarios, not one prediction

[paragraph] Test at least three versions of your plan before you trust any of them. The goal is not predicting the future perfectly — it is finding decisions that stay workable when the future refuses to read your spreadsheet.

[ordered]
  - An expected retirement date, normal spending — Build the plan you actually expect to live.
  - An earlier retirement or a work interruption — Test what happens if the plan starts sooner than scheduled.
  - A longer life with higher health or support costs — Stress-test the plan against decades, not just years.

## Asha and Leon Build the Bridge

[story] Meet Asha and Leon
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ Asha is 61 and Leon is 64. They have a small defined benefit pension, two RRSPs, TFSAs and no consumer debt. Their first plan was simple: retire when Leon turned 65 and start every benefit immediately.
  ¶ They collected their pension statements and built a monthly spending estimate, including travel to visit family, home repairs and a future accessibility budget. Then they ran the numbers through the Canadian Retirement Income Calculator and compared several starting dates.
  ¶ The couple learned they did not need every income source at once. Leon's workplace pension and planned RRSP withdrawals could cover the first stage, so they asked a fee-for-service planner and tax professional to test whether delaying one public pension made sense, while keeping a larger cash reserve for the first two years.
  ¶ Their final plan was not flashy. It was clear. They knew which account would pay for what, which dates needed review, and what would make them change course. The improvement came from coordination — Asha and Leon already had the resources, they just turned them into a sequence.

[heading] What resolved the conflict?

[paragraph] They combined lender-grade evidence with a household stress test and let the lower, safer number guide the timeline — the same discipline your income map should run on.

## Give Each Account a Job

[paragraph] Account labels describe tax rules. They do not create a complete withdrawal plan by themselves.

[cards]
  - RRSP & RRIF — RRSP contributions can earn a deduction; withdrawals are generally taxable income. An RRSP must mature by the end of the year you turn 71 — into cash, a RRIF, or an eligible annuity. A RRIF pays retirement income, with minimum withdrawals starting the year after it's set up; at setup only, you may be able to use a younger spouse's age to shrink that minimum.
  - TFSA — TFSA withdrawals are generally tax-free, and the room you use comes back — just not until next calendar year. That flexibility makes it useful for repairs, travel or a rough year, but flexible doesn't automatically mean 'spend first' or 'spend last.' The right order depends on your taxes, investments, estate plans and benefit eligibility.
  - NON-REGISTERED SAVINGS — Outside an RRSP or TFSA, interest, dividends and capital gains all get taxed differently. Track the adjusted cost base of what you hold and keep every tax slip — selling an investment can create taxable income even when the cash is just funding ordinary groceries.

[spotlight] Withholding tax on an RRSP withdrawal is only a down payment on the real bill. Your final tax depends on total income and deductions for the whole year, settled when your return is assessed.

[myths]
  - “An RRSP balance is the same as cash in a bank account.” — RRSP withdrawals are generally taxable. The number that actually matters is the after-tax income the account can support over time — not the sticker price on the statement.

[paragraph] A real withdrawal plan asks which account should fund which stage of retirement — considering annual taxable income, public pension timing, RRIF minimums, pension credits, possible income splitting and both partners' needs. For some households, modest RRSP withdrawals before the big pensions and RRIF minimums begin can ease future tax pressure; for others, protecting registered savings matters more. This is a planning question, not a slogan — model it with a qualified professional before making a large withdrawal.

## Protect the Plan From Predictable Risks

[ordered]
  - LONGEVITY RISK — Living longer is good news that still needs funding. Test your plan well past average life expectancy, especially if longevity runs in the family or one partner is younger.
  - INFLATION RISK — Know which income is indexed and which is fixed. CPP and OAS adjust under their program rules; a workplace pension might be fully, partially or never indexed. Cash sitting in savings doesn't grow just because it's being polite.
  - MARKET & SEQUENCE RISK — Bad investment returns right at the start of retirement can do outsized damage when you're withdrawing at the same time. A cash reserve, a sensible asset mix and a written withdrawal policy can reduce how often you're forced to sell into a downturn.
  - HEALTH, CARE & HOUSING RISK — Provincial health coverage doesn't pay for everything — prescriptions, dental care, mobility equipment, home modifications and long-term support can all fall outside it. Review insurance before you retire, since employer coverage often ends or changes.
  - FAMILY & FRAUD RISK — Supporting adult kids, grandkids or aging relatives is common in retirement — put a limit and a purpose around it so it doesn't quietly consume essential income. Use unique passwords and multi-factor authentication, and remember that no real government agency demands instant payment by gift card or cryptocurrency.

[spotlight] A retirement plan needs a named backup person who knows the plan exists and where the records live. They do not need every password today — they just need to know where to look.

[heading] What changes by province or territory?

[paragraph] Federal programs like CPP and OAS apply almost everywhere, but where you live still shapes the plan. Quebec runs its own QPP, pension agency and tax system. Pension standards, locked-in account rules, drug coverage, home care and senior benefits can all vary by province or territory — do not assume a benefit your friend gets somewhere else exists under the same name where you live. Write your province or territory on page one of your income map, and record the governing jurisdiction next to every workplace pension or locked-in account.

[heading] Key takeaway

[paragraph] Retirement planning is income coordination, not a contest to reach one impressive number. Start with realistic spending, map every income layer, compare starting dates, give each account a job, and stress-test the plan against a longer life and higher costs. The strongest plan is not the one with the flashiest projection — it is the one you can explain, monitor and adjust.

[spotlight] Insider tip: build a retirement decision calendar — the date a workplace pension estimate expires, when you will review CPP or QPP and OAS timing, the year an RRSP must mature, insurance conversion deadlines, mortgage renewal dates and an annual beneficiary review. A calendar turns distant rules into appointments you can actually keep.

## Quick Check: Choose the Best Next Move

[accordions]
  - What three factors commonly shape a CPP or QPP retirement pension? — Your starting age, your contribution history and your earnings history.
  - Why convert irregular annual costs — like property tax or a roof — into a monthly figure? — It makes the budget honest and turns a once-a-year surprise into a predictable line item.
  - What happens to CPP under current rules if it starts before or after 65? — It is permanently reduced for every month before 65, and permanently increased for every month of delay up to age 70.
  - Why can one large RRSP withdrawal matter for more than just the account balance? — The withdrawal is generally taxable income, and a large one can push you into a higher bracket or affect income-tested benefits.
  - Name two ways your province or territory can change a retirement plan. — Examples include QPP in Quebec, provincial tax brackets and credits, pension standards, locked-in account rules, drug coverage, home care and senior benefits.

## Glossary of Terms

[glossary]
  - Annuity — A contract that provides scheduled income, often for a fixed period or for life.
  - Canada Pension Plan (CPP) — A contributory public pension for eligible workers outside Quebec.
  - Defined benefit pension — A workplace pension that generally calculates retirement income using a plan formula.
  - Defined contribution pension — A workplace pension in which contributions are invested and the eventual income depends on the accumulated value and withdrawal choices.
  - Guaranteed Income Supplement (GIS) — An income-tested federal benefit for eligible low-income OAS recipients.
  - Indexing — An adjustment intended to help income respond to changes in prices or inflation.
  - Old Age Security (OAS) — A federal pension based mainly on age, legal status and Canadian residence history.
  - Quebec Pension Plan (QPP) — Quebec's contributory public pension plan.
  - Registered Retirement Income Fund (RRIF) — A registered arrangement that receives eligible retirement funds and pays taxable retirement income.
  - Registered Retirement Savings Plan (RRSP) — A registered savings plan whose contributions may be deductible and whose withdrawals are generally taxable.
  - Sequence risk — The risk that poor investment returns early in retirement will cause greater damage while withdrawals are occurring.
  - Tax-Free Savings Account (TFSA) — A registered account in which eligible investment growth and withdrawals are generally tax-free.
