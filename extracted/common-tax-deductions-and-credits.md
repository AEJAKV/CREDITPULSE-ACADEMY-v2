## Start here

[paragraph] Deduction. Credit.

[paragraph] People use them interchangeably all the time. They are not the same thing, and mixing them up is one of the most common sources of tax confusion in this whole course.

[paragraph] Here’s the short version. A deduction shrinks the income you get taxed on. A credit shrinks the tax bill itself, after that math is already done.

[paragraph] Once that distinction clicks, almost everything else in this module gets easier.

## How each one actually works

[paragraph] Deductions come first. A deduction is subtracted from your income, before tax gets calculated at all.

[paragraph] Say you earned $60,000, and you had a $5,000 deduction. Your taxable income drops to $55,000. Tax gets calculated on that smaller number.

[paragraph] The value of a deduction depends on your tax bracket — the shelf idea from Module 2.2. A deduction is worth more to someone on a higher shelf, because it’s shielding income that would otherwise have been taxed at a higher rate.

[paragraph] Credits come after. A credit works differently. Tax gets calculated first, on your full taxable income. Then the credit is subtracted directly from the tax bill itself.

[paragraph] Most credits in Canada are non-refundable, meaning they can only bring your tax bill down to zero — they can’t push you into a refund beyond what you already paid in. A smaller number of credits are refundable, meaning you get the money regardless of how much tax you owed in the first place.

[paragraph] That refundable-versus-non-refundable line matters enormously, and we’ll come back to it.

[cards]
  - Applied to — Deduction: Your income, before tax is calculated — Credit: Your tax bill, after it’s calculated
  - Worth more to — Deduction: Higher earners, on a higher shelf — Credit: Depends on refundable vs non-refundable
  - Example — Deduction: RRSP contribution — Credit: Basic Personal Amount

[myths]
  - “A $1,000 deduction and a $1,000 credit save you the same amount of tax.” — They usually don’t. A $1,000 deduction saves you $1,000 times your marginal rate — more for higher earners, less for lower ones. A $1,000 credit typically saves a flat amount, calculated at the lowest tax rate, regardless of your income. They’re built on completely different math.

## The deductions most people actually use

[cards]
  - RRSP contributions. — The best-known deduction. Money you put into a registered retirement account comes off your taxable income for that year.
  - Union and professional dues. — If your job requires membership dues, they’re often deductible — check your T4 slip, which usually lists them.
  - Child care expenses. — Daycare, camps, and caregivers, generally claimed by the lower-earning spouse.
  - Moving expenses. — If you moved at least 40 kilometres closer to a new job or school.

## The refundable vs. non-refundable line

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: THE REFUNDABLE VS. NON-REFUNDABLE LINE

[spotlight] This is the single most important distinction in this entire module, and it comes up again and again across this course. A non-refundable credit can only reduce tax you actually owe. If your tax bill is already near zero — a common situation for lower-income or part-time earners — claiming more non-refundable credits doesn’t put extra money in your pocket. There’s simply no tax bill left to reduce. A refundable credit is different. You get it regardless of how much tax you owed. The GST/HST credit and the Canada Workers Benefit are both refundable, which is exactly why they matter so much for lower-income households. Before assuming any credit is or isn’t worth claiming, check which kind it is. The paperwork is often identical. The payoff is not.

## The credits nearly everyone claims

[cards]
  - Basic Personal Amount. — Automatic, and covered in Module 2.1 — the first slice of your income that’s never taxed.
  - Canada employment amount. — A small automatic credit for anyone with employment income, meant to offset basic work-related costs.
  - Tuition credit. — For eligible tuition paid to a recognized institution. Unused amounts carry forward, or can be transferred to a spouse or parent.
  - Charitable donation credit. — A percentage of eligible donations, with the rate increasing for larger annual totals.
  - Medical expense credit. — Eligible costs above a threshold, for you, your spouse, and dependants.

[paragraph] This is the everyday list — the ones most Canadians touch at some point. Module 1.8 in the Platinum tier goes deeper into the credits people commonly forget entirely, like caregiving support and medical travel.

[myths]
  - “Claiming more deductions and credits always means a bigger refund.” — Not always. Deductions and non-refundable credits only help you if you have income or tax to offset in the first place. Piling up claims beyond what your income or tax bill can use doesn’t create extra refund out of nowhere — it just goes unused, or in some cases carries forward to a future year.

## Sorting your own claims

[chips]
  - I know which of my claims are deductions, and which are credits.
  - I know whether each credit I’m claiming is refundable or non-refundable.
  - I’ve checked my T4 for union or professional dues I might be missing.
  - If I have unused tuition credits, I know whether to carry them forward or transfer them.
  - I haven’t assumed a bigger pile of claims automatically means a bigger refund.

## Farida and the RRSP question

[story] Farida and the RRSP question
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ Farida’s coworker told her, with total confidence, that maxing out her RRSP every year was simply what smart people did. Farida, twenty-four and in her first year out of school, took the advice and put in three thousand dollars — money she’d been saving for a car.
  ¶ At tax time, the deduction barely moved her tax bill. Her income that year was low enough that she was sitting on the bottom shelf already. The deduction had shielded income that was barely being taxed to begin with.
  ¶ Her coworker wasn’t wrong, exactly. He was just describing his own situation — a decade further into his career, sitting on a much higher shelf, where the same deduction would have saved him far more. Farida’s tax preparer explained the difference in about two minutes: the RRSP deduction is worth more in your highest-earning years, not necessarily your earliest ones.
  ¶ Farida kept the RRSP contribution room for later. She bought a slightly older car instead. Both were fine choices — she just wanted to make them with the actual math in hand, not someone else’s math borrowed for a very different income.

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: YOU CAN CONTRIBUTE NOW AND CLAIM LATER

[spotlight] Farida’s tax preparer left out one option worth knowing on its own. You don’t have to claim an RRSP deduction the same year you make the contribution. The contribution and the claim are two separate steps, and the CRA lets you carry an unclaimed contribution forward indefinitely, with no deadline. If Farida had already made the contribution before learning about the shelf effect, she wouldn’t have needed to undo anything — she could simply leave that year’s contribution unclaimed on her return and use it in a future year when her income, and her shelf, are higher. This is especially useful around a raise, a new job, or the end of a parental leave: contribute when you have the cash on hand, and time the actual deduction for the year it saves you the most.

[takeaway] Deductions shrink your income before tax is calculated; credits shrink your tax bill after. A deduction is worth more to higher earners; a credit’s value often depends on whether it’s refundable. Know which is which for every claim you make, and remember that piling on claims only helps if you actually have income or tax owing left to offset.

## Glossary

[glossary]
  - Deduction — An amount subtracted from your income before tax is calculated.
  - Credit — An amount subtracted from your tax bill, after tax is calculated.
  - Refundable credit — Paid to you regardless of how much tax you owed.
  - Non-refundable credit — Can only reduce tax owing to zero; cannot create a refund beyond that.
  - Taxable income — Your income after deductions are subtracted. What your tax bracket actually applies to.
