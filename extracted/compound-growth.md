## Growth on top of growth

[paragraph] Simple growth is straightforward: you earn a return on what you put in, every year, on the same original amount.

[paragraph] Compound growth is different. You earn a return on your original amount, plus a return on all the growth that’s accumulated so far. Every year, the base you’re earning on gets a little bigger — not because you added more money, but because last year’s growth is now part of this year’s base.

[paragraph] That’s the whole mechanism. It sounds small. Over enough years, it isn’t.

[myths]
  - “Compound growth mostly just means my money grows steadily over time.” — Steady growth and compound growth are different shapes entirely. Steady growth is a straight line. Compound growth curves upward, slowly at first, then dramatically — because the base it’s calculated on keeps getting bigger. Most of the real growth in a long-term investment happens in the later years, not the early ones.

## Let’s actually watch it happen

[paragraph] Picture $10,000 invested, earning 7% a year, left completely untouched.

[cards]
  - Start — Year: $10,000 — Balance: —
  - Year 10 — Year: $19,672 — Balance: $1,288
  - Year 20 — Year: $38,697 — Balance: $2,532
  - Year 30 — Year: $76,123 — Balance: $4,981

[paragraph] Look at that last column. The growth in a single year, by year thirty, is nearly four times what it was in year ten — on the exact same original $10,000, with nothing added the whole time. That’s not a bigger return rate. It’s the same 7%, working on a base that had quietly grown enormous in the background.

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: THE RULE OF 72

[spotlight] There’s a shortcut worth carrying in your head for the rest of this: divide 72 by your expected annual return, and the answer is roughly how many years it takes your money to double. At 7%, that’s 72 divided by 7, or just over ten years — which is almost exactly what the table above shows. $10,000 grew to $19,672 by year ten, a hair under double, right on schedule. Run the same shortcut on a fee drag. A 2% fee knocking a 7% return down to 5% doesn’t just cost you 2% a year. It stretches the doubling time from about ten years to about fourteen and a half, using the same 72-divided-by-the-rate math. No calculator, no spreadsheet, just one number that turns Module 2.12’s fee comparison into something you can estimate on the back of a napkin.

## Why the first ten years matter more than they look like they do

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: WHY THE FIRST TEN YEARS MATTER MORE THAN THEY LOOK LIKE THEY DO

[spotlight] Here’s the part that catches people off guard: in the example above, the account barely looks impressive for a long stretch. It takes ten full years to grow by less than $10,000. To someone watching closely, that can feel disappointingly slow. But those quiet early years aren’t wasted time. They’re the years building the base that later growth compounds on top of. Skip them — wait ten years to start instead of starting now — and you don’t just lose ten years of contributions. You lose ten years of compounding on top of those contributions, which is a much larger loss than it first appears. This is why the single most repeated piece of advice across this entire course, in module after module, has been to open the account now, even with a small first deposit. Modules 2.5, 2.7, 2.8, and 2.9 all said some version of this. Now you know exactly why: the quiet early years are doing more work than they look like they’re doing.

## Two levers you actually control

[paragraph] You can’t control the market’s return. But two things inside your control matter enormously to how compound growth plays out.

[paragraph] Time. The longer money stays invested, the more years compounding has to work. This is why time horizon, covered in Module 2.14, is such a central question — it’s not just about risk, it’s about how much room compounding has to operate.

[paragraph] Fees. Module 2.12 showed a 1.5% fee gap compounding into six figures over 25 years. That’s not a separate lesson from this one — it’s the exact same mechanism, working in reverse. A fee doesn’t just cost you the fee amount. It costs you every year of compounding that money would have gone on to earn.

[myths]
  - “A slightly higher fee is a minor tradeoff if the fund performs a little better.” — Because fees compound in reverse, a seemingly small ongoing fee difference can outweigh years of slightly better performance. The fee is certain and permanent; the extra performance is neither. This is the same math as Module 2.12, viewed from the growth side instead of the cost side.

## The twins, ten years apart

[story] The twins, ten years apart
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ Two sisters, Elin and Karin, both eventually invested the same amount — $200 a month, for exactly twenty-five years each, at a similar average return.
  ¶ Elin started at twenty-two, right out of school, with a modest first job and no real sense of urgency. Karin started at thirty-two, after a decade of telling herself she’d get to it once things settled down — which they eventually did.
  ¶ By the time both had invested for their full twenty-five years, Elin’s account held nearly double Karin’s, despite both contributing the exact same monthly amount for the exact same number of years. The only difference was when those twenty-five years happened to fall — Elin’s compounding had a full decade’s head start on hers, quietly building a bigger base for every year that followed.
  ¶ Karin didn’t do anything wrong. She simply started a decade later, in a system where the decade you start in matters almost as much as how much you contribute once you do.

[takeaway] Compound growth means earning returns on your returns, not just on your original contributions — which is why growth accelerates dramatically in later years, after quietly building a base in the early ones. Time and fees are the two levers you actually control: more time means more years of compounding, and lower fees mean less of that compounding lost along the way. Starting now, even modestly, beats waiting for a better moment almost every time.

## Glossary

[glossary]
  - Compound growth — Growth calculated on both your original contributions and all previously accumulated growth, not on the original amount alone.
  - Base — The total amount growth is calculated on in a given year, which increases as prior growth accumulates.
  - Time horizon — How long until money is needed, and therefore how many years compounding has to work with.

[lists]
  - WHERE TO GET HELP — A financial advisor: for help projecting how compounding might apply to your own specific savings timeline.; Your investment provider’s growth calculator: most major banks and brokerages offer a simple compound growth calculator online, useful for testing your own numbers.

[paragraph] This module uses illustrative figures at a fixed 7% return to demonstrate the mechanism. Actual investment returns vary and are never guaranteed — nothing in this module should be read as a projection of real results.
