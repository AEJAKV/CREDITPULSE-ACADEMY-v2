## Passport brief

[paragraph] You can arrive with years of responsible money management and still receive the Canadian credit equivalent of “new phone, who is this?”

[paragraph] A thin Canadian file is a documentation problem, not a character flaw. Preserve records, select one legitimate reporting product, protect due dates, control reported balances and review both bureau files. Do not collect applications like souvenir stamps.

[spotlight] Equifax and TransUnion build Canadian reports from activity reported in Canada. Foreign history does not automatically become a Canadian file, although some lenders may consider it.

[lists]
  - Separate a thin Canadian file from poor credit behaviour. — Prepare identity, income, banking and international-credit evidence. — Compare newcomer and secured-card offers by fees, deposit terms and reporting. — Build a twelve-month routine around payment history, low use and selective applications. — Complete a first-year credit roadmap with report-review dates.

[myths]
  - “No Canadian score means I have bad credit.” — A limited or unscoreable file may just lack enough Canadian history. It is not the same as missed payments or excessive debt.

## Your First Passport Stamps

[paragraph] A bank account supports everyday records, but normally does not build a score by itself. A file starts when a reporting organization sends eligible account information.

[cards]
  - (01) — STAMP 1 · PRESERVE EVIDENCE — Keep an international report where available, bank reference letters, income proof, rental records, savings history and current identity documents. A lender decides what it accepts.
  - (02) — STAMP 2 · CHOOSE ONE PRODUCT — Ask whether it reports to Equifax, TransUnion or both. Compare annual fees, interest, deposit, upgrade and cancellation before applying.
  - (03) — STAMP 3 · PROTECT THE DUE DATE — Set alerts and automatic minimum protection. Pay the statement balance in full when affordable. A secured-card deposit is collateral, not payment.
  - (04) — STAMP 4 · KEEP USE MODEST — FCAC suggests trying to use less than 30% of total available credit. Lower reported use may help, especially with a small limit.
  - (05) — STAMP 5 · VERIFY REPORTING — After several update cycles, obtain both reports. Checking your own file is a soft inquiry and does not lower your score.

[spotlight] A secured card builds history only if the issuer reports and you manage it well. The deposit does not erase interest, fees or missed payments.

[spotlight] An authorized user may spend, but FCAC says those purchases do not build the user’s credit history.

[myths]
  - “Applying for several cards will build history faster.” — Multiple applications can create hard inquiries, fees and more due dates. One affordable reporting account can create useful history.

[source] FCAC: Choosing a credit card
  url: https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/credit-cards/choose-credit-card.html

## Amina’s File Was New; Her Habits Were Not

[story] Meet Amina (DEDICATED STORY)

Amina Yusuf moved to Winnipeg for a project-coordinator job with savings, steady employment and strong foreign credit. Her Canadian report was nearly blank, the financial version of a freshly painted apartment: clean, promising and containing absolutely no furniture.

An apartment application, card decline and expensive “instant Canadian credit” ads created pressure to apply repeatedly. Instead, Amina built a Credit Passport with her international report, employer letter, pay stubs, lease, bank reference and identification.

She compared a newcomer card and secured card, asked which bureaus received updates and chose one low-limit product. A small transit charge, payment alerts and automatic minimum protection created a simple routine.

Amina paid in full, kept reported use low and declined store cards. After several cycles, she checked both files. One showed the account; the other misspelled her surname. She corrected it officially and retained confirmation.

At year-end there was no cinematic score reveal. There was just a stable Canadian file, preserved international evidence and no expensive collection of beginner products.


[spotlight] Amina protected one account long enough for accurate Canadian history to develop.

[heading] What resolved the conflict?

[paragraph] A single reporting path, reliable documents and scheduled bureau checks replaced application urgency with a repeatable plan.

[transition] Amina's report was blank, not empty, and one misspelled surname almost cost her the difference.
  detail: A checked report caught the error a rushed application never would have, which is the whole case for verifying instead of guessing.

## The First-Year Roadmap

[paragraph] Advance by evidence, not score chasing.

[cards]
  - DAYS 1–30 · SET THE BASE — Open essential banking, learn fees, create a payment calendar and preserve records from abroad. Do not buy optional products merely because ‘newcomer’ is on the brochure.
  - MONTHS 1–3 · OPEN CAREFULLY — Choose one reporting product after comparing cost, limit, deposit, bureau reporting and cancellation or upgrade rules.
  - MONTHS 3–6 · CREATE A CLEAN PATTERN — Make every required payment on time, keep use modest and avoid borrowing just to make the account look active.
  - MONTHS 6–12 · AUDIT AND PREPARE — Review both reports, correct errors and retain proof. For a major loan, bring income, savings and international-credit evidence.

[spotlight] CMHC newcomer guidance allows alternative evidence of creditworthiness in qualifying cases. This can include an international report or financial-institution reference. Full underwriting still applies.

[spotlight] A successful roadmap controls payments, balances and records. No score or approval date is guaranteed.

[myths]
  - “A foreign credit score converts directly into the same Canadian score.” — Canadian bureaus use Canadian activity. Some lenders may review foreign reports or references, but there is no universal conversion shortcut.

## Quick Check: Choose the Best Next Move

[accordions]
  - Difference between a thin Canadian file and poor credit? — A thin file lacks enough Canadian reported history; a poor file contains negative behaviour such as missed payments or excessive debt.
  - What should you verify before choosing a newcomer or secured card? — Fees, interest, deposit rules, limit, cancellation/upgrade terms and whether it reports to Equifax, TransUnion or both.
  - Does a secured-card deposit replace monthly payment? — No. It protects the issuer; the card bill remains payable.
  - Why is one manageable product often stronger than several applications? — It can create useful history with fewer inquiries, fees and due dates.
  - Which records may help a lender assess limited Canadian history? — International reports, bank references, income, savings, rental records and current identity documents, subject to lender policy.

## Glossary of Terms

[glossary]
  - Thin file — A credit file with too little reported information for a robust lending assessment or score.
  - Reporting product — A credit account whose issuer sends account information to one or both Canadian credit bureaus.
  - Secured credit card — A card backed by a security deposit; the borrower still owes the monthly balance.
  - Credit utilization — The reported revolving balance compared with the available credit limit.
  - Hard inquiry — A lender’s credit check connected with an application that may affect the score.
  - Soft inquiry — A credit check, such as reviewing your own report, that does not affect the score.
