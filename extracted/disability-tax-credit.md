## Start here

[paragraph] A diagnosis may be one line long. A Tuesday involving medication, supervision, recovery time and twelve careful steps is rarely so considerate.

[paragraph] The Disability Tax Credit, or DTC, is a non-refundable tax credit. It can lower the income tax of an approved person or, in some cases, a family member who supports them. It recognizes severe and long-lasting impairments. It is not a monthly disability payment, and approval is not based on a diagnosis alone.

[spotlight] DTC categories include walking, speaking, hearing, vision, feeding, dressing, eliminating (using the washroom) and the mental functions needed for everyday life. Life-sustaining therapy and cumulative effects have their own routes.

[chips]
  - Tell a diagnosis apart from how a condition affects daily life.
  - Organize your evidence around frequency, time, support and duration.
  - Explain the jobs of the applicant, the practitioner and the CRA.
  - Choose the current application route.
  - Build a short record of daily impact to bring to your practitioner.

[myths]
  - “Only visible or physical disabilities can qualify.” — DTC categories cover physical, sensory and mental functions. The test is the certified effect on daily function under the law, not whether a stranger can spot the impairment.

[transition] Learn what the CRA looks for, and your application can describe your real days instead of just naming a diagnosis.
  detail: Specific, truthful detail about how often, how long and with what help gives your practitioner something concrete to certify.

## Show the Pattern, Not Just the Label

[paragraph] A marked restriction generally means you can't do an activity, or it takes about three times as long as it would for a person of similar age without the impairment, even with the right therapy, devices and medication. It must be present all or substantially all of the time, generally at least 90%, and last or be expected to last at least 12 months in a row.

[cards]
  - FUNCTION — Describe the everyday activity that's affected and what happens in ordinary life.
  - FREQUENCY — Note how often the problem happens, including the pattern across better and harder days.
  - TIME — Explain how much longer the activity takes compared with a person of similar age, where that test applies.
  - SUPPORT — List the therapy, medication, devices, reminders, supervision or hands-on help you already use.
  - DURATION — Say when the pattern began and whether it has lasted, or is expected to last, at least 12 months in a row.

[spotlight] Two or more significant limitations can sometimes combine into the equivalent of a marked restriction. Each limitation must meet the CRA’s cumulative-effect conditions, and minor inconveniences do not become eligibility by committee.

[spotlight] For qualifying life-sustaining therapy, the test looks at the purpose, frequency and time rules. People with type 1 diabetes are considered to meet the therapy criterion for 2021 and later years.

[myths]
  - “A medical diagnosis guarantees DTC approval.” — A practitioner certifies the impairment and its effects, but the CRA decides eligibility using the legal criteria. Specific, truthful detail about daily function helps more than a diagnosis name travelling alone.

## Give Each Person the Correct Job

[cards]
  - APPLICANT — Fills in Part A, names who may claim the amount and gives consent. You can start online and give your practitioner a reference number.
  - MEDICAL PRACTITIONER — Fills in Part B for the right category using professional judgment. A fee may be charged, so ask ahead of time.
  - CRA — Reviews the certification and decides whether the rules and years are met. The CRA may ask for more information.
  - AFTER THE DECISION — Read the notice carefully. Check the approved years and tax instructions, or use the explanation and official review options.

[spotlight] The CRA’s online DTC form is the preferred route. For paper, use the latest T2201. From 8 September 2026, versions issued before 2023 are no longer accepted.

[spotlight] From 14 July 2026, don't use the CRA’s general Submit Documents service for a new DTC application unless the CRA specifically asks you for more information.

[myths]
  - “A non-refundable credit is useless if the approved person owes no tax.” — A family member who supports you may sometimes claim unused amounts, and approval can matter for other programs. Each claim and program has its own conditions.

[heading] Key takeaway

[paragraph] DTC eligibility depends on severe and long-lasting effects on daily function, not diagnosis alone. Build specific, truthful evidence, let your practitioner certify within their role and let the CRA decide.

## Leah and the Form That Did Not Know Tuesday

[story] Meet Leah
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ Leah Cardinal lived in Winnipeg and managed a long-term condition that made everyday tasks hard to predict. Her goal was clear. She wanted the Disability Tax Credit approved before tax season, because therapy and medication costs took a big bite out of every month.
  ¶ She had a well-documented diagnosis, so she expected the form to be easy. Her first draft named the condition three times and said almost nothing about her days. A friend who had been turned down warned her. The diagnosis knew who it was. The form did not know Tuesday.
  ¶ Leah pushed back. She had the letters, the test results and a thick folder. Then she reread her draft. She had described her hardest day as if it happened every day, and she had left out the supports that made other days possible: a timer on her phone, a rail on the stairs and an hour of rest after lunch.
  ¶ Her appointment with her nurse practitioner was two days away. She could hand over the folder and ask for it to be made to work. Or she could spend two weeks writing down what her days looked like and bring honest questions instead. The deadline pushed her toward the first choice. She chose the second.
  ¶ Each night she wrote down which tasks were hard, how long they took and what help she used. She marked better days and harder days. She did not exaggerate, and she did not leave out the good days.
  ¶ At the appointment, the log changed the conversation. It showed that her hard days were not rare. They were most days, and some tasks took far longer than she had noticed. Her practitioner asked about a second activity she had never mentioned. Leah brought questions, not a prewritten conclusion, and the practitioner certified using clinical judgment.
  ¶ She sent the online application and kept every copy, including the reference number. The CRA made the decision. The first line of her log, a plain note about a Tuesday in March, stayed pinned above her desk. The application now told one consistent, truthful story.

[spotlight] A short diary can show a pattern that memory squeezes flat. Record typical days, supports and time demands. Do not exaggerate, play things down or coach a practitioner toward an answer you've already picked.

[paragraph] Studio note: humour belongs with the paperwork, never with a person’s disability, pain or need for support.

  detail: Two weeks of simple notes turned a vague form into a clear picture her practitioner could certify with confidence.

## Quick Check: Choose the Best Next Move

[accordions]
  - Why isn't a diagnosis alone enough for the DTC? — The CRA decides using severe and prolonged effects on daily function under the legal criteria, not the name of the diagnosis alone.
  - What five evidence lenses belong in a daily-impact record? — Function, frequency, time, support and duration.
  - What does ‘all or substantially all of the time’ generally mean? — Generally at least 90% of the time.
  - Who certifies the effects, and who decides eligibility? — A medical practitioner certifies the impairment and its effects. The CRA decides eligibility.
  - Which application version and submission cautions apply in 2026? — Use the preferred online form or the latest T2201. From 8 September 2026, versions from before 2023 are not accepted. From 14 July, don't use the general Submit Documents service for a new application unless the CRA asks.

## Glossary of Terms

[glossary]
  - DTC — Disability Tax Credit. A non-refundable credit that can lower income tax.
  - Marked restriction — You can't do an activity, or it takes about three times as long as it would for others your age.
  - Prolonged — Lasting, or expected to last, at least 12 months in a row.
  - Cumulative effect — Several significant limits that together meet the test, even if none meets it alone.
  - Life-sustaining therapy — Therapy you need to keep a vital function going, under specific rules.
  - T2201 — The Disability Tax Credit Certificate form.
  - Supporting family member — A person who may claim your unused amount when the transfer rules are met.
