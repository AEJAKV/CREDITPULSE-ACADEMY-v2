## Start here

[paragraph] An emergency fund is money set aside for big, sudden, unplanned needs.

[paragraph] It is not a general savings account, a guilt account or a tiny museum where money must sit untouched forever.

[paragraph] This lesson works like emergency triage. You will decide which expenses belong and build your target in manageable layers. Then you will pick a safe, easy-to-reach home for the money and create a rebuild plan for the day the fund does exactly what it was hired to do.

[source] FCAC: Setting up an emergency fund
  url: https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/savings-investments/setting-up-emergency-funds.html

[spotlight] FCAC separates emergencies from occasional expenses. A car repair, urgent vet visit, job loss or health issue may qualify. School supplies, winter tires and holidays are predictable costs that belong in the budget.

[heading] Welcome to Emergency Triage

[paragraph] Ask these four questions before reaching for the fund. A strong emergency usually earns four convincing answers.

[cards]
  - SUDDEN — Could the expense reasonably have been known or scheduled earlier?
  - NECESSARY — Does it protect health, housing, income or a basic household function?
  - UNPLANNED — Was it outside the regular budget and any existing sinking fund?
  - TIME-SENSITIVE — Would delay cause serious harm, a larger cost or loss of essential access?

[myths]
  - “Anything that feels surprising is an emergency.” — Feelings are useful alarms, but they are not the whole way to sort an expense. A known annual fee can feel rude when it arrives; it is still predictable, and a sinking fund should be ready for it.

[transition] A clear job description for your savings stops every surprise bill from raiding the same account.
  detail: Four quick questions tell you whether an expense earns emergency-fund money or belongs in its own sinking fund.
  extra: The triage test ahead sorts real emergencies from costs that just feel urgent.

## The Triage Board: Emergency or Sinking Fund?

[paragraph] A sinking fund is money saved gradually for a cost you know is coming.

[paragraph] It prevents predictable expenses from competing with true emergencies.

[heading] Emergency

[paragraph] Use when the cost is: Sudden, necessary, unplanned and time-sensitive.

[cards]
  - (EMERGENCY) — Transmission failure — Sudden and necessary — Ask before deciding: Is the vehicle required for work or essential travel?
  - (EMERGENCY) — Urgent dental treatment — Necessary and cannot wait — Ask before deciding: What portion is not covered by insurance?
  - (EMERGENCY) — Temporary loss of income — Threatens essentials — Ask before deciding: Which essential costs must the fund carry first?

[heading] Sinking fund

[paragraph] Plan ahead when the cost is: Known, seasonal, recurring or reasonably predictable.

[cards]
  - (SINKING FUND) — December gifts — Expected yearly — Ask before deciding: How much can be set aside monthly?
  - (SINKING FUND) — Winter tires — Seasonal and predictable — Ask before deciding: What is the replacement date and target?
  - (SINKING FUND) — Annual professional fee — Known renewal — Ask before deciding: How many months remain before it is due?

[heading] TRIAGE RULE

[paragraph] Predictable costs still need a plan, and true emergencies still deserve care.

[paragraph] Classification tells the household which savings tool should do the work.

[myths]
  - “If a bill arrives only once a year, it is automatically unexpected.” — An annual licence, insurance premium or holiday only comes around once a year, but it can still be scheduled and split into monthly sinking-fund transfers.

[spotlight] The emergency fund protects the household from shocks. Sinking funds protect the emergency fund from the calendar.

[story] Meet Maya
  detail: MAYA’S THREE-ALARM MONTH
  ¶ Maya lives in Fredericton and drives to early hospital shifts, so her car has exactly one job: start every morning. In January it fails at that job. A $720 alternator repair and tow bill lands the same week she remembers a $480 winter-tire replacement and a $240 annual professional fee. All three show up acting like emergencies. Maya has $900 in her emergency fund and one nervous eyebrow.
  ¶ Maya runs each cost through the triage test. The alternator failure was sudden, her car is necessary for her shifts, the repair was not in the budget, and waiting would threaten her income. It passes on all four counts. The tires and the professional fee are real costs too, but she has known both dates for months. They belong in separate sinking funds, not the emergency fund.
  ¶ Using $720 leaves Maya with $180, and that number looks thin next to a full tank of gas and a grocery list. For a second she considers the credit card sitting in her wallet. Paying with plastic would keep her savings total looking untouched. Maya catches herself mid-thought: a fund that looks full but cannot be used is not protecting anyone. She pays the mechanic straight out of savings instead.
  ¶ She records the withdrawal, then restarts her transfer at $45 every two weeks. Sixteen deposits will rebuild the $720. She also opens two new sinking funds, $40 a month for tires and $20 a month for the professional fee, so next January does not try out for the emergency role again.
  ¶ By the next payday, Maya has paid a real emergency without touching a credit card, and two costs that used to ambush her now have their own accounts. Her fund is smaller, but it did exactly what it was hired to do. Her eyebrow, for the record, has relaxed.

## Build the Fund in Three Layers

[cards]
  - (LAYER 1) — STARTER SHIELD — Choose a first target you can reach and repeat. $500 may be a useful example, but your amount should reflect your risks and capacity.
  - (LAYER 2) — ONE MONTH OF ESSENTIALS — Add only the costs needed to keep essentials running. Include housing, food, transportation, insurance, medication and minimum payments.
  - (LAYER 3) — THREE TO SIX MONTHS OF RESILIENCE — Extend the target based on income stability, household size, health, insurance, dependants and other real risks.

[spotlight] FCAC describes three to six months of regular expenses or income as an ideal target, while also telling people to start with a realistic amount and save gradually. The ladder gives both ideas a place.

[myths]
  - “If I cannot save three to six months now, there is no point starting.” — A smaller first layer can absorb part or all of a shock and cut the amount you need to borrow. Progress is useful before you reach the final target.

[heading] Calculate the Essential-Expense Ladder

[paragraph] This fictional household uses essential monthly costs, not every normal purchase, to build its planning range.

[paragraph] Essential monthly baseline: $3,500. The amount required to keep this household’s core needs running for one month.

[paragraph] Protected plan: 4 categories.

[cards]
  - Housing and utilities — $1,800 / month — Essential — 51% of essential month — Planning question: What amount must continue during an income interruption?
  - Food and household basics — $650 / month — Essential — 19% of essential month — Planning question: What is the realistic reduced amount?
  - Transport, insurance and medication — $700 / month — Essential — 20% of essential month — Planning question: Which items protect work, health or access?
  - Required minimum payments — $350 / month — Essential obligation — 10% of essential month — Planning question: What must remain current?

[heading] TARGET RANGE

[paragraph] $3,500 for one month; $10,500 for three months; $21,000 for six months.

[paragraph] These are planning points, not a pass-or-fail test.

[heading] Turn the Target into Paydays

[heading] WORKING FORMULA

[paragraph] (Target − current emergency savings) ÷ transfer per payday = number of deposits needed.

[paragraph] $45 every two weeks creates $1,170 over 26 paydays. Automation can move the money before it is invited to become something else.

## Give the Fund a Safe, Usable Home

[paragraph] The best home is usually boring in the most helpful way. It should be easy to reach when you need it, separate from everyday spending, low or no fee, free to withdraw from without a penalty, stable in value, and earning interest where available.

[carousel]
  - (ACCOUNT CHECK 01 · ACCESS WINDOW) — Check access. — Know how quickly money can move, whether holds apply and whether weekend or holiday access differs.
  - (ACCOUNT CHECK 02 · FEE SCAN) — Check the full fee schedule. — Review transfer, withdrawal, minimum-balance and transaction fees before choosing the account.
  - (ACCOUNT CHECK 03 · DEPOSIT STATUS) — Check protection. — Confirm the institution, product and ownership category with the official insurer. Also check current deposit-insurance rules.
  - (ACCOUNT CHECK 04 · VALUE STABILITY) — Avoid sharp market risk. — Money needed on short notice should not depend on selling an asset after its value has fallen.

[source] FCAC: Savings accounts
  url: https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/banking/bank-accounts/savings-account.html

[myths]
  - “The account with the highest possible return is always the best emergency-fund home.” — Return matters, but access, fees, stability and protection come first. A high quoted rate is not helpful if withdrawals are restricted, fees erase the benefit or the product can fall sharply when cash is needed.

[spotlight] CDIC automatically protects eligible deposits at member institutions. Coverage goes up to $100,000 in each insurance category, including principal and interest. Mutual funds, stocks, bonds, ETFs and cryptocurrencies are not CDIC-covered deposits.

## Province Lens: Verify the Institution, Not Just the Logo

[cards]
  - AB and BC — Provincial credit-union systems currently guarantee 100% of eligible deposits. A federal credit union operating in BC uses CDIC instead.
  - NB and PEI — NBCUDIC and PEI CUDIC protect eligible member deposits under provincial rules. Verify current limits and account eligibility.
  - ON — FSRA currently covers eligible non-registered credit-union deposits up to $250,000. It covers eligible registered deposits without a dollar limit.
  - NL — CUDGCNL is the provincial credit-union deposit guarantor. Confirm current coverage directly before choosing a product.

## Quick Check: Where Does It Go?

  - (Sinking fund or budget.) — School supplies needed every September.

[heading] Before You Open the Triage File

[myths]
  - “Using the emergency fund means I failed to protect it.” — Using the fund for a true emergency is success, not failure. Record what happened, protect your essentials right now, and begin a manageable rebuild. Guilt is not an interest-bearing account.

[spotlight] A rebuild plan should exist before the withdrawal. Record the amount, restart an affordable automatic transfer, send any windfalls or freed-up debt payments to the gap, and create a sinking fund if the event turns out to be predictable.

## Glossary of Terms

[glossary]
  - Emergency fund — Money set aside for big, sudden needs that fall outside your regular budget.
  - Sinking fund — Money saved a little at a time for an expense you know is coming.
  - Essential expense — A cost that protects housing, food, health, income or basic day-to-day life.
  - Starter shield — A first savings goal you can reach quickly, set before the bigger long-term target.
  - Resilience target — A bigger savings goal, often based on several months of essential or regular costs.
  - Liquidity — How fast and easily you can get at your money without losing value or paying a penalty.
  - Principal — The original amount of money deposited, before interest.
  - Interest — Money paid on savings or charged on borrowed money, usually shown as a rate.
  - Deposit insurance — Protection for eligible deposits if a member bank or credit union fails, under the insurer’s rules.
  - Eligible deposit — A deposit product that meets the deposit insurer’s coverage rules.
  - CDIC — The federal Crown corporation that insures eligible deposits at its member institutions.
  - Automatic transfer — A scheduled move of money between accounts on a chosen date or cycle.

## Check-in

1. EMERGENCY EXAMPLES — List three emergencies when you would use this fund.
2. NOT EMERGENCIES — List three planned or optional expenses when you would not use this fund.
3. MONTHLY ESSENTIALS — What is your total monthly cost for housing, food, utilities, transportation, insurance, medication and minimum debt payments?
4. SAVINGS GOAL — What is your first emergency-fund goal? How much would you like to save later?
5. WHERE YOU WILL KEEP IT — Name the bank, credit union and account. How quickly can you get the money, and are there any fees?
6. DEPOSIT PROTECTION — Who protects the money in this account? Confirm that the institution and account qualify.
7. AUTOMATIC SAVINGS — How much will you save each payday, and when will you check your progress?
8. REBUILDING THE FUND — If you use the money, how much will you start saving again, and when will you check your progress?
