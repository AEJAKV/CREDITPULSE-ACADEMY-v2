## Start here

[paragraph] The man at the door has a clipboard and a lanyard. He is friendly, he knows your street, and he says a government rebate program by name. None of that is proof of anything.

[paragraph] Natural Resources Canada does not send anyone to your door. It says so on its own website.

[heading] What this course does

[paragraph] Energy rebates changed twice in the last two years, and most of what you find online is out of date. This module sorts what is actually open right now, the order that keeps a claim valid, and how to keep a salesperson from ending up on your property title.

## What is open, what is closed

[paragraph] Most of what you find searching this topic is confidently out of date. Here is where things actually stand.

[cards]
  - CLOSED: The old federal reimbursements — The Canada Greener Homes Grant, which paid homeowners back after an EnerGuide evaluation, stopped taking new applicants at the end of 2025. The Canada Greener Homes Loan stopped in October 2025. Both still show up everywhere online as though you can apply tomorrow. You cannot.
  - OPEN: What replaced them — The Canada Greener Homes Affordability Program installs retrofits at no cost to the household, for low-to-median-income homeowners and, for the first time, tenants too. It only exists where your province has signed on. The Oil to Heat Pump Affordability program remains open and remains the single largest incentive for a household heating with oil. Every province also runs its own programs, which for many households are now the main event.

[myths]
  - “The government sent someone to my door about the rebate.” — It did not. No federal energy program uses unsolicited door-to-door or phone contact, and NRCan says so plainly. If someone arrives uninvited, take the company name, close the door, and look the program up yourself. Nothing legitimate is lost by waiting a day.

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: THE ACRONYMS CHANGED, THE MONEY DID NOT DISAPPEAR

[spotlight] It is tempting to read “the grant closed” and assume the money is gone. It moved. The federal dollars shifted from a broad reimbursement anyone could claim toward a targeted, no-cost install for lower-income households, delivered by the provinces. At the same time, provincial programs got substantially bigger. Start with your provincial program page and your utility, not with a federal search, and ignore any article about this topic that is more than six months old.

## The order is the program

[paragraph] Almost every rejected rebate fails for the same reason: the homeowner did the work first and applied afterward. If your program needs an assessment, it needs it before anything is touched. There is no appeal for a furnace that is already installed.

[ordered]
  - Find out which path your program uses — Most provinces now offer two. A single-upgrade path, where you install one thing with no assessment required. And a bundled path, combining insulation, air sealing and windows for a larger total, but only after a pre-retrofit assessment.
  - Book the pre-retrofit evaluation, if your path needs one — You book it. A registered energy advisor does it. The report shows where your home leaks and which upgrades qualify.
  - Confirm what stacks before you choose equipment — Federal, provincial and utility incentives can often combine on the same upgrade, but each has its own equipment list.
  - Use a contractor from the program’s list — Most programs require registered or certified installers. A cheaper quote from someone off the list is not cheaper.
  - Do the work, keep every invoice and model number — Programs verify specific equipment, not general descriptions.
  - Complete the post-retrofit evaluation and submit on time — This second evaluation is what proves the improvement, and its deadline is a real one.

[heading] Quick math: is the bundle worth it?

[paragraph] Add up the cost of the two evaluations, minus whatever the program rebates back for them. Compare the total bundled rebate against the total single-upgrade rebate for the same work. Then price the weeks of delay: another winter on a dying furnace is a real cost, not a rounding error. If the bundled premium clearly beats the evaluation cost, take the bundle. If it is close and your furnace is limping, take the single upgrade and stop optimizing.

[myths]
  - “I will start the work now and apply once it is finished.” — This is the most expensive sentence in the module. On any bundled or assessment-based path, work started before the pre-retrofit evaluation disqualifies the whole claim. If a contractor tells you it will be fine, ask them to put that in writing next to their name.

## Where to look, by province

[paragraph] These programs change names and terms too often for a printed list to stay honest for long. Start with the operator, not the program name.

[lists]
  - Ontario — Home Renovation Savings Program, delivered by Save on Energy and Enbridge Gas.; Single-upgrade and bundled paths. Condos, apartments and new builds are excluded.
  - British Columbia — CleanBC Better Homes, with an income-qualified tier that pays substantially more.; BC Hydro rebates stack on top.
  - Quebec — Rénoclimat for the building envelope, Hydro-Québec’s LogisVert for equipment.; Chauffez vert for getting off oil.
  - Manitoba — Efficiency Manitoba, and the first province delivering the federal affordability program.
  - Nova Scotia and New Brunswick — Efficiency Nova Scotia and NB Power’s Total Home Energy Savings Program.; Nova Scotia’s heat pump support is among the most generous in the country.
  - Alberta and Saskatchewan — Thinner coverage. Check SaskPower’s retrofit rebate and your municipality.
  - Municipal — Several cities run their own low-interest retrofit loan programs, repaid through the property tax bill.

[myths]
  - “There is a thirty percent federal tax credit for home solar.” — There is a thirty percent Clean Technology Investment Tax Credit, and it is available to taxable Canadian corporations, not to individuals installing panels on their own roof. This one circulates constantly in homeowner forums. Read who the claimant has to be before you build a budget on it.

## The gap between invoice and rebate

[paragraph] A rebate is not a discount. On most programs you pay the contractor in full, then wait, often several months, for a cheque. The exception is the federal affordability program, where the delivery organization pays the contractor directly and the household pays nothing.

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: REBATES ARRIVE MONTHS AFTER THE INVOICE DOES

[spotlight] That gap is where energy retrofits quietly turn into credit problems. Putting a heat pump on a high-rate credit card while waiting for a rebate can erase most of the rebate, and every month of delay costs more. A rebate you have not received yet is not money you have. Three sensible ways through it: check whether your province still offers low-interest or on-bill financing, ask the program whether it pays the contractor directly, or phase the work so each piece is paid from cash rather than credit. Then run the real math: rebate amount minus interest paid while waiting for it. If the second number is a meaningful share of the first, the timing is wrong, not the project.

## Protecting your home’s title

[paragraph] For years, door-to-door equipment sales in Ontario followed a pattern: an uninvited salesperson would arrange a rental or financing agreement for a water heater or furnace, then register a notice of security interest against the title of the home. Many owners only found out when they went to sell.

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: WHAT CAN END UP ATTACHED TO YOUR HOUSE

[spotlight] Ontario’s Homeowner Protection Act, in force since June 2024, banned new NOSIs for consumer goods and deemed existing ones expired. They can be removed from title on application, for a government fee. Other provinces have not all followed. If you own a home in Ontario and ever signed an equipment agreement at your door, a title search is worth doing. And in any province, before signing anything for home equipment, ask directly whether anything will be registered against your title, and get the answer in writing.

[paragraph] Six checks before you sign anything for home equipment.

[chips]
  - I contacted them, not the other way around.
  - I can find this program named on a provincial, federal or utility website, not just a contractor’s brochure.
  - The contractor appears on the program’s registered installer list.
  - I have asked in writing whether anything will be registered against my property title.
  - I have read what happens if I cancel, and when the cancellation window closes.
  - Nobody has told me the offer expires today.

## Priya, the lanyard and the twenty minutes that settled it

[story] Priya and the lanyard
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ Priya’s father-in-law lives with them in Brampton and answers the door, because he is retired and considers it his job. The man on the step in March had a lanyard, a tablet, and the name of a federal program Priya had actually read about. He wanted to check the furnace, and he mentioned the funding was almost gone.
  ¶ Her father-in-law was polite and let him talk. Priya asked for a business card and said they would call back, a sentence with no downside that is far harder to say than it looks when someone helpful is standing in your doorway.
  ¶ She spent twenty minutes that evening on the Natural Resources Canada site and found the notice sitting right there: no third party is authorized to make unsolicited contact about the program, and evaluations are booked by homeowners only. She also learned the federal grant he had half-named had closed the year before, and that Ontario had not signed on to the program that replaced it.
  ¶ What they did next was less dramatic and more useful. Priya looked up Ontario’s actual rebate program, found that the single-upgrade path needed no assessment, and booked a registered contractor from the program list to replace the twenty-two-year-old furnace with a cold-climate heat pump. The rebate arrived that summer. The man with the lanyard never called back, which told them everything they needed to know about how badly he wanted the work.

[takeaway] Everything legitimate in this module starts with you picking up the phone. Check what is actually open this month rather than what a blog says, start with your province and your utility rather than Ottawa, complete the assessment before anyone touches the house, and never sign for home equipment without asking in writing what gets registered against your title.

## Glossary

[glossary]
  - EnerGuide evaluation — A home energy assessment by a registered energy advisor. It produces a rating and a list of qualifying upgrades. Booked by the homeowner, never sold at the door.
  - Registered energy advisor — The certified professional who performs the evaluation, working through a licensed service organization.
  - CGHAP — Canada Greener Homes Affordability Program. Federal no-cost retrofits for low-to-median-income households and tenants, delivered by participating provinces.
  - OHPA — Oil to Heat Pump Affordability. Federal support for households moving off oil heating, often topped up provincially.
  - Stacking — Combining federal, provincial, utility and municipal incentives on the same upgrade, where each program's rules allow it.
  - Single-upgrade vs bundled path — One measure with no assessment required, compared with several measures that need pre- and post-retrofit evaluations and pay a larger total.
  - Cold-climate heat pump — A heat pump rated to keep working at low outdoor temperatures. The rating, not the label, is what programs check.
  - NOSI — Notice of security interest. A registration against a property title tied to financed or rented equipment. Banned for consumer goods in Ontario since June 2024; rules differ elsewhere.

[lists]
  - WHERE TO GET HELP — Natural Resources Canada: the authoritative page for federal programs and current energy-scam warnings.; Your provincial program or utility: Save on Energy, CleanBC Better Homes, Rénoclimat and LogisVert, Efficiency Manitoba, Efficiency Nova Scotia, NB Power, SaskPower.; Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre: 1-888-495-8501, to report a door-to-door or telephone approach.; Provincial consumer protection office: for cancelling a contract signed under pressure. Cancellation windows are short, act the same week.; A community legal clinic or real estate lawyer: if something has already been registered against your title.

[paragraph] This module skips dollar figures on purpose. Rebate levels, income thresholds and equipment lists in this field change several times a year. Confirm every figure on the program’s own page the day you need it.
