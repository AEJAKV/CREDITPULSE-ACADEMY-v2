## Start here

[paragraph] You pull both of your credit reports, and the numbers do not match. Before you panic, understand this: that is completely normal.

[paragraph] Canada has two major credit bureaus, Equifax and TransUnion, and each one maintains its own separate file on you. They never compare notes. Think of two coworkers who both took minutes at the same meeting, then typed them up from memory a week apart.

[paragraph] Lenders also decide for themselves which bureau receives their information. Some report to both, some report to only one, and every one of them sends its updates on its own schedule.

[paragraph] So a gap between your two reports is not automatic proof that somebody made a mistake. Your job is not to pick a winner, but to answer three questions: what is different, why is it different, and does it need action from you?

[source] FCAC: Getting your credit report and score
  url: https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/credit-reports-score/order-credit-report.html

[spotlight] There is no master credit file in Canada. Equifax and TransUnion keep separate files. One report can hold facts the other one never received.

[heading] The Report and the Score Are Not Twins

[paragraph] A credit report is the record itself, while a credit score is a number calculated from that record. Because of this, two different scores are not evidence that one of the bureaus got something wrong.

[cards]
  - Credit report — The record. It lists your accounts and how you paid them.
  - Credit score — The number. It is worked out from what the record says.

[paragraph] Picture two photos of the same busy street, one taken at noon and one taken at four o’clock. The street is real in both pictures, but the cars have moved between them. Check the timestamp before you report a traffic mystery.

[source] TransUnion Canada: What is a credit report?
  url: https://www.transunion.ca/credit-report

## Why the two reports differ

[paragraph] Most gaps start making sense the moment you compare the underlying account details instead of the page layout.

[cards]
  - Different update dates — One bureau may show an older balance because the lender sent its update on a different day. (/images/credit-mastery/01-starter/credit-02/credit02-image-3.webp)
  - Different reporting habits — A lender can report your account to one bureau and skip the other entirely. That is allowed, and nobody has to tell you. (/images/credit-mastery/01-starter/credit-02/credit02-image-4.webp)
  - Different names — A store card can appear under the finance company that legally owns it. The name looks unfamiliar, but the card is still yours. (/images/credit-mastery/01-starter/credit-02/credit02-image-5.webp)
  - Different layouts — The same status can sit in a different spot, or use different words. Compare facts, not aisle numbers. (/images/credit-mastery/01-starter/credit-02/credit02-image-6.webp)
  - Different check lists — The copy you receive can list more credit checks than the shorter version a business buys. (/images/credit-mastery/01-starter/credit-02/credit02-image-7.webp)

[source] TransUnion Canada: What is a credit report?
  url: https://www.transunion.ca/credit-report

[spotlight] A reported balance is only a snapshot tied to a particular date. If you paid yesterday but the bureau file was updated last week, the report has not failed math class. It is showing you an older photo.

[heading] Quick Myth Check

[myths]
  - “If the two reports differ, one of them must be wrong.” — Lenders report at different times, and that timing alone creates genuine gaps. Check the update dates and the account details before deciding anything is wrong.
  - “The report with the better number is the accurate one.” — A nicer-looking report is not a more accurate one. What makes a report correct is the underlying detail: who owns the account, the dates, the status, the balance and the credit limit.
  - “Checking both reports will lower my score.” — Checking your own report or score has no effect whatsoever on your credit score, and that is FCAC’s word on it rather than ours.

[source] FCAC: Credit report and score basics
  url: https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/credit-reports-score/credit-report-score-basics.html


## The $920 mystery

[story] Jamie and the $920 That Went Missing
  detail: ONE CARD · TWO BALANCES · ONE ANSWER
  - Jamie is 29 and wants a used car. Nothing fancy. Something that starts in February.
  - He does the responsible thing and pulls both credit reports before breakfast, which is a bold thing to do to a perfectly innocent morning.
  - Equifax says his card balance is $920. TransUnion says $1,840. Same card, same Jamie, and $920 sitting in the gap between them.
  - His first thought is that somebody at a bureau dropped a calculator. He opens a dispute form and starts typing in capital letters.
  - Then he stops. He closes the laptop, opens it again, and checks the dates instead.
  - There it is. TransUnion took its photo on June 18. Jamie paid $920 on June 22. Equifax took its photo on July 3. One report caught the card before the payment. The other caught it after. Both are telling the truth.
  - Jamie relaxes. Two lines further down, he stops.
  - There is a credit card on one report that he has never opened.
  - That is a different animal. A date can explain a balance. A date cannot explain a card that is not yours.
  - Jamie makes two piles. The balance gap goes in “explained.” The card he has never seen goes in “check this today.”
  - He got the car loan. The car starts in February.

## The five-step check

[paragraph] Compare one account at a time. That keeps the job small enough to finish, and it makes a harmless timing gap much easier to tell apart from a fact that needs your attention.

[ordered]
  - Make sure the account is yours. — Match the lender name, the account type and enough detail to recognize it on sight, remembering that a parent company name can look unfamiliar even when the account is yours.
  - Check the update dates. — Do this before you compare balances. Different update dates create ordinary gaps, and this one step explains most of them.
  - Compare the facts that count. — Look at the balance, the credit limit or original loan amount, the payment status, and whether the account is currently open, closed or paid in full.
  - Tell layout apart from fact. — Different wording or a different position on the page matters far less than a wrong owner, an incorrect payment history, or a balance that does not match reality.
  - Give it a label. — Choose one of four labels: Match, Explained, Needs a Check, or Dispute Now. The label is what gets you to do something about it next week, instead of worrying about it next week.

[spotlight] Looking at your own credit report counts as a soft check, and FCAC confirms that it does not affect your credit score, so you can check yours as often as you like. The ‘view report’ button is not a trap door.

[source] FCAC: Getting your credit report and credit score
  url: https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/credit-reports-score/order-credit-report.html


## What can wait, and what cannot

[paragraph] Some gaps are ordinary, and you do not need to lose sleep over them. You should still check anything you cannot explain, but the two lists below tell you where to start.

[heading] THIS CAN WAIT · Usually just normal

[chips]
  - A different update date
  - An old address on one report only
  - A card listed under its finance company
  - A closed account in a different spot
  - A lender that shows up on one file only

[heading] DEAL WITH THIS NOW · Do not let these sit

[chips]
  - An account that is not yours
  - A late payment you did not make
  - A credit check you never applied for
  - A debt you paid, still shown unpaid
  - A wrong collection or bankruptcy record
  - A balance or limit that is way off

[heading] When to Move Fast

[paragraph] Move quickly whenever a difference could change who owns an account, or how your payment history appears to a lender. Those are the items in the second list above, and none of them improve with time.

[paragraph] If you believe something is wrong, keep the proof that supports you: statements, receipts, payment confirmations. Then follow that bureau’s dispute process. FCAC says you have the right to dispute information you believe is wrong, and that credit bureaus have to correct real errors at no cost to you.

[source] FCAC: Checking for errors and fraud
  url: https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/credit-reports-score/check-errors.html

[source] Equifax Canada: Dispute information
  url: https://www.equifax.ca/personal/dispute-credit-report/

[heading] One Odd Thing About TransUnion Lists

[paragraph] TransUnion calls the version you see a Consumer Disclosure, and it lists every check ever made on your file. That includes checks by your own lenders, checks that have nothing to do with credit applications, and the times you looked at your own information.

[paragraph] The shorter version that businesses buy shows considerably less than that, so a long list on your own copy is not proof that you applied for a pile of credit. It means your copy shows more of the picture.

[source] TransUnion Canada: Consumer Disclosure
  url: https://www.transunion.ca/product/consumer-disclosure

[spotlight] Your own TransUnion copy can list far more checks than any lender ever sees, so a long inquiry list is not the same thing as a long list of credit applications.

[heading] THE FIVE-QUESTION MISMATCH TEST

[ordered]
  - Dates — Are the two update dates the same?
  - Reporting — Does this lender report to both bureaus?
  - Layout — Is this a different account, or the same one under a different name?
  - Impact — Does the difference change who owns the account, how you paid it, the balance, the limit or its status?
  - Priority — Is it a match, explained, worth a check, or a dispute?


## Quick check

[paragraph] Before you move on, decide what you would do in each of these five cases, then tap to compare your answer.

[accordions]
  - Same account, two balances, two different update dates | Check the update dates and your recent statements before calling it an error, because this is almost always a timing difference.
  - Same date, but one report says you paid 30 days late and your receipt says you paid on time | Verify the details, then prepare a dispute, because your dated receipt beats their file.
  - A store card shows up under a finance company name you recognize | That is a naming difference rather than a new debt, so you can safely mark it explained.
  - An account you have never seen in your life | Look into this one today, because who owns an account matters far more than where it sits on the page.
  - You pull both of your own reports | Nothing at all happens to your score, and FCAC confirms it in writing.

[heading] Your Turn

[paragraph] Pick one account you know well and compare it across the newest Equifax and TransUnion reports you can get hold of. One account is enough for tonight. Nobody has ever finished their whole credit history in a single sitting, and nobody has ever enjoyed trying.

[takeaway] A tidier-looking report has not earned the benefit of the doubt.
  detail: Start with who owns the account. Dates next, then the numbers. A gap between two reports is not an answer. It is a question with paperwork attached.

[spotlight] If something is wrong, you can dispute it, and FCAC says bureaus have to correct real errors free of charge. Just remember that a correction at one bureau does not travel to the other. If the error sits on both files, you file twice.

## Glossary of terms

[glossary]
  - Credit bureau — A company that collects and maintains credit information about you. Canada has two major bureaus: Equifax and TransUnion.
  - Credit report — The record of your credit accounts, your balances, and how you have paid them over time.
  - Credit score — A number calculated from the information in your credit report. It is not the same thing as the report itself.
  - Update date — The date attached to the account details a bureau shows, which explains why a balance can look older than the one in your banking app.
  - Creditor / lender — The company that lent you the money and reports the account to a bureau.
  - Hard inquiry — A credit check connected to an application for new credit, which may affect your credit score.
  - Soft inquiry — A credit check that does not affect your score, such as looking at your own credit information.
  - Consumer Disclosure — The TransUnion file prepared for you, which lists more credit checks than the shorter version sold to businesses.
  - Business Version — The shorter TransUnion report that businesses buy. It shows less than your own copy.
  - Dispute — A formal request to investigate something on your credit report that you believe is wrong or incomplete.
  - Fraud alert — A notice on your credit file asking lenders to take extra steps to confirm your identity.
