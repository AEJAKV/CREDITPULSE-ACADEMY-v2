## Start here

[paragraph] An estate plan is the paperwork that lets other people act when you can't — while you're alive and unable to manage things, and after you're gone. It isn't about controlling every last detail. It's about cutting down on confusion, delay, arguments and unnecessary cost for whoever gets left holding the job.

[paragraph] Paperwork has terrible timing. It shows up exactly when nobody wants to deal with it. A clear plan means your family spends less time hunting for account numbers and more time handling what actually matters.

[paragraph] This topic deserves care, so we'll keep the language plain. Estate law runs by province and territory, and your own circumstances can change the outcome — treat this as a starting point, and use a lawyer or notary qualified where you live for personal advice and document preparation.

[chips]
  - Explain the jobs of a will, an executor or liquidator, and incapacity documents.
  - Tell a beneficiary apart from an estate representative.
  - Identify which accounts need a beneficiary or successor review.
  - Spot common conflicts between account forms and a will.
  - Build an organized estate information file.
  - Recognize which questions actually need provincial legal advice.

[myths]
  - “A will keeps my estate out of probate entirely.” — A will names your instructions and your representative — it doesn't guarantee a court will skip its confirmation step. Probate can still apply depending on the property, the institution and where you live.

## Treat Your Estate Plan Like a System

[paragraph] A will matters, but it's only one piece of a bigger machine. A complete basic system is really nine smaller jobs working together.

[chips]
  - A valid will, prepared and signed under the law that applies where you live.
  - An estate representative — an executor, estate trustee, personal representative or liquidator.
  - Incapacity documents that work while you're still alive.
  - Beneficiary or successor instructions, kept current on every account where the law allows them.
  - A plain-language inventory of your assets and debts.
  - Insurance and pension information in one place.
  - Guardianship or tutorship planning, where minor children are involved.
  - Instructions for digital accounts and important personal property.
  - A secure location the right people can actually find.

[paragraph] Each piece runs on a different timeline. A will generally kicks in after death. A power of attorney — whatever your province calls it — works while you're alive, under its own rules, and that authority ends the moment you die. From there, the estate representative's job takes over.

[spotlight] Naming the same trusted person for every role isn't automatically simplest — financial decisions, health decisions, child care and estate administration all call for different skills.

[paragraph] The plan should also note what skips the estate altogether. Joint ownership, insurance, pensions, trusts and valid beneficiary designations can all move property a different way — the details depend on the asset, the contract and your province.

[myths]
  - “Adding my adult kid as joint owner is basically the same as estate planning.” — Joint ownership can trigger its own tax, creditor, control and family complications. What it actually does often depends on the intention behind it and your province's law — get advice before changing ownership.

## The Will, and the Person Who Carries It Out

[cards]
  - WHAT A WILL CAN DO — A will can say who gets what, name the person responsible for running the estate, set up trusts, and give instructions for beneficiaries who are still minors. It can share wishes about guardianship too, though a court usually keeps the final say when a child is involved.
  - WHAT A WILL DOESN'T CONTROL — Insurance policies, pensions, registered accounts and other contracts can carry their own valid designations that route money outside the estate. Jointly owned property can follow its own path as well. A will simply doesn't reach everything.

[paragraph] Die without a valid will — "dying intestate" — and provincial or territorial law decides who administers the estate and how it's split. Those default rules don't know your actual relationships. An unmarried partner, a stepchild, a close friend or a favourite charity can walk away with nothing unless a valid document says otherwise.

[myths]
  - “The oldest child should automatically be the executor.” — Age isn't a job qualification. Pick the person based on ability, availability, trust, location and how the family actually gets along.

[paragraph] The person who settles an estate goes by different names — executor, estate trustee, administrator, personal representative, or in Quebec, liquidator. The job covers finding the will, tracking down property and debts, protecting assets, notifying institutions, filing tax returns, paying real obligations, keeping records, talking to beneficiaries, and distributing what's left.

[paragraph] Look for someone organized, trustworthy, comfortable asking for professional help, and able to handle conflict. Talk to them before you name them, and name a backup in case the first choice can't do it. Being good at family dinners is not proof of being good at estate accounting — use the actual job description.

[spotlight] An executor can also be a beneficiary — that's fine, but they still need to keep their personal interest separate from their duties, with careful records.

## Give Beneficiary Designations Their Own Review

[paragraph] A beneficiary is whoever is meant to receive the money or property — not automatically the executor, and an executor isn't automatically an heir. These instructions can live on life insurance, TFSAs, RRSPs, RRIFs, pensions and annuities, and what they actually do depends on the plan and your province. Trust the record the institution has on file, not a note in a personal folder.

[cards]
  - TFSA: SUCCESSOR HOLDER VS. BENEFICIARY — Where it's allowed, a spouse or common-law partner can be named successor holder — they become the new account holder, and the TFSA keeps its tax-free status. A beneficiary instead receives proceeds, not the account, and tax can apply to income earned after death. Quebec doesn't recognize TFSA successor-holder designations, so Quebec residents need advice on using a will instead.
  - RRSP AND RRIF DESIGNATIONS — The usual rule pulls the full value of an RRSP or RRIF into the deceased's income right before death. Rollovers may be available for a spouse, common-law partner, or a financially dependent child or grandchild — but the conditions, forms and deadlines all matter. Naming a beneficiary doesn't make that tax bill disappear; the person who gets the account and the estate that pays the tax may not be the same person.
  - PENSIONS AND LIFE INSURANCE — Workplace pensions can include survivor benefits under their own rules. A form signed years ago might not reflect a relationship that has since changed. Life insurance can carry both primary and contingent beneficiaries — ask the insurer or plan administrator to confirm, in writing, what's currently on file.

[spotlight] Divorce, separation, marriage or a new will do not automatically update every beneficiary form. Check each account directly after any major life event.

[myths]
  - “A new will automatically updates every account.” — Account contracts and direct designations can run on their own track, separate from the will. Review both together, account by account.

## Priya Makes the Records Agree

[story] Meet Priya
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ Priya owns a home, has a workplace pension, one RRSP, two TFSAs at different banks, and a life insurance policy. Her will names her sister Meena as executor and splits the estate between her two adult kids.
  ¶ During an annual check, Priya asks every institution to confirm what's actually on file. One TFSA still lists a former partner. Her pension has no alternate contact. The life insurance form names only one child.
  ¶ She doesn't rush to fix anything online. She brings her statements, her will and her family's goals to an estate lawyer and a tax professional, who explain which forms she can update directly, which instructions belong in the will, and how tax on the registered accounts could hit the estate.
  ¶ Priya talks it through with Meena, names a backup executor, and builds a secure estate information file. Her kids don't get account balances or passwords — they get something more useful right now: who to call, and where the plan lives.

[heading] What actually fixed things?

[paragraph] A yearly check found the mismatches while Priya could still fix them. The improvement wasn't new money — it was coordinating what she already had.

## Plan for Incapacity, Not Just Death

[paragraph] Estate planning usually gets attention after a health scare, but incapacity documents solve a different problem: they let someone else make specific decisions for you while you're still alive, if you can't.

[paragraph] The names change across the country. A financial version might be called an enduring power of attorney, a continuing power of attorney, a mandate, or another provincial term. A health or personal-care version might be a personal directive, a representation agreement, a power of attorney for personal care, an advance health care directive, or a protection mandate.

[paragraph] A basic power of attorney can stop working the moment someone becomes mentally incapable — an enduring or continuing version is built to keep working through incapacity, if it meets your province's requirements. Don't trust a downloaded form just because of its title. Confirm what it actually does, when it starts, and whether it survives incapacity where you live.

[paragraph] Pick decision-makers with the same care you'd use for an executor. Whoever handles finances should understand record-keeping and conflicts of interest. Whoever handles health or personal care should understand your values and be able to speak up under pressure. Talk to them before signing, give them what they need to act, and keep the sensitive records protected.

[spotlight] A power of attorney ends at death — it doesn't let anyone keep paying bills or handing out property as if they were the executor.

## Build an Estate Information File

[paragraph] An estate file is a map, not a vault for every secret. It should tell your representative what exists, where the real records live, and who to call.

[cards]
  - IDENTITY & LEGAL DOCUMENTS — Your full legal name, province or territory, marital status and key contacts. Note where the original will and incapacity documents actually sit, plus your lawyer or notary's contact info. Don't leave the only original will somewhere nobody can reach — a safe deposit box can be surprisingly hard to open quickly.
  - ASSETS & DEBTS — Financial institutions, account types, the last four digits of account numbers, property addresses, pensions, insurance policies, business interests, loans, credit cards and anything valuable. Update it whenever an account opens, closes or moves.
  - DIGITAL & PASSWORDS — Skip writing full passwords into the inventory. Use a reputable password manager with emergency access, or leave instructions your lawyer has approved. Note how to find online businesses, domains, subscriptions, loyalty points, cloud storage, crypto and social accounts.
  - TAX & GOVERNMENT INFO — Your representative will likely need old tax returns, notices of assessment, property records, benefit info and your SIN. CRA won't hand over tax records without proof — usually registration through Represent a Client plus a death certificate and the document naming the executor or liquidator.

[paragraph] The representative also has to file a final T1 return, possibly a T3 return for the estate, and may need a CRA clearance certificate before handing anything out — distributing assets while tax is still owing can leave them personally on the hook, up to what they handed out.

[paragraph] Add practical notes too — pets, personal items, funeral wishes, charitable intentions, and who should be told. These aren't legally binding on their own, but they help your representative guess less. Keep them consistent with the will.

[spotlight] Tell your executor where the actual will is stored. Taping the only clue to the back of the will itself is a filing system designed by a mystery novelist.

## What Changes by Province or Territory

[paragraph] Estate and incapacity law is mostly provincial or territorial, so the same document can behave differently depending on your address.

[ordered]
  - How a will must be signed and witnessed — Requirements differ by province or territory.
  - Whether electronic wills are allowed — Not every province recognizes a digital will.
  - The name and authority of the estate representative — Executor, estate trustee, administrator or liquidator, depending on where you live.
  - Rights of spouses, partners, dependants and children — Some family members can have legal claims regardless of what the will says.
  - What happens without a valid will — Default distribution rules may not match your actual relationships.
  - Probate or estate administration procedures — Court processes and charges vary by jurisdiction.
  - Rules for beneficiary designations and joint property — What passes outside the will differs from province to province.
  - Documents used during incapacity — Financial and health decision-making forms carry different names and rules.
  - Family claims that can change a distribution — Some claims can override what the will or default rules say.

[paragraph] Ontario tends to use "estate trustee" and "continuing power of attorney." British Columbia runs on its Wills, Estates and Succession Act and offers a Wills Registry to record where a will lives. Quebec follows civil law, uses "succession" and "liquidator," and recognizes notarized, witnessed and even handwritten wills under its own rules — a non-notarial Quebec will generally needs verification after death, and Quebec treats things like TFSA designations differently too.

[paragraph] These are flavour, not a province-by-province guide. Use your jurisdiction's official legal-information service, and have documents reviewed locally. Moving provinces, marrying, separating, divorcing, having a kid, starting a business, or buying property outside Canada are all good reasons for a fresh look.

[heading] Key takeaway

[paragraph] A basic estate plan is coordinated instructions — the will, the representative appointments, the beneficiary records, the incapacity documents, the tax plan and the estate file all need to agree with each other. Clear documents matter, but organized information is what actually makes them usable.

[spotlight] Run a one-page alignment check: write the name on your will, then on every insurance policy, registered account, pension and incapacity document. Flag anything that doesn't match, and skip account values on this page.

## Quick Check: Test What Stuck

[accordions]
  - When does a power of attorney's authority end? — At death. What it can do while you're alive depends on the document and your province's rules.
  - What's the actual difference between an executor and a beneficiary? — The executor administers the estate. A beneficiary just receives money or property from it.
  - Why treat a TFSA successor holder differently from a TFSA beneficiary? — A successor holder generally becomes the new account holder where that's allowed. A beneficiary receives proceeds instead, under different tax and account rules.
  - Give two reasons to review an account designation alongside the will. — A designation can route money outside the estate entirely, and the person who receives the account may not be the one whose estate pays the related tax. Old designations can also clash with what you actually want now.
  - Why might a representative wait for a CRA clearance certificate before distributing anything? — It lets them distribute without risking personal liability for unpaid CRA amounts the certificate covers.

## Glossary of Terms

[glossary]
  - Administrator — A person appointed to administer an estate when there is no executor able to act, often through a court process.
  - Beneficiary — A person or organization intended to receive money, property or a benefit.
  - Clearance certificate — A CRA certificate that can protect a legal representative from personal liability for covered unpaid tax amounts before distributing property.
  - Estate — The property, rights, debts and obligations dealt with after a person's death.
  - Executor — A person named in a will to administer an estate. Other jurisdictions may use estate trustee, personal representative or liquidator.
  - Intestate — Dying without a valid will that disposes of the relevant estate property.
  - Liquidator — The term commonly used in Quebec for the person who settles a succession.
  - Power of attorney — A legal document giving another person authority to act in specified matters while the person granting authority is alive.
  - Probate — A court process that confirms authority to administer an estate or validates a will for estate administration. Names and procedures vary.
  - Successor holder — Where permitted, a spouse or common-law partner named to become the holder of a TFSA after the original holder's death.
  - Will — A legal document that gives instructions for property and estate administration after death.
