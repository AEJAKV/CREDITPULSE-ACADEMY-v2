## Start here

[paragraph] Buying individual stocks one at a time is slow, expensive, and risky — all your eggs in a handful of baskets.

[paragraph] Funds solve that. Instead of picking single companies, you buy one product that holds hundreds or thousands of them at once.

[paragraph] There are two main ways to do this in Canada: mutual funds and ETFs. They solve the same problem. They just work differently underneath — and Module 2.12 already showed you the biggest difference between them is often cost.

## What they actually have in common

[paragraph] Both mutual funds and ETFs pool money from many investors into one fund. Both hold a mix of investments — stocks, bonds, or both. Both let you own a small slice of a large, diversified basket without buying each piece individually.

[paragraph] The difference isn’t what they hold. It’s how you buy them, and how they’re priced.

## Where they actually diverge

[paragraph] How you buy them. A mutual fund is usually bought directly through a bank or an advisor, at a price set once a day, after markets close. An ETF trades on a stock exchange, just like an individual stock. You can buy or sell it any time markets are open, at a price that moves throughout the day.

[paragraph] How they’re priced. Mutual funds have one price a day — the Net Asset Value, calculated after the market closes. Whether you place your order at 9am or 3pm, you get that same end-of-day price. ETFs have a price that moves constantly during market hours, the same way a stock does. Buy at 10am, and you might pay a different price than someone who bought the same ETF at 2pm.

[paragraph] How they’re managed. Many mutual funds are actively managed — a person or team decides what to buy and sell, trying to beat the market. Most ETFs are passively managed, simply tracking an index like the S&P 500, with no one trying to outguess the market.

[paragraph] This isn’t an absolute rule — actively managed ETFs exist, and passive index mutual funds exist too. But it’s the general pattern, and it’s a big part of why fees differ so much between the two.

[cards]
  - Bought through — Mutual Fund: Bank or advisor — ETF: A brokerage, like a stock
  - Priced — Mutual Fund: Once a day, after close — ETF: Continuously, during market hours
  - Usually managed — Mutual Fund: Actively — ETF: Passively
  - Typical MER — Mutual Fund: Around 1.5% — ETF: 0.06% to 0.25%

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: USE A LIMIT ORDER, NOT A MARKET ORDER

[spotlight] Continuous pricing cuts both ways. A mutual fund buyer never has to think about this, since every order fills at the same end-of-day price. An ETF buyer does. A market order fills immediately at whatever price is available right now, which on a thinly traded ETF, or during a volatile few minutes, can be noticeably worse than the price you saw quoted a moment earlier. A limit order fixes that: you set the highest price you are willing to pay to buy, or the lowest you’ll accept to sell, and the order only fills at that price or better. It might not fill instantly, but it protects you from an unexpectedly bad fill. For a widely traded, broad-market ETF this rarely matters much. For anything less liquid, it is the one habit that separates a clean ETF purchase from a slightly expensive surprise — and it costs nothing extra to use.

[myths]
  - “Active management means someone is protecting my money from market drops.” — Active managers try to beat the market, but decades of research show most fail to do so consistently, especially after fees are subtracted. Active management isn’t a safety net — it’s a bet that a manager can outguess the market, made at a meaningfully higher cost.

## Passive doesn’t mean careless

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: PASSIVE DOESN’T MEAN CARELESS

[spotlight] The word passive undersells what’s actually happening. A passive index ETF isn’t unmanaged — it’s automatically and continuously rebalanced to match its target index, without a person making judgment calls about which stocks to favour. When a company grows, it naturally makes up more of the index and more of the fund. When a company shrinks, the same happens in reverse. All without anyone deciding to buy or sell. This isn’t a lesser version of active management. It’s a different philosophy entirely: rather than trying to guess which companies will outperform, you simply own the whole market, and let the market’s own math do the rebalancing. That philosophy is a large part of why passive funds tend to cost less — there’s no team of analysts to pay for picking stocks.

## The all-in-one option

[paragraph] A newer category worth knowing about: all-in-one ETFs. Instead of buying separate ETFs for Canadian stocks, US stocks, international stocks and bonds, one single fund holds a pre-built mix of all of them.

[paragraph] These funds come in different risk levels — some heavier in stocks, some heavier in bonds — and automatically rebalance to keep that mix steady over time. For someone who wants broad diversification without managing several separate funds, this is often the simplest starting point.

[paragraph] They typically charge a slightly higher MER than a single-index ETF, since they’re bundling several funds together — but still dramatically less than a typical mutual fund.

[myths]
  - “ETFs are too complicated for a beginner investor.” — A single all-in-one ETF can actually be simpler than a mutual fund portfolio, not more complicated. One purchase, automatic rebalancing, broad diversification, and a low fee — there’s an argument it’s the more beginner-friendly option, not the less friendly one.

## Choosing between a mutual fund and an ETF

[chips]
  - I understand both hold similar underlying investments — the difference is structure, not necessarily what’s inside.
  - I know whether my current holdings are actively or passively managed.
  - I’ve compared the MER of what I hold against a comparable ETF option.
  - I understand ETF prices move throughout the day, while mutual fund prices are set once daily.
  - I’ve considered whether an all-in-one ETF could simplify a portfolio I’m currently managing across several separate funds.

## Wei’s spreadsheet

[story] Wei’s spreadsheet
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ Wei had four separate mutual funds, each recommended over the years by a different advisor as her situation changed — one for Canadian stocks, one for US stocks, one for bonds, one balanced fund thrown in for good measure. She’d never sat down and looked at them together.
  ¶ Curious after a conversation with a coworker, she built a simple spreadsheet: fund name, MER, and what each one actually held. It took an evening. The pattern that emerged surprised her — there was significant overlap between three of the four funds, all quietly charging her separate management fees to hold many of the same large companies.
  ¶ She didn’t rush into anything. Over a few months, working with a fee-only advisor, she consolidated into a single all-in-one ETF that covered roughly the same overall mix she’d had before — at a fraction of the combined cost, and with far less to track.
  ¶ Her portfolio didn’t get simpler by accident. It got simpler because she finally looked at what she actually owned, all in one place, instead of trusting that four separate recommendations, made at four separate times, had added up to a coherent whole.

[takeaway] Mutual funds and ETFs both pool money into diversified baskets of investments — the real differences are how you buy them, how they’re priced, and typically how they’re managed. ETFs trade like stocks throughout the day; mutual funds price once daily. Passive index funds simply track the market rather than trying to beat it, which is a major reason they tend to cost far less.

## Glossary

[glossary]
  - ETF — Exchange-Traded Fund. A fund that trades on a stock exchange throughout the day, like a stock.
  - Net Asset Value (NAV) — The once-daily price at which mutual fund units are bought and sold.
  - Active management — A fund manager making buy and sell decisions, trying to outperform the market.
  - Passive management — A fund simply tracking an index, without a manager picking individual investments.
  - All-in-one ETF — A single fund holding a pre-built, diversified mix across regions and asset types, automatically rebalanced.
