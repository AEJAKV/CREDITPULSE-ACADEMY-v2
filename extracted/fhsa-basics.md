## Start here

[paragraph] For a long time, Canadians saving for a first home had two options, and neither was quite right for the job.

[paragraph] An RRSP gave a deduction, but withdrawals for anything other than retirement usually meant tax, with one narrow exception. A TFSA gave tax-free withdrawals, but no deduction on the way in.

[paragraph] In 2023, a new account showed up that took the best part of each. The First Home Savings Account — FHSA for short — gives you a deduction going in, like an RRSP, and tax-free withdrawals coming out, like a TFSA. If you’re saving for a first home, it’s worth understanding well.

## Who actually qualifies

[paragraph] Two conditions, both fairly simple.

[chips]
  - You’re a Canadian resident, 18 or older. The usual baseline for a registered account.
  - You’re a first-time home buyer. Meaning you haven’t owned and lived in a home — as your principal residence — in the current calendar year or the four before it. This applies to your spouse or partner too, if you have one.

[paragraph] That second condition trips people up. It doesn’t mean you’ve never owned a home, ever. It means you haven’t lived in one you owned recently. Someone who owned and sold a home eight years ago, and has been renting since, can still qualify.

[myths]
  - “Once you’ve owned a home, you can never open an FHSA again.” — Not true. The rule looks at the current year and the four before it — a rolling window, not a lifetime ban. Owned a home a decade ago and rented ever since? You very likely qualify now.

## How the money moves

[paragraph] Contributions are deductible. Put money in, and it comes off your taxable income for that year — the same benefit as an RRSP contribution.

[paragraph] Growth is tax-free. While the money sits in the account, any growth, interest, or dividends are untouched by tax.

[paragraph] Qualifying withdrawals are tax-free too. Take the money out to buy your first qualifying home, and none of it — not your contributions, not the growth — gets taxed. That’s the part that makes this account genuinely unusual: a deduction going in, and no tax coming out. Most registered accounts only offer one or the other.

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: OPEN IT BEFORE YOU’RE READY TO FILL IT

[spotlight] This is the single most valuable piece of advice in this whole module, and it costs nothing to act on. Contribution room doesn’t exist until the account exists. It doesn’t build in the background the way some other room does — you have to actually open the account first. Someone who opens an FHSA the year they’re finally serious about buying has already missed every year of room they could have quietly built up before that. Someone who opens it years earlier, even with a token first deposit, starts stacking up room immediately — room that carries forward and waits for them. If buying a first home is anywhere on your horizon, even a distant one, opening the account today costs you nothing and loses you nothing. Waiting costs you real room.

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: WHAT HAPPENS IF YOU NEVER BUY

[spotlight] The advice above only works if opening early feels safe, so here’s the part that makes it safe. An FHSA does not force a taxable withdrawal or simply vanish if your plans change and you never end up buying a qualifying home. Before the account’s 15-year or age-71 deadline, you can transfer the balance into your RRSP or RRIF, tax-free, and it does not use up any of your separate RRSP contribution room — it moves over on top of whatever room you already have. The transfer itself isn’t deductible again, since you already claimed that deduction when you contributed, but nothing about the money gets taxed on the way over, and nothing forces your hand before the deadline. That single feature is why opening an FHSA the moment you’re eligible carries almost no downside, whether or not a home purchase actually happens.

## Stacking it with the Home Buyers’ Plan

[paragraph] The FHSA isn’t your only tool. The Home Buyers’ Plan lets you withdraw up to $60,000 from an RRSP for a first home too — though unlike the FHSA, that money has to be repaid over 15 years, or it becomes taxable.

[paragraph] Here’s the part worth knowing: you can use both, on the same home purchase. A single buyer can draw on up to $100,000 combined. Two first-time buyers purchasing together can draw on up to $200,000.

[paragraph] They’re not the same tool, though, and it’s worth keeping the difference straight.

[myths]
  - “The FHSA and the Home Buyers’ Plan are basically the same thing.” — They aren’t. FHSA money is never repaid — it’s yours outright once withdrawn for a qualifying home. Home Buyers’ Plan money is a loan from your own RRSP, with a real repayment schedule attached. Using both together is smart. Treating them as interchangeable is how people end up owing money back to an RRSP they thought was theirs free and clear.

## Does this apply to me?

[chips]
  - I haven’t owned and lived in a home, as a principal residence, in the current year or the prior four.
  - I have an FHSA open, even if I’m not close to buying yet.
  - I know my current contribution room, not just the annual limit.
  - If I’m also using the Home Buyers’ Plan, I understand it’s a loan I’ll need to repay, unlike the FHSA.
  - I know the account has to close within 15 years, or by the year I turn 71.

## Jamal’s four hundred dollars

[story] Jamal’s four hundred dollars
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ Jamal was twenty-six, renting, and years away from affording a home in his city. Opening a home-buying savings account felt premature — almost like planning a wedding before finding a partner.
  ¶ A colleague mentioned, almost in passing, that the point of an FHSA wasn’t to be ready. It was to have the room waiting for when you were. Jamal opened one that week with four hundred dollars, mostly to see what the paperwork even involved.
  ¶ He kept contributing modestly for three years, never treating it as a priority, never rushing it. By the time he was actually ready to buy — sooner than he’d expected, after a promotion — he had meaningful room built up, a real deduction history behind him, and a head start he wouldn’t have had if he’d waited until he felt ready to open the account in the first place.

[takeaway] The FHSA gives you a tax deduction going in and tax-free withdrawals coming out — a combination no other registered account offers. Room only builds once the account exists, so open one as soon as you’re eligible, even far ahead of buying. It can be combined with the Home Buyers’ Plan for even more, but remember: the FHSA never needs repaying, and the Home Buyers’ Plan does.

## Glossary

[glossary]
  - FHSA — First Home Savings Account. Deductible contributions, tax-free qualifying withdrawals, for first-time home buyers.
  - Qualifying withdrawal — A withdrawal used to buy an eligible first home, made tax-free.
  - Home Buyers’ Plan (HBP) — A separate program letting first-time buyers withdraw from an RRSP, which must be repaid over 15 years.
  - First-time home buyer — Someone who hasn’t owned and lived in a home, as a principal residence, in the current year or the prior four.
