## Start here

[paragraph] Most people dread tax season before they’ve even opened a single form.

[paragraph] Here’s the truth. For most Canadians, filing a return takes less time than a load of laundry. Software does the math. The CRA even fills in half the boxes for you.

[paragraph] The part that actually causes trouble isn’t the filing. It’s not knowing what you’re supposed to do, or waiting so long that a small task turns into a stressful one.

[paragraph] This module walks through the whole process, start to finish, in plain steps.

## Do you even need to file?

[paragraph] Short answer: almost always yes, even with little or no income.

[paragraph] You must file if you owe tax, if the CRA sends you a request, or if you’re self-employed. But here’s what most people miss: you should file even when none of those apply.

[paragraph] Filing is how you unlock benefits. The GST/HST credit. The Canada Child Benefit. Provincial credits. None of these get paid automatically — they get calculated from your tax return. Skip filing, and you skip the benefit, even if you’d have qualified for every dollar of it.

[paragraph] Zero income is not a reason to skip filing. It’s often the strongest reason to file.

[myths]
  - “If I made no money this year, there’s no point filing.” — This is backwards. A year with little or no income is often the year you qualify for the most in benefits and credits. Filing with zero income costs you nothing and can put real money in your pocket.

## The calendar, in order

[paragraph] A handful of dates matter each year. Here’s the order they arrive in, using 2026 as the example year.

[chips]
  - Late February. T4 and other tax slips arrive, either by mail or in your CRA account. NETFILE, the online filing system, opens around this time too.
  - April 30. The deadline for most Canadians to file, and to pay anything owed.
  - June 15. The filing deadline if you or your spouse are self-employed. But watch closely — any money owed is still due April 30, even with the later filing date.

[paragraph] That gap between the two June 15 rules trips people up every year. Filing late is one problem. Paying late is a separate, and more expensive, one.

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: SELF-EMPLOYED? TWO DEADLINES, NOT ONE

[spotlight] If you’re self-employed, you get extra time to file, until June 15. Many people hear that and relax completely. But the CRA still wants any money you owe by April 30, the same date as everyone else. Miss that, and interest starts building on May 1, regardless of your June 15 filing deadline. The fix is simple. File whenever you’re ready between now and June 15. But if you think you’ll owe money, make a payment, even an estimate, by April 30. You can always true it up later.

## Three ways to actually file

[paragraph] NETFILE, with software. The most common route. You use CRA-certified software — several free options exist — to prepare your return, then submit it electronically. Fast, and it gives you your Notice of Assessment within days rather than weeks.

[paragraph] Auto-fill My Return. Not a separate filing method — a feature inside NETFILE software. Log into your CRA account, and the software pulls in your T4s, T5s, RRSP contributions and more automatically. Less typing, fewer mistakes.

[paragraph] A tax professional, using EFILE. An accountant or tax preparer files on your behalf, for a fee. Worth it if your situation is complicated — self-employment, rental income, a life change like a divorce or an inheritance.

[paragraph] Free help exists too. Community Volunteer Income Tax Program clinics prepare simple returns at no cost for modest-income households. Worth knowing about, even if you don’t need it yourself this year.

[myths]
  - “I need to hire an accountant every year or I’ll do it wrong.” — Most Canadians with a straightforward employment income can file accurately using free certified software in under an hour. Save the accountant for the years your situation actually gets complicated, self-employment, a big life change, or investment income you’re unsure how to report.

## What happens after you file

[paragraph] You’ll get a Notice of Assessment. Think of it as the CRA’s receipt — confirming what they received, what they calculated, and whether you owe money or get a refund.

[paragraph] Filed online with direct deposit set up? Refunds can land in as little as eight business days. Paper filing is slower, often by weeks.

[paragraph] Keep that Notice of Assessment. It shows your RRSP contribution room for next year, and it’s often the fastest way to prove your income if a lender or landlord ever asks for it.

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: LATE FILING COSTS MORE THAN YOU’D THINK

[spotlight] Miss the deadline and owe money? The penalty starts at 5% of the balance owing, plus 1% for every additional month you’re late, up to twelve months. Been late before? The CRA can double that penalty for repeat offenders, 10% plus 2% a month. Here’s the part people miss. That penalty applies to the return, not just the payment. Even if you can’t pay what you owe right now, file on time anyway. A late return with no payment costs far more than an on-time return with a payment plan attached.

## Before you file

[chips]
  - I have all my tax slips — T4, T5, tuition receipts, anything else that applies to me.
  - I know whether I’m filing by April 30 or June 15.
  - If I’m self-employed, I know my payment is still due April 30, regardless of my filing deadline.
  - I’ve set up direct deposit with the CRA, for a faster refund if I’m owed one.
  - I’m filing even if I had little or no income this year.
  - I know where to find free help if my situation feels complicated.

## Mateo’s first return

[story] Mateo’s first return
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ Mateo worked his first job the summer he turned nineteen — a warehouse gig, minimum wage, a few months. He figured it was too small to matter and never filed a return for that year.
  ¶ Three years later, applying for a student line of credit, the bank asked for a Notice of Assessment. Mateo didn’t have one. Not for that first year, and not for the two after it — he’d assumed, each year, that a modest income meant filing wasn’t worth the trouble.
  ¶ A friend at his campus’s free tax clinic helped him file all three years at once. It took one afternoon. Two of those years, it turned out, would have qualified him for GST credit payments he’d simply never claimed — money that had been sitting there the whole time, waiting on a form nobody had filled in.
  ¶ Mateo files every year now, the same week his T4 arrives. It takes him about twenty minutes.

[takeaway] File every year, even with little or no income — it’s how you unlock benefits like the GST credit. Most Canadians file by April 30; self-employed filers get until June 15, but any money owed is still due April 30. File on time even if you can’t pay in full, since the late-filing penalty is steeper than the cost of a payment plan. And keep your Notice of Assessment — you’ll need it more often than you’d expect.

## Your action this week

[ordered]
  - Check whether you have a CRA My Account. If not, set one up — it’s the fastest way to see your slips, your Notice of Assessment, and your RRSP room.
  - Gather your tax slips as they arrive, in one folder, physical or digital, so you’re not hunting for them in April.
  - If you skipped filing in a past low-income year, consider filing it now. It’s rarely too late, and it may unlock benefits you never claimed.

## Glossary

[glossary]
  - T4 — The slip your employer issues showing your income and deductions for the year.
  - NETFILE — The CRA’s system for submitting your return electronically through certified software.
  - Auto-fill My Return — A feature that pulls your CRA-held slips directly into your tax software.
  - Notice of Assessment — The CRA’s summary after processing your return, showing what you owe or what you’re refunded.
  - Late-filing penalty — A charge starting at 5% of your balance owing, plus 1% per month late, applied when you file after the deadline and owe money.
  - CVITP — Community Volunteer Income Tax Program. Free tax clinics for people with a modest income and a simple return.
