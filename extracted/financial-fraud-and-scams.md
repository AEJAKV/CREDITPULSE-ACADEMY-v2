## Start here

[paragraph] A scammer’s favourite financial product is urgency. It has no prospectus, no cooling-off period and apparently comes with unlimited exclamation marks.

[paragraph] Fraud can arrive as a bank alert, government impersonation, romance, investment pitch, job offer, marketplace buyer, family emergency or recovery service. The costume changes; the pressure pattern often repeats. This drill teaches a repeatable response without blaming the person targeted. Skilled fraudsters use emotion, timing, trusted names and realistic technology to interrupt ordinary judgment.

[spotlight] A caller ID, email logo or familiar-looking website is not proof of identity. End the contact and verify through a phone number or website you locate independently.

[chips]
  - Recognize pressure, secrecy, impersonation and unusual-payment red flags.
  - Run a separate-channel verification before sending money or information.
  - Protect accounts and evidence after exposure.
  - Use Canada’s reporting and escalation routes.
  - Complete a Household Scam Response Card.

[myths]
  - “Only careless people fall for scams.” — Fraud is engineered to exploit trust, fear, hope, authority and time pressure. Anyone can be targeted. A rehearsed pause and verification routine is more useful than shame.

[transition] A five-question scan is what separates a real emergency from a rehearsed one.
  detail: Running the same five checks every time is what turns a racing pulse back into a decision.

## Run the Five-Red-Flag Scan

[spotlight] Use the STOP test. Source: did you start the contact? Time: are you being rushed? Offer: does the claim survive independent checking? Payment: does the method remove ordinary protections? One red flag does not prove fraud, but it earns a pause.

[heading] Read the pressure pattern

[cards]
  - 01 — Source: did you start the contact through a channel you trust?
  - 02 — Time: are you being rushed, threatened or told to keep the request secret?
  - 03 — Offer: does the claim survive an independent check?
  - 04 — Payment: does the requested method remove ordinary protections?
  - 05 — Credentials: are you being asked for a password, PIN, one-time code, remote access or card pickup?

[spotlight] One-time passcodes are keys, not customer-service trivia. A legitimate code arriving on your device can still be part of a fraudster’s login attempt. Never read it to an unsolicited caller.

[myths]
  - “If the caller knows my name, bank and recent details, the call is genuine.” — Personal information can come from breaches, social media, stolen mail or earlier contact. Verify independently and never rely on knowledge alone.

## Colin, the Bank Investigator and the Very Helpful Courier

[story] Meet Colin
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ Colin Fraser was making supper in Winnipeg when his phone displayed the name of his bank. The caller knew his full name and said the fraud department had detected a transfer. To protect his savings, Colin needed to move the money into a ‘secure account’ before the branch closed. The caller was calm, precise and impressively concerned about Colin’s financial well-being.
  ¶ The conflict tightened when a real one-time code arrived by text. The caller asked Colin to read it aloud, then said a courier could collect his bank card as evidence. Colin felt embarrassed that he was unsure. The potatoes were boiling over, the caller was counting down and the entire evening had become a low-budget thriller with excellent caller ID.
  ¶ Colin remembered a family rule: no money, code or card leaves during an incoming contact. He hung up, despite the caller’s warning that the transfer would become permanent. Using the number on the back of his card, he contacted the bank. The bank confirmed it had not called and locked the attempted login. Colin changed the exposed password from a clean device, checked recent transactions and enabled stronger multi-factor authentication.
  ¶ He saved the phone number, text, time and notes without clicking anything. Because an account had been targeted, he followed the bank’s instructions, reported the incident through the Canadian fraud-reporting channel and shared the details with local police as appropriate. He also told his sister, who had received a similar call that week.
  ¶ Two days later, someone offered to recover money for an upfront fee. Colin had lost no money, but the second caller apparently had optimism and an invoice ready. He refused and added the contact to his report. The conclusion was not that Colin had outsmarted every scam. He had used a routine that worked while stressed: pause, leave the channel, verify, protect and report.

[spotlight] Recovery scams often target people already contacted or harmed. No legitimate recovery service proves itself just because the caller knows details from the first incident. Verify independently, and never send an upfront payment under pressure.

[heading] What resolved the conflict?

[paragraph] Colin used a pre-decided family rule, a trusted contact path and immediate account protection. The routine broke the pressure cycle, not perfect confidence.

[transition] Colin's rule cost him nothing and saved his account, because he had decided it before he ever needed it.

## Use the First-Hour Response Ladder

[ordered]
  - Protect safety — If anyone is in immediate danger, call 911. Do not meet a courier, confront a suspect or continue contact to collect evidence.
  - Contact the money channel — Call the bank, card issuer, payment service, crypto platform or transfer provider immediately using a trusted number. Ask about stopping, recalling, freezing or disputing the transaction.
  - Secure access — Change exposed passwords from a trusted device, starting with email and financial accounts. Turn on MFA and follow the provider's instructions.
  - Preserve evidence — Keep receipts, transaction IDs, messages, numbers, profiles, websites, dates and a short timeline. Do not keep malware open merely to take a better screenshot.
  - Report and update — Report fraud or attempted fraud to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre. Report to local police when appropriate, and get a file number. Update the report if new activity appears.

[spotlight] Reporting an attempted scam still matters. It can connect patterns, warn others and support investigations even when no money left your account.

[spotlight] Preserve facts, not shame: what contact arrived, what was shared, what moved, when it happened and what protection followed.

[myths]
  - “Once money is sent, reporting is pointless.” — Recovery is not guaranteed, but rapid contact may improve the chance of stopping or tracing a payment. Reporting also creates records and intelligence. Act quickly and beware anyone promising recovery for an upfront fee.

## Quick Check: Choose the Best Next Move

[accordions]
  - Why is a separate channel essential for verification? — It leaves the suspicious contact and reaches the real organization through independently located information.
  - Name three pressure signals that justify a pause. — Urgency, secrecy, threats, impersonation, unusual payment methods or requests for codes and credentials.
  - What should you do with an unexpected one-time passcode request? — Do not share it. End the contact and verify independently.
  - What are the first two organizations to contact after money is transferred fraudulently? — The financial or payment provider immediately, then local police or the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre as appropriate.
  - Why report attempted fraud and recovery scams? — Reports connect patterns, warn others, support investigations and document follow-up activity.

## Glossary of Terms

[glossary]
  - Phishing — Fraudulent digital contact designed to obtain information, money or access.
  - Spoofing — Falsifying displayed caller, sender or website information to appear trusted.
  - Social engineering — Manipulating people through emotion, trust or pressure rather than only technical attack.
  - One-time passcode — Temporary code used to confirm access or a transaction. Do not share it with an unsolicited contact.
  - Recovery scam — A follow-up fraud promising to recover losses, often for an upfront payment.
  - MFA — Multi-factor authentication requiring more than one type of proof to access an account.
