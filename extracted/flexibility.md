## Start here

[paragraph] This course provides general education, not diagnosis, treatment, or rehabilitation. Seek qualified advice before stretching an acutely injured area, a recently operated joint, an unstable joint, or an area with unexplained pain, numbness, weakness, or swelling. Follow condition-specific guidance for osteoporosis, joint replacement, hypermobility, neurological conditions, pregnancy, or disability. Stop and seek urgent medical help for sudden neurological symptoms, a serious fall, a visibly deformed joint, or a new inability to bear weight.

[paragraph] Before stretching, you will answer four questions: What is the goal? Which area limits it? What type of stretch fits? How did the body respond? This protects learners from collecting stretches as if the human body awards loyalty points.

## Give Flexibility a Job

[paragraph] Flexibility describes the available range at a joint or the ability of muscles and other tissues to tolerate lengthening. It can support comfort and movement when a task requires more range than is currently available.

[paragraph] Choose a specific reason: reaching overhead, taking a longer walking stride, using a work position, performing a sport skill, or reducing the restricted feeling after prolonged sitting. The desired range should match the activity.

[paragraph] Flexibility is not a complete fitness program. Strength, balance, aerobic capacity, coordination, sleep, and daily movement still matter. A person can touch their toes and remain unprepared to carry the recycling downstairs.

[spotlight] Record the movement you want to make more comfortable. A stretch without a purpose is difficult to evaluate.

[myths]
  - “Everyone should be able to touch their toes.” — Body proportions, joint structure, nerve sensitivity, movement skill, and training history differ. Toe touching is one movement, not a health certificate.

[heading] Name your flexibility goal

[paragraph] Finish this sentence: “I want more comfortable range for ___.”

## Choose Dynamic or Static Work

[paragraph] Dynamic flexibility uses controlled movement through a comfortable range. Leg swings performed with control, arm movements, walking lunges, and gentle trunk rotations are examples. Dynamic work can prepare the movements and temperature needed for activity.

[paragraph] Static stretching holds a position for a period of time. It can be used in a separate flexibility session or after activity when the goal is to practise relaxed range. The position should allow normal breathing and should not create sharp pain or neurological symptoms.

[paragraph] Before strength, speed, or sport activity, begin with easy whole-body movement and task-related dynamic practice. Long, intense static stretching immediately before maximum efforts may temporarily change force or power for some activities. Timing should serve the next task.

[spotlight] Use movement to prepare for movement. Use longer holds when improving or maintaining range is the main job.

## Find a Tolerable Stretch Dose

[paragraph] Move slowly into a mild-to-moderate stretch sensation. Stop before sharp pain, pinching, burning, numbness, tingling, or joint instability. Breathe normally and avoid bouncing.

[paragraph] A short hold repeated several times can be useful. Exact duration and frequency depend on the person, tissue, goal, and professional guidance. The stretch should be repeatable and should not disrupt the activity that follows.

[paragraph] Progress through a slightly longer comfortable hold, another repetition, improved position, or an appropriate increase in frequency. Change one feature at a time. The muscle does not need a surprise party.

[myths]
  - “A stretch only works if it hurts.” — Pain can trigger guarding and may signal irritation or injury. A tolerable stretch sensation is enough for practice.

## Use Position to Target the Right Area

[paragraph] Small setup changes can move a stretch from the intended muscle into a joint or another body region. Use support and stable alignment. Keep the movement within the joint's comfortable path.

[paragraph] For a calf stretch, foot direction and heel contact matter. For the hip, pelvis and trunk position matter. For the chest, shoulder position matters. For the back of the thigh, bending from the hip differs from rounding the whole spine.

[paragraph] Do not copy the deepest-looking version. Choose the version that creates the intended sensation without joint pressure or loss of control. The internet's most flexible person is not present to complete your consent form.

[spotlight] Adjust the stance, support, or angle. Better positioning often creates a clearer stretch with less effort.

## Know When Stretching Is the Wrong Tool

[paragraph] A restricted feeling is not always a flexibility problem. Muscle fatigue, weakness, joint irritation, nerve sensitivity, swelling, stress, poor sleep, prolonged position, or unfamiliar activity can feel like tightness.

[paragraph] If a few gentle movements improve the feeling, movement variety may be enough. If the same area repeatedly feels tight during a task, check technique, workload, strength, and setup. If symptoms include tingling, numbness, weakness, significant swelling, instability, or pain that worsens, seek assessment.

[paragraph] Hypermobility means some joints move beyond a typical range. A person with symptomatic hypermobility may need control, strength, pacing, and individualized care rather than more stretching.

[myths]
  - “Every tight feeling should be stretched.” — The sensation may reflect several causes. Stretching is useful only when limited tissue tolerance or range is part of the actual problem.

## Sophie Stops Stretching Everything

[story] Meet Sophie
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ Sophie, 44, lived in Montreal and worked at a computer. Her neck and shoulders felt tight by late afternoon, so she followed a fifteen-minute full-body stretch video every evening. Some stretches felt pleasant, but the same discomfort returned before dinner.
  ¶ She used the Stretch Decision Guide. Her goal was to finish work with less neck and shoulder discomfort. She noticed that the problem grew after long, uninterrupted screen periods. Her desk setup placed the screen to one side, and her upper-back endurance faded as the day continued.
  ¶ Sophie moved the screen in front of her, used brief movement breaks, practised gentle upper-back rotation and shoulder movement, and added a light row twice a week. She kept one chest stretch that felt useful and removed several unrelated stretches.
  ¶ After six weeks, she reported fewer uncomfortable afternoons and could work longer before needing a break. Her routine fell from fifteen minutes to four. The other eleven minutes returned to civilian life.

[heading] What Sophie did well

[paragraph] She defined the task, examined the environment and workload, combined movement with strength, and kept only the stretch that served the goal.

[spotlight] If a stretch has no clear target, no useful response, and no effect on the chosen task, it may not belong in the routine.

## Protect Joints, Nerves, and Healing Tissue

[paragraph] Stretching should not force a healing injury, unstable joint, or painful surgical area. Follow the restrictions and progression given by the treating professional. Scar, tendon, joint, muscle, and nerve conditions can require different approaches.

[paragraph] Numbness, tingling, electric sensations, spreading pain, or weakness may involve nerve irritation. Do not use a strong stretch to chase these symptoms. Stop and seek qualified assessment.

[paragraph] This lesson is humour-free because acute injury, instability, and neurological symptoms require clear care decisions.

## Measure Range That Matters

[paragraph] Retest the named task under similar conditions. Can you reach, turn, step, or use the position more comfortably? Is the range easier to control? Did the change last beyond the immediate stretch?

[paragraph] An immediate increase can reflect reduced tension or greater tolerance. Longer-term change requires repeated practice and continued use of the range. Strengthening the available range can help make it functional.

[paragraph] Review every two to four weeks. Keep the useful stretch, adjust one dose feature, or replace the exercise if the task does not improve. After illness, injury, or a flare, return to an easier version.

[myths]
  - “A flexibility routine must keep getting deeper.” — Maintenance may be the correct goal. Useful range that supports activity is more valuable than increasing range without a purpose.

[spotlight] A good session should leave the area comfortable and usable. More range accompanied by irritation is not a winning trade.

## Access Across Canada

[paragraph] Qualified help may be available through physiotherapy, occupational therapy, community health centres, recreation programs, Indigenous health organizations, campuses, and adapted exercise services. Public coverage, private benefits, referrals, regulation, and wait times vary by province and territory.

[paragraph] CSEP professionals can provide exercise guidance within their scope. Physiotherapists assess and treat movement and injury concerns. Use qualified care when symptoms, surgery, instability, or a condition changes stretching choices.

[heading] Key takeaway

[paragraph] Flexibility work is most useful when it serves a specific task, uses a tolerable dose, and matches the timing and condition of the body. Stretch for useful range, then practise control and strength there.

[spotlight] Save one photo or written setup cue for each stretch. “Left foot forward, heel down” is more useful than rebuilding the pose from memory while leaning against a laundry basket.

## Quick Check: Test What Stuck

[accordions]
  - What does flexibility describe? — Available joint range or the tolerance of muscles and other tissues to lengthening.
  - How does flexibility differ from mobility? — Mobility adds active control and the ability to use the range in a task.
  - When may dynamic movement fit better than a long static hold? — Before exercise, sport, strength, or another activity requiring movement and force.
  - What sensations mean a stretch should stop? — Sharp pain, pinching, burning, numbness, tingling, instability, or worsening symptoms.
  - Why should setup change before force increases? — A better stance, support, or angle can target the intended area with less joint stress.
  - What actually helped Sophie's discomfort? — Improved desk setup, movement breaks, upper-back and shoulder movement, rowing, and one focused stretch.
  - Why can hypermobility change the plan? — More range may worsen instability; control, strength, pacing, and individual guidance may be needed.
  - What makes flexibility progress useful? — It improves the named task or comfort and remains controllable without irritation.

## Glossary of Terms

[glossary]
  - Dynamic flexibility — Controlled movement through a range without a prolonged hold.
  - Flexibility — Available range or the ability of tissues to tolerate lengthening.
  - Hypermobility — Joint movement beyond a typical range, which may or may not cause symptoms.
  - Static stretch — A position held for a period of time to practise tissue tolerance or range.
  - Stretch tolerance — The level of lengthening sensation a person can comfortably accept.
