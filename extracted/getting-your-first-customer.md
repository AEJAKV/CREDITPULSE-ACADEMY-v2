## Start here

[paragraph] Your first customer probably will not arrive because your new logo sensed a disturbance in the economy. Logos are talented, but they rarely make phone calls. A first sale usually comes from one clear offer, one suitable person and one useful conversation.

[paragraph] The goal is not to convince everyone. It is to find a person with a real problem you can solve safely and profitably, then make the next step easy. Your early process can be simple: identify a narrow customer, build a small prospect list, ask questions, send a written offer, follow up and deliver exactly what was promised.

[spotlight] A first customer is evidence, not a lifetime business model. Use the sale to learn who buys, why they buy, what delivery costs and what must improve.

[chips]
  - State one clear customer problem and outcome.
  - Build a small, qualified contact list.
  - Hold a useful discovery conversation.
  - Present an honest scope, price and next step.
  - Complete a First-Customer Launch Card.

[myths]
  - “I need a large audience before I can make a sale.” — A relevant conversation can matter more than 2,000 indifferent followers. The first customer needs a reason to act—not a stadium.

## Make It Easy to Understand and Easy to Buy

[paragraph] Describe your offer in one sentence: “I help [specific customer] solve [specific problem] by providing [defined service or product] for [price or starting price].”

[cards]
  - CHOOSE THE CUSTOMER — Start with one group whose problem, budget and buying situation you understand. “Local landlords with one to five units” gives clearer direction than “anyone who owns walls.”
  - NAME THE OUTCOME — Describe the useful result, not every task. A customer buys a clean move-out inspection package, a working booking page or three prepared freezer meals—not your internal checklist.
  - SET THE STARTER SCOPE — Limit time, location, revisions, materials, delivery and exclusions. A small fixed pilot can reduce risk for both sides without disguising unpaid work as exposure.
  - PRICE THE WHOLE JOB — Include preparation, travel, materials, fees, communication, delivery, tax treatment and follow-up. State deposits, payment timing and cancellation terms before work begins.

[spotlight] Market research begins with customer and competitor evidence. Ten short conversations can reveal language, objections and buying triggers that a month of logo polishing cannot.

[spotlight] A paid pilot should have a real scope and fair value. Discounting may support a test; permanently underpricing teaches customers a lesson you did not intend.

[myths]
  - “The lowest price wins the first customer.” — Price is only one decision factor. Relevance, trust, proof, speed, clarity and low perceived risk can matter more than being the cheapest unfamiliar option.

## Invite a Next Step, Not a Parade

[paragraph] Build a list of 20 qualified prospects from your existing network, local organizations, referrals, public business directories or appropriate community groups. Record why each person may fit.

[ordered]
  - W — a
  - P — e
  - D — i
  - W — r

[spotlight] A useful opening message is short: relevant observation, specific result, one reason you may help and a low-pressure question. Personalization means relevance—not inserting a first name into a novel.

[spotlight] Follow up once or twice with new value or a clear decision question. Silence is information; it is not permission to begin a 14-message documentary series.

[myths]
  - “A good salesperson handles every objection immediately.” — An objection may signal missing information, poor timing, no authority, weak fit or a genuine no. Ask one respectful question, answer honestly and allow the customer to decide.

[spotlight] Key takeaway: Your first customer comes from focus and trust: choose one suitable buyer, diagnose a real need, make a specific written offer, follow up respectfully and deliver the promised outcome before chasing scale.

## Leah Stops Selling “Everything Digital”

[story] Meet Leah
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ Leah Martin opened a small digital-service business in Winnipeg. Her first post offered websites, social media, logos, newsletters, videos and “basically anything online.” It was less an offer and more a department store wearing one laptop.
  ¶ Three weeks passed without a sale. The conflict was not a lack of effort: Leah posted daily, but nobody knew which problem to bring her. During a conversation with Samir, who owned a neighbourhood physiotherapy clinic, she learned that new patients abandoned the clinic’s confusing mobile booking page.
  ¶ Leah proposed a fixed paid pilot: reorganize one booking page, test it on mobile and provide one round of revisions within seven days. She stated the price, access needed and what was excluded. Samir agreed because the outcome was specific and the risk was contained.
  ¶ Leah delivered on time and asked for factual feedback. Samir reported fewer booking questions and permitted her to use an accurate testimonial. Her first customer did not buy “everything digital.” He bought one easier path to an appointment—which was considerably simpler to invoice.

[spotlight] Ask for feedback after delivery: what changed, what was easiest, what caused hesitation and who else has the same problem. Request permission before publishing a name, quote or result.

[heading] What resolved the conflict?

[paragraph] A narrow, specific offer replaced a vague menu of everything—and gave one prospect a reason to say yes.

## Quick Check: Choose the Best Next Move

[accordions]
  - What belongs in a one-sentence offer? — The specific customer, the specific problem, the defined service or product and the price or starting price.
  - Why is a small qualified prospect list useful? — Twenty people with a clear reason to fit is a sales plan; a huge unqualified list is just noise to work through.
  - What should happen during a discovery conversation? — Ask what is happening now, what result matters, what has been tried, timing, decision process and budget comfort—then listen before presenting the offer.
  - Which details belong in a written proposal or confirmation? — The problem, outcome, scope, price, timing, responsibilities, acceptance method and a genuine expiry date if one applies.
  - What does a respectful follow-up avoid? — Pressuring the prospect with excessive messages—one or two follow-ups with new value or a clear decision question is enough.

## Glossary of Terms

[glossary]
  - Ideal customer — Narrow group most likely to need and value the offer.
  - Value proposition — Clear reason a customer should choose the offer.
  - Qualified prospect — Potential customer with plausible need, fit and ability to decide.
  - Discovery conversation — Questions used to understand a prospect before recommending a solution.
  - Scope — Written boundaries of what will and will not be delivered.
  - Conversion — Movement from one sales step to the next, such as conversation to paid customer.
  - CASL — Canadian rules that include consent and identification requirements for commercial electronic messages.
