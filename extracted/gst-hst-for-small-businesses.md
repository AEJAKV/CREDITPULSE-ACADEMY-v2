## Start here

[paragraph] GST/HST isn't just an extra percentage tacked onto an invoice. It's a system built on four connected decisions: whether the business has to register, whether a sale is taxable, which rate applies, and how much net tax actually gets reported and remitted.

[paragraph] Get those decisions in the right order and GST/HST becomes routine. Try to reconstruct them from memory after the sale, and an invoice can turn into a small detective novel with no satisfying final chapter.

[paragraph] This is general Canadian education, not personal tax, accounting, legal or business advice. GST/HST, QST and provincial sales-tax rules can change and depend on the exact supply, customer, location and business structure — verify anything time-sensitive with the CRA, Revenu Québec where it applies, and a qualified professional. Each section here answers one question in order: must I register, is the sale taxable, which rate applies, what can I claim, when do I file and pay. The sequence matters — tax portals are already fond enough of making people revisit earlier screens.

[chips]
  - Explain how GST/HST differs from income tax.
  - Apply the basic small-supplier registration tests.
  - Distinguish taxable, zero-rated and exempt supplies.
  - Identify why place-of-supply rules matter.
  - Explain the purpose of input tax credits.
  - Recognize the basic invoice, filing and remittance duties of a registrant.
  - Create a GST/HST registration decision record.

[myths]
  - “GST/HST collected is extra profit.” — Collected tax belongs in a tax-payable record, not the owner's spending plan. Revenue and tax can arrive in the same customer payment while still doing completely different jobs.

## Separate Sales Tax From Business Income

[paragraph] GST is a federal value-added tax. Participating provinces combine their provincial component with GST and call it HST. The rate charged depends on the place-of-supply rules and the type of sale — not simply on where the seller happens to be sitting.

[paragraph] For a registered business, GST/HST collected from customers isn't ordinary revenue. The business holds that tax in trust and reports it on a GST/HST return. Under the regular method, eligible input tax credits — ITCs — can reduce the net amount remitted.

[paragraph] Income tax works differently. It's generally based on taxable income after eligible deductions. A business can owe income tax without being registered for GST/HST at all, and it can file a GST/HST return with little or no income-tax profit. Keep the two calculations, and their deadlines, separate.

[spotlight] Put the GST/HST portion of every payment into a separate bookkeeping category immediately. A separate savings account adds protection — money with a government name tag has a habit of being needed later.

## Decide Whether Registration Is Required

[paragraph] Most businesses making taxable supplies in Canada have to register once they stop qualifying as a small supplier. Under the current general rule, the key threshold is $30,000 of worldwide taxable supplies — including zero-rated supplies — before expenses. Revenue from associated persons may need to be folded in.

[paragraph] The threshold can be crossed two different ways.

[cards]
  - ONE CALENDAR QUARTER — Exceed the threshold in a single quarter and the business generally stops being a small supplier on the sale that pushed the total over. Registration is required, and GST/HST generally has to be charged on that very sale — which is why a large contract deserves a registration review before the invoice goes out.
  - FOUR CONSECUTIVE CALENDAR QUARTERS — Exceed the threshold across four consecutive quarters without crossing it in any single one, and the timing works differently — the business generally stops being a small supplier after the month following the end of that four-quarter period, and must register within the required time. Confirm the exact effective date with the CRA's current guidance.

[paragraph] Track the rolling total every month. The test uses taxable revenue before expenses, not profit — a business can run thin margins and still cross the registration threshold.

[myths]
  - “I only test the $30,000 threshold at year-end.” — One test watches a single calendar quarter. The other watches four consecutive calendar quarters. December is not the only month with a calculator.

[heading] Special registration rules

[paragraph] Some activities follow their own rules. A person providing taxable taxi or commercial ride-sharing services generally must register from the start, even below the usual small-supplier threshold. Non-residents, public service bodies, charities, real-property transactions, digital-economy businesses and certain other activities may have distinct rules too.

[spotlight] The threshold applies across all of a person's taxable supplies, not separately to every brand, marketplace or side business. Three small sales channels don't necessarily create three small-supplier limits.

[heading] Voluntary registration

[paragraph] An eligible small supplier can sometimes register voluntarily. It can unlock ITCs on eligible purchases, but it also creates duties — charging and collecting tax, filing returns, keeping records, and normally staying registered for at least a year unless commercial activity stops. Weigh your customers, expected purchases, pricing and administration before registering just because an online form had a friendly green button.

## Classify the Sale Before Choosing a Rate

[paragraph] Registration doesn't mean every sale gets taxed the same way — classify the supply first.

[cards]
  - TAXABLE SUPPLIES — Most goods and services sold in the course of commercial activity are taxable. A registered business generally charges GST/HST at the applicable rate and may claim eligible ITCs connected to that activity.
  - ZERO-RATED SUPPLIES — Taxable at zero percent — the seller doesn't collect GST/HST, but a registrant may generally still claim eligible ITCs related to making the supply. Certain basic groceries, prescription drugs, medical devices and exports can qualify when the legal conditions are met.
  - EXEMPT SUPPLIES — No GST/HST is charged, and unlike zero-rated supplies, expenses used to make exempt supplies generally don't support ITCs. Examples can include many health, education, child-care, legal-aid, residential-rent and financial services — every category has its own conditions.

[paragraph] A customer's invoice can show no tax for both zero-rated and exempt sales. The business result isn't the same — ITC treatment is one major difference.

[myths]
  - “No tax on the invoice means the sale is exempt.” — A sale can be zero-rated, exempt, outside the scope, or made by a non-registrant. The empty tax line doesn't explain why it's empty.

[spotlight] Write the tax classification beside every product or service in the price list. Do the research once, record the source, and review it when the offering changes. Guessing at checkout is a poor customer-service feature.

[heading] Use place-of-supply rules

[paragraph] The applicable rate can depend on where the supply is considered made. Different rules apply to goods, services, intangible property and real-property transactions — a seller in Alberta can make a sale subject to HST in another province. A customer's billing address can matter, but it isn't the universal answer. Use the CRA's current place-of-supply guidance and keep the customer information used to determine the rate.

## Elena Catches the Threshold Before the Invoice

[story] Meet Elena
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ Elena runs a small corporate-training business in Nova Scotia. She checks revenue at year-end and has never come close to the small-supplier threshold — until a national client asks her to deliver a large training project inside one quarter.
  ¶ Her first instinct is to send the usual invoice with no HST and worry about registration later. Instead, she updates a rolling revenue tracker before accepting the final project stage, and finds the new sale would push her worldwide taxable supplies over the threshold within one calendar quarter.
  ¶ She confirms the effective date with an accountant and the CRA guidance, registers, updates her invoice template, checks the customer's province and place-of-supply rule, and adds her business number. She also opens a tax-payable account and a folder for purchase invoices that might support ITCs.
  ¶ The client isn't surprised, because Elena explains the tax before the contract amendment gets signed. Her price stays her price — the HST just shows up separately. At filing time, her sales records, tax collected, and eligible ITCs all reconcile to the return.

[heading] What actually changed?

[paragraph] Elena didn't avoid the administration — she moved it to the point where it could still be handled cleanly. That's usually the less expensive side of a deadline.

## Build Invoices and ITCs From Evidence

[paragraph] A registrant's sales documents should clearly support the tax charged. Depending on the amount and circumstances, required information can include the supplier's name, invoice date, total amount, GST/HST charged (or a statement that tax is included), business number, purchaser name, payment terms and a description of the supply.

[paragraph] Use an invoice template that stores the customer's location, supply date, subtotal, rate, tax amount, total and payment status. Number invoices in sequence, and if one needs correcting, preserve the original record and issue a documented credit note or replacement instead of quietly editing it.

[heading] Input tax credits

[paragraph] An ITC lets a registrant recover GST/HST paid or payable on eligible purchases and expenses used in commercial activities — but it needs the required supporting information from a registered supplier. Personal costs, exempt-activity costs and unsupported amounts don't become ITCs just because they passed through a business credit card.

[paragraph] For mixed-use purchases, claim only the eligible commercial portion. Capital property has its own ITC rules, and meals and entertainment, passenger vehicles, home offices, financial institutions and changes in use can all involve restrictions or special calculations.

[myths]
  - “Any receipt showing GST/HST supports an ITC.” — The registrant still has to meet the eligibility rules and hold the required documentary information. A tax amount printed on paper is a starting point, not a permission slip.

[spotlight] Confirm a supplier's GST/HST account number when an invoice looks unusual or the claim is material — the CRA provides an online registry. ITCs shouldn't rely on a business number assembled with creative punctuation.

[cards]
  - REGULAR METHOD — Net tax generally begins with GST/HST charged or collectible and subtracts eligible ITCs — the standard, itemized approach.
  - QUICK METHOD — Uses prescribed remittance rates for eligible businesses and limits many operating-expense ITCs, since those costs are already built into the rate. It's an election, not a casual shortcut picked after the return is already calculated — compare both methods with a qualified professional first, since fewer calculations don't always mean a lower remittance.

## File the Return, Even When the Period Was Quiet

[paragraph] The CRA assigns a reporting period — commonly annual, quarterly or monthly. Filing and payment deadlines depend on that period and the registrant's circumstances; annual individual filers with business income can even have a payment deadline that arrives before the filing deadline. Use the due dates shown in My Business Account or the official filing information.

[paragraph] Most GST/HST registrants must file electronically for reporting periods beginning in 2024 or later, subject to limited exceptions, through methods like My Business Account or GST/HST NETFILE. Quebec businesses generally file GST and QST through Revenu Québec.

[paragraph] File every required return, including a nil return when no net tax is owing. Registration continues until it's formally cancelled or another authorized change applies — ignoring the account doesn't cause the filing calendar to lose interest.

[spotlight] Reconcile three totals before filing: taxable sales, GST/HST charged or collectible, and eligible ITCs. Then compare the result with the tax-payable account. A return should summarize the books, not introduce itself to them.

[heading] Charged versus collected

[paragraph] Under ordinary invoice accounting, the invoice date can determine when tax must be reported — even if the customer hasn't paid yet. A business can owe tax before receiving the cash. Deposits, bad debts, credit notes and instalment billing can all need special handling. Protect cash flow by stating payment terms, following up on receivables, and moving collected tax out of operating money — GST/HST is not required to wait politely beside an overdue invoice.

[heading] Correct errors openly

[paragraph] Charged the wrong rate, missed a registration date, claimed an unsupported ITC, or filed the wrong amount? Don't quietly alter old records. Preserve the audit trail and use the CRA's correction, adjustment, voluntary-disclosure or objection processes as applicable, and get advice when the amount or period is material.

## What Changes by Province or Territory

[paragraph] HST applies in participating provinces. GST applies federally everywhere else, but provincial sales taxes can operate separately in British Columbia, Saskatchewan, Manitoba and Quebec. Alberta and the territories don't impose a general provincial sales tax at all.

[paragraph] Quebec generally administers both GST and QST for businesses located there — QST registration, invoices, input tax refunds, reporting and place-of-supply rules need their own Quebec-specific review. A federal GST/HST account doesn't replace every QST duty.

[paragraph] Provincial sales taxes aren't copies of GST/HST. Registration thresholds, taxable items, exemptions, tax-on-tax treatment, marketplace rules and filing systems all differ. A business selling goods, software, accommodation or services across Canada should map each province or territory where customers actually receive the supply — record the seller's location, customer location, delivery location, type of supply and tax charged for each recurring sales pattern. This small location file is far more useful than a colour-coded map with no supporting rule.

[heading] Key takeaway

[paragraph] GST/HST works best as a decision sequence: track the registration threshold before invoicing, classify the supply, apply the correct place-of-supply rule, collect and record the tax separately, claim only supported ITCs, and file every required return on time. The strongest control isn't memorizing every rate — it's knowing which fact to verify before the sale.

[spotlight] Build a one-page GST/HST control sheet — registration effective date, reporting period, filing and payment deadlines, sales-tax codes, the account used for collected tax, invoice template version, ITC-document checklist, and who's responsible for review. Update it whenever the business adds a new product, province, platform or large contract.

## Quick Check: Test What Stuck

[accordions]
  - Why isn't GST/HST collected ordinary business revenue? — A registrant generally holds collected GST/HST in trust and reports and remits the net tax through a GST/HST return.
  - What two time periods do the general small-supplier threshold tests use? — A single calendar quarter, and four consecutive calendar quarters.
  - What's one important difference between zero-rated and exempt supplies? — A registrant can generally claim eligible ITCs connected to zero-rated supplies, while expenses used to make exempt supplies generally don't support ITCs.
  - Why might a business in one province need to charge another province's rate? — Place-of-supply rules can base the applicable rate on where the supply is considered made or delivered, not simply the seller's location.
  - Why might GST/HST be payable before the customer pays the invoice? — Under ordinary invoice accounting, tax charged can become reportable based on the invoice date, even when the sale remains unpaid.

## Glossary of Terms

[glossary]
  - Exempt supply — A supply on which GST/HST is not charged and for which related expenses generally do not support ITCs.
  - GST/HST registrant — A person registered or required to be registered for a GST/HST account.
  - Input tax credit (ITC) — A credit that can allow a registrant to recover GST/HST paid or payable on eligible business purchases used in commercial activities.
  - Place of supply — The legal location used to determine whether and at what rate GST/HST applies.
  - Quick Method — An elective accounting method that uses prescribed remittance rates for eligible businesses and changes how many ITCs are claimed.
  - Reporting period — The monthly, quarterly or annual period covered by a GST/HST return.
  - Small supplier — A person whose taxable supplies remain within the applicable revenue tests and who is not required to register under a special rule.
  - Taxable supply — A supply made in commercial activity that is subject to GST/HST, including a zero-rated supply.
  - Zero-rated supply — A taxable supply taxed at zero percent that can generally support related ITCs for a registrant.
