## Start here

[paragraph] This course provides general education, not personal medical care. Complete CSEP’s Get Active Questionnaire before a major increase in activity. Follow its advice if you answer yes to a screening question. Speak with a qualified health professional about relevant medical conditions, recent surgery, pregnancy or postpartum recovery, injury, unexplained symptoms, or medicines that affect exercise. Stop and seek urgent medical help for chest pressure or pain, fainting, severe or unusual shortness of breath, or sudden weakness or numbness.

## Claim a Safe Movement Zone

[paragraph] Start with enough room to stand, sit, reach, and take a step in each direction. You do not need a dedicated room. You need a predictable patch of floor.

[paragraph] Remove loose rugs, cords, toys, bags, and anything breakable. Check the ceiling before raising your arms. Make sure chairs do not roll or fold. Keep pets and young children outside the movement zone when weights, bands, or quick steps are involved.

[paragraph] Test the surface. Socks may slide on smooth floors. Outdoor footwear may track water or snow inside. If you need balance support, use a wall, railing, or heavy counter rather than a chair that can travel without notice.

[spotlight] Check floor, ceiling, supports, traffic, and equipment before every session. It takes less time than explaining a lamp-related incident.

[myths]
  - “A home workout needs a home gym.” — A clear floor area, a stable chair, and suitable movements are enough to begin. Equipment expands options; it does not issue permission.

[heading] Mark your zone

[paragraph] Choose one primary location and one backup. Your backup could be a hallway, bedroom corner, building common room, porch, or covered outdoor area when conditions are safe.

## Build the Movement Menu

[paragraph] A balanced home routine can use six jobs. The exact exercise may change, but the job stays recognizable.

[ordered]
  - SIT OR SQUAT — Rise from a stable chair, squat toward a chair, or use hand support. This supports getting up, climbing, and lifting.
  - HINGE — Send the hips backward toward a wall, then stand. This helps you practise using the hips and legs when picking things up.
  - PUSH — Press away from a wall or counter. A higher surface is easier; a lower stable surface is harder.
  - PULL — Use a resistance band attached to a proper anchor. Without a band, pull outward on a towel for a firm hold. Pulling options are the hardest to improvise safely, so do not hang from doors or furniture.
  - STEP OR MARCH — March in place, move side to side, or step onto a low secure platform while holding support.
  - CARRY OR BRACE — Carry two light balanced items, or hold one object near your centre while keeping your trunk steady and breathing.

[spotlight] If a movement does not suit your body or room today, replace it with another exercise that does the same job. The program survives the substitution.

[heading] Add heart, balance, and mobility

[paragraph] Walk, dance, wheel, march, cycle, or follow low-impact movement for cardio. Practise balance beside a stable support. Use comfortable joint movements during the warm-up and brief mobility work after the main session.

## Household Item or Hidden Hazard

[paragraph] Household objects can provide light resistance, but they were not designed as fitness equipment. Choose objects with secure handles, balanced contents, and no sharp edges. A partly filled water bottle is more predictable than an open detergent jug.

[paragraph] Inspect resistance bands for cracks, thin areas, or damage. Use an anchor made for the purpose and follow its instructions. Keep the band’s line of pull away from your face. Never attach it to a loose handle, glass fixture, folding furniture, or anything you merely hope is structural.

[myths]
  - “If the object feels heavy, it works as a weight.” — A useful load must also be secure, balanced, easy to grip, and safe to put down. The family-size pickle jar has other responsibilities.

## The Twelve-Minute Foundation

[paragraph] Warm up for at least five minutes with easy walking or marching and comfortable joint movement, as recommended in current federal safety guidance. Begin gently and let breathing rise gradually.

[paragraph] Perform one controlled set of the following. Use a number of repetitions that keeps your breathing, comfort, and form steady. Rest as needed.

[ordered]
  - CHAIR RISE — Stand from a stable chair and lower slowly.
  - WALL PUSH — Bend the elbows and bring your body toward the wall, then press away.
  - WALL-TAP HINGE — Send the hips backward to touch a wall, then stand.
  - TOWEL PULL — Pull outward on a towel for a short hold while breathing.
  - SUPPORTED MARCH — Hold a stable support and alternate legs.
  - BALANCED CARRY — Walk a short clear route with two light objects, or hold and brace in place.

[paragraph] Move slowly enough to stay in charge. Stop a set when control changes. The first session is a practice, not an audition.

[spotlight] Count only repetitions performed with the chosen range, steady control, and normal breathing. A smaller honest number is useful information.

[heading] Make any movement easier

[chips]
  - Reduce the range or pace.
  - Use more support.
  - Choose a wall instead of a counter.
  - Use no load or a lighter object.
  - Work for less time and rest longer.

[heading] Make it harder later

[paragraph] Add a few repetitions, a little resistance, a longer range, or a harder variation. Change one item at a time after the current version feels steady on more than one occasion.

## Cardio Without Leaving the Address

[paragraph] Marching, dancing, stair walking, shadow boxing without impact, seated cardio, and indoor walking can raise breathing. At moderate effort, you can talk in sentences but singing is difficult. Keep enough awareness to avoid furniture, people, and pets.

[paragraph] Noise matters in apartments and shared homes. Choose soft landings, controlled steps, and lower-impact options. A quiet workout can still be demanding. Your downstairs neighbour does not need a live broadcast of every squat.

[paragraph] Hydrate, wear suitable clothing and footwear, and adjust for heat. Open windows only when outdoor air quality and temperature are suitable. In wildfire smoke or extreme weather, follow local public-health advice.

## Samira’s One-Square-Metre Plan

[story] Meet Samira
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ Samira, 31, rented a small apartment in Winnipeg with her cousin. She saved home workouts online, but most required a long room, jumping, and equipment she did not own. Her exercise mat spent three months functioning as an expensive wall decoration.
  ¶ She measured a safe square beside the sofa and moved one side table before each session. Her routine used chair rises, wall pushes, hinges, towel pulls, supported marches, and two water bottles for carries. On quiet evenings, she marched. When her cousin was sleeping after a night shift, she used slow seated steps instead.
  ¶ Samira created three versions. The small session was the warm-up plus one circuit. The standard session used two circuits and ten minutes of cardio. The extended session added a walk outside when weather and sidewalks were safe.
  ¶ She placed a short checklist inside a kitchen cupboard. Four weeks later, the movements felt smoother and the standard session no longer required negotiation with herself. She added a resistance band with a proper anchor. The mat was promoted from décor to employee.

[heading] What Samira did well

[paragraph] She designed around real space, noise, weather, and another person’s schedule. She kept one movement menu while changing session size. Her environment became easier to use because the setup was simple.

[myths]
  - “Home workouts are automatically easier than gym workouts.” — Challenge comes from movement choice, resistance, range, pace, and duration. Location changes convenience, not physiology.

## Three Session Sizes

[paragraph] Use the same movement menu so every session feels familiar.

[cards]
  - SMALL SESSION — Warm up, then perform one set of four to six movements. This is useful on a crowded day.
  - STANDARD SESSION — Warm up, complete one or two circuits, then add a short moderate cardio period.
  - EXTENDED SESSION — Add time, another controlled circuit, balance practice, or an outdoor activity. Extra work is optional and should still allow reasonable recovery.

[spotlight] If moving furniture takes longer than the workout, simplify the location or routine. Friction is a design problem, not a character defect.

## Progress the Routine, Not the Clutter

[paragraph] Repeat a plan long enough to observe it. When form is controlled and recovery is reasonable across more than one session, change one variable. Add a few repetitions, a little resistance, a longer cardio period, or one weekly session.

[myths]
  - “A new routine every day prevents plateaus.” — Frequent novelty can make progress hard to judge. Repeating useful movements builds skill. Variety can enter gradually without turning your week into a subscription catalogue.

[heading] Equipment buying filter

[paragraph] Before purchasing anything, ask:

[chips]
  - Which movement job will this improve?
  - Do I have safe space to use and store it?
  - Can I adjust it as I progress?
  - Are instructions, anchors, and weight ratings clear?
  - Will I realistically use it twice a week?

[paragraph] Adjustable resistance bands or weights may offer several levels in a small space, but no item suits everyone. Check construction, condition, warranty, and recall information. Used equipment needs inspection before use.

[spotlight] Repeat your no-equipment or current-equipment plan for two weeks. Then buy only what solves a specific limitation. Shopping is not progressive overload.

## Access Across Canada

[paragraph] Home exercise may be especially useful where travel, cost, disability access, weather, or distance limits facility use. Public libraries, municipal recreation departments, community health organizations, friendship centres, and settlement agencies may offer free videos, equipment loans, or virtual classes. Availability differs by community.

[paragraph] For virtual instruction, check the instructor’s credential, privacy practices, platform requirements, and ability to offer alternatives. Provincial and territorial rules differ for health professions. A qualified exercise professional can help with programming; a physiotherapist can assess pain, injury, and movement limitations within their scope.

[heading] Canadian weather check

[paragraph] Outdoor add-ons need local judgment. Check temperature, humidity, air quality, smoke, ice, daylight, and alerts. Tell someone your route when appropriate. Indoor exercise is a useful backup, not a consolation prize.

[heading] Key takeaway

[paragraph] A home workout succeeds when the space is safe, the movements are balanced, and the setup is easy to repeat. Start with what you have. Scale each movement, keep three session sizes, and progress one variable after the current plan becomes steady.

[spotlight] Store the plan where the routine begins, not inside an app you avoid opening. A paper checklist beside the band or a note near the chair removes one decision. Home is full of reminders already. Let one of them be helpful.

## Quick Check: Test What Stuck

[accordions]
  - What five things belong in the room scan? — Floor, ceiling, supports, traffic, and equipment.
  - Name the six movement jobs in the foundation. — Sit or squat, hinge, push, pull, step or march, and carry or brace.
  - What makes a household object suitable as resistance? — It should be secure, balanced, easy to grip, free of sharp edges, and safe to place down.
  - True or false: Jumping is required for effective home cardio. — False. Marching, dancing, cycling, wheeling, seated movement, and other low-impact choices can raise breathing.
  - When should you stop a set? — Stop when breathing, comfort, range, or control changes noticeably, or when concerning symptoms appear.
  - Why use three session sizes? — They allow the routine to fit different time, energy, space, and noise conditions.
  - Which variable should you change first when progressing? — Change one appropriate variable after the current plan feels controlled and recovery is reasonable.
  - What question should an equipment purchase answer? — It should solve a specific movement, resistance, access, or progression limitation.

## Glossary of Terms

[glossary]
  - Anchor — A secure attachment point designed to hold resistance-band force.
  - Circuit — A sequence of exercises performed one after another, with rest as needed.
  - Intensity — How hard activity feels or how much it changes breathing and heart rate.
  - Progression — A planned, gradual increase in challenge.
  - Range of motion — The distance and direction through which a joint moves comfortably.
  - Resistance — A force that muscles work against.
  - Scaling — Changing an exercise so its challenge matches current ability or conditions.
