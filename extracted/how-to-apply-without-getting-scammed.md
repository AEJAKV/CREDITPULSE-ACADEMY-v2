## Start here

[paragraph] Nine modules of real programs, real deadlines and real dollar amounts is exactly the raw material a scammer needs. They do not have to invent a convincing lie about veterans’ benefits or home renovation grants or GST rebates. The real programs already come with real names, real numbers and real urgency built in.

[paragraph] All a scam has to do is borrow the name and change who is asking for your information.

[paragraph] So this final module is not really about fraud in general. It is about the specific moment where everything else in this course becomes usable against you, and the handful of habits that close that door regardless of which program’s name gets borrowed next.

## The one fact that ends most of these before they start

[paragraph] No government department in Canada, not the CRA, not Veterans Affairs, not Natural Resources Canada, not Employment and Social Development Canada, will call, text or email you demanding immediate payment, threatening arrest, or asking for banking details, a credit card number or your full Social Insurance Number over the phone. This is not a guideline with exceptions. It is stated flatly, in writing, by every one of these departments.

[paragraph] Legitimate government contact looks unglamorous by comparison. A letter by mail. A notice in a secure online portal you logged into yourself. A callback you initiated to a number you looked up independently. None of it arrives with the emotional temperature of a threat, and none of it needs an answer in the next twenty minutes.

[paragraph] Learn that one fact and you have already closed the door on most of what follows in this module. Everything else here is what to do with the fact once you notice the door being tried.

[myths]
  - “If they knew my name, my address and mentioned a real program, it must be legitimate.” — Scammers increasingly do their homework, and spoofed caller ID can display a real government phone number. Knowing personal details or a real program name is evidence someone did research, not evidence of who they work for. Verify through a channel you control, a number you looked up yourself, regardless of how much the caller already seems to know.

## The shape of it, whichever program’s name gets used

[paragraph] Different scripts, same structure. Recognizing the structure matters more than memorizing any single version, because the version changes every tax season and the structure does not.

[ordered]
  - Urgency with a countdown — Pay in the next thirty minutes or face arrest, account suspension, or losing the benefit. Real programs do not work on a clock that starts when you pick up the phone.
  - An unusual payment method — Gift cards, cryptocurrency, e-transfer to a personal account, or a courier picking up cash. No Canadian government department accepts any of these for a debt, a fee, or to release a benefit.
  - A link instead of a login — An email or text urging you to click through to claim your refund or verify your account, rather than telling you to log into an account you already have, the way you normally would.
  - A fee to access something free — Being asked to pay to be told about a grant, or asked for a percentage of an award before you have applied for anything. Every genuine program covered in this course is free to learn about and free to apply for.
  - Contact you did not initiate, about something you did not start — A call about a grant you never applied for, a rebate you never requested, a benefit review you never triggered.

## Verify through your own door

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: VERIFY THROUGH A DOOR YOU OPENED YOURSELF

[spotlight] Do not call the number the caller gave you. Do not click the link in the text or email. Instead, independently look up the department’s number, from a bill you already have or a search you run, and contact them that way. If the approach was real, this costs a few minutes and confirms it. If it was fake, you have closed the door without giving up anything. It works whether the borrowed name is the CRA, Veterans Affairs, or a small-business grant. It is worth being the one rule you actually memorize word for word.

## What a real application actually needs from you

[paragraph] Across the nine modules in this course, legitimate programs asked for identity documents, income figures, medical certification, and banking details for direct deposit, always through a form you accessed yourself, on a site you navigated to directly, never dictated over an unsolicited phone call.

[paragraph] Your Social Insurance Number deserves particular care. It is required by some legitimate applications, and it is also one of the most valuable pieces of information to a fraudster building a false identity. Give it only when you initiated the contact, on an official government site or form, and never in response to an email, text or unsolicited call, regardless of how official the request sounds. If you are ever unsure whether a document truly needs it, ask the organization directly, through a number you looked up yourself, before providing it.

[paragraph] The fee-taking helper showed up back in Module 1.5 for small-business grants, and it applies just as much to a veteran’s disability claim, a senior’s renovation grant, or a training credit. Anyone charging an up-front fee or a percentage of an award to help you apply for something that is free to apply for on your own is not offering a shortcut. Every legitimate free-help channel named across this course, Service Officers, Community Futures, Small Business Enterprise Centres, employment counsellors, 211, exists precisely so nobody has to pay for this.

[myths]
  - “A grant consultant with a professional website and a logo must be legitimate.” — A website costs very little to build, and a logo can be designed to sit close to an official one without technically copying it. Neither is evidence of a government relationship. The evidence that matters is whether the program exists on the official page, and whether you can apply to it yourself for nothing.

## What to do if you already gave something up

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: WHAT TO DO IF YOU ALREADY GAVE SOMETHING UP

[spotlight] This happens to careful people. The response matters more than the mistake, and speed matters more than shame. If money moved, contact your bank or card issuer immediately: some transactions can still be stopped or reversed in the first hours. If you shared your SIN, banking details or other identifying information, contact the relevant department directly through a number you look up yourself, and ask specifically about fraud alerts on your account. Report the incident to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre regardless of whether money was lost. They track patterns across many reports, and a report that seems small on its own can be the piece that helps them stop the next one. Nobody who reports this is required to justify how they were caught out. These approaches are built by people who do this for a living, refined against thousands of targets, and are designed specifically to work on careful, intelligent people under time pressure. Being targeted is not a judgment on you.

[heading] Before you respond to anything claiming to be from a government program

[chips]
  - I did not initiate this contact, or I am not certain who initiated it.
  - I am being asked to act within an unusually short window.
  - I am being asked to pay by gift card, cryptocurrency, e-transfer to a person, or courier.
  - I am being asked to click a link rather than log into an account I navigate to myself.
  - I am being asked for my SIN, full banking details, or a credit card number before I have verified who is asking.
  - I have not yet looked up the department’s number independently and called it myself.

[paragraph] If more than one of these is true, stop. Hang up, close the message, and verify independently before doing anything else.

## Harold, the courier, and the phone call he made instead

[story] Harold and the call he made instead
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ Harold is sixty-eight and lives in Winnipeg. The call came on a Tuesday afternoon: a man identifying himself as being with the Canada Revenue Agency, stating that Harold owed several thousand dollars in back taxes tied to a benefit overpayment, and that a warrant would be issued within the hour unless it was resolved immediately. The caller ID showed a number that, when Harold glanced at it, looked plausibly official.
  ¶ The man was patient, which in hindsight was itself a kind of pressure. He stayed on the line, walked Harold through exactly what to say to a courier who would arrive to collect payment in cash, and reassured him repeatedly that this was routine and that he was helping Harold avoid a much worse outcome. Harold, who describes himself as not usually anxious about much, felt his hands shaking.
  ¶ What stopped it was a single sentence his daughter had said to him months earlier, after watching a news segment about exactly this scam: if anyone ever calls saying they are the CRA and you owe money, hang up and call the real number yourself. He remembered it mid-call, told the man he needed to call him back, hung up, and dialled the number from a previous year’s Notice of Assessment, not the number the caller had given him.
  ¶ The real CRA had no record of any debt, any warrant, or any call. Harold reported the incident to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre that afternoon. Nothing was lost except twenty minutes and a genuinely unpleasant hour.

[takeaway] No government department demands immediate payment by phone, text or email, or accepts gift cards, cryptocurrency or courier cash. Verify any unexpected contact through a number you look up yourself, never the one the caller gives you. Nothing in this entire course requires paying a fee to a third party. Every legitimate program is free to learn about and free to apply for. And if something does get through, report it and move fast. Speed matters far more than embarrassment.

## Glossary

[glossary]
  - Spoofed caller ID — A technique that displays a false number, including real government numbers, on a recipient’s phone.
  - Phishing — An email or text designed to look official, aimed at getting you to click a link or share information.
  - CAFC — Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre. The national body, run jointly by the RCMP, the Ontario Provincial Police and the Competition Bureau, that collects fraud reports across Canada.
  - Verification through an independent channel — Contacting an organization using a number or address you found yourself, not one supplied by the person contacting you.

[lists]
  - WHERE TO GET HELP — Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre: 1-888-495-8501, or report online at antifraudcentre.ca. Report every incident, even if nothing was lost.; Canada Revenue Agency: 1-800-959-8281 for individuals, to confirm whether a contact claiming to be the CRA is genuine.; Your bank or card issuer: immediately, if money has moved or account details were shared.; Local police, non-emergency line: if you have lost money or believe your identity has been compromised.

[paragraph] If you are in immediate danger or a crime is in progress, call 911.
