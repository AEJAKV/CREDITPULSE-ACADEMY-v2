## Command brief

[paragraph] Identity fraud is the only situation where someone borrows your name and returns it with paperwork.

[paragraph] This masterclass gives you a calm sequence for suspicious credit activity. Classify what happened, protect money and access, preserve evidence, report through verified channels, correct both files and monitor for the next attempt. Speed matters; accurate speed matters more than the first number in a frightening text.

[spotlight] Identity theft is stealing personal information. Identity fraud is using it. Exposure is a warning; an unauthorized account or transaction is evidence of misuse.

[lists]
  - Distinguish data exposure, identity theft, identity fraud, account takeover and payment-card fraud. — Run a first-hour response without destroying useful evidence. — Contact financial institutions, both bureaus, police and the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre in a sensible order. — Build a correction file for unauthorized accounts and transactions. — Leave with a 12-month monitoring calendar and recovery-scam defence.

[myths]
  - “One call to my bank fixes every identity-fraud problem.” — Your bank can protect its accounts, but other creditors, both bureaus, police, CAFC and affected government issuers may each have a separate role.

## Noah’s Name Had a Very Busy Weekend

[story] Meet Noah (DEDICATED STORY)

Noah Cardinal, an electrical apprentice in Saskatoon, was gathering documents for a rental application when one report showed a mobile account and credit card he never opened. The next morning, a collector called about the mobile bill.

Then a “fraud recovery specialist” offered to erase the accounts for a fee and asked him to install remote-access software. It knew part of his address and wanted cryptocurrency, which is rarely how reputable paperwork says hello.

Noah stopped replying and began an incident log. Using official numbers, he contacted his bank and unfamiliar providers. They secured access, opened investigations and issued case numbers. On a trusted device he secured email first, changed reused passwords, enabled multi-factor authentication and saved evidence before deleting anything.

He checked both credit files, requested fraud alerts and disputed unauthorized accounts. He reported to police and CAFC. Because his SIN might be exposed, he followed Service Canada guidance and checked CRA through its official site.

Corrections took weeks. One account disappeared; the other needed a second package. Noah kept every reply, rechecked both files and rejected more “recovery” offers. His rental timeline shifted, but rushing did not cost him more money or access.

[spotlight] Noah’s incident log connected every organization, date, case number and next action.

[heading] The conclusion

[paragraph] Recovery was not one heroic call. It was contain, document, report, correct and verify, always through official channels.

[transition] Exposure and confirmed fraud are not the same warning, and mixing them up wastes the response window.

## Round 1: Classify the Incident Before You Chase It

[spotlight] A breach notice does not prove a new loan exists. An unknown tradeline, collection call or credit notice deserves immediate investigation.

[ordered]
  - Unfamiliar accounts, inquiries, addresses, employers or balances on either credit report.
  - Missing bills, redirected mail, unexpected one-time passcodes or password-reset notices.
  - Transactions, benefits, tax changes or service accounts you did not authorize.
  - Collection calls or credit decisions for an account or application you did not make.

[myths]
  - “No money left my bank account, so there is no identity-fraud problem.” — Fraud may first appear as new credit, telecom service, redirected benefits or account takeover. Check the type of misuse, not only chequing.

[source] FCAC: Checking reports for errors and fraud
  url: https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/credit-reports-score/check-errors.html

## Round 2: The First-Hour Response Kit

[paragraph] Use a trusted device and official contact information. A suspicious link or number is evidence, not directions.

[cards]
  - PROTECT MONEY — Call affected banks, card issuers or providers. Report unauthorized activity, block cards, secure access and open investigation files. — 01
  - PROTECT ACCESS — Secure primary email and change reused passwords. Enable multi-factor authentication and review recovery addresses, devices and forwarding rules. — 02
  - PRESERVE EVIDENCE — Save messages, screenshots, statements, envelopes, transaction details and call notes before accounts or devices change. — 03
  - CHECK BOTH CREDIT FILES — Obtain Equifax and TransUnion reports. Ask both bureaus for a fraud or identity alert and dispute unfamiliar information. — 04
  - CREATE OFFICIAL REPORTS — Contact local police and keep the file number. Report to CAFC online or at 1-888-495-8501. — 05
  - PROTECT IDENTITY RECORDS — If a SIN, CRA account, passport, driver’s licence or health card is involved, follow the issuing authority’s official process. — 06

[spotlight] A fraud alert asks lenders for extra verification. It is useful, not a guarantee every application will stop.

[myths]
  - “I should delete scam messages immediately.” — First preserve sender details, dates, payment instructions and screenshots. Then follow provider or police guidance on safe removal.

[spotlight] Secure email early. A compromised inbox can reset passwords across banking, telecom and government accounts.

[source] CAFC: What to do if you are a victim
  url: https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca/scams-fraudes/victim-victime-eng.htm

## Round 3: Turn the Incident Into a Case File

[paragraph] Correction can take more than one cycle. Keep one secure folder with a master log so each organization receives relevant evidence, not every private document you own.

[cards]
  - Incident summary — One paragraph describing when activity was found, affected accounts and what was not authorized. — 01
  - Evidence index — Numbered statements, screenshots, notices and identification copies. Send copies and redact what is not required. — 02
  - Case-number register — Organization, channel, date, representative, case or police number, promised action and follow-up. — 03
  - Credit correction — Dispute each unfamiliar account, inquiry, address or balance with the creditor and both bureaus where it appears. — 04
  - Monitoring calendar — Recheck statements and both files at 30, 60 and 90 days, then 6 and 12 months, or sooner if signals appear. — 05

[spotlight] The two bureaus may hold different information. Correcting one file does not prove the other is accurate.

[myths]
  - “A recovery company can guarantee every fraud item will vanish for a fee.” — Legitimate investigations rely on evidence and official correction processes. No one can guarantee an outcome, and victims are often targeted by recovery scams.

## Quick Check: Choose the Best Next Move

[accordions]
  - You receive a breach notice but see no unauthorized activity. Exposure or confirmed fraud? — Exposure. Treat it as a warning and monitor; confirmed misuse requires evidence such as an unauthorized account or transaction.
  - A text says to call immediately about a frozen bank account. Which route? — Use the number on an official statement, card or the institution’s independently located website, not the text.
  - Why secure email early? — A compromised inbox can reset passwords across banking, telecom and government accounts.
  - An unknown account appears on only one report. Who may need the request? — The creditor and the bureau showing it; still review the other bureau because files differ.
  - A recovery expert requests remote access and cryptocurrency. Safest response? — Stop contact, do not pay or install software, preserve evidence and verify/report through official channels.

[spotlight] Keep watching after the first correction. Stolen information can be reused months later.

## Glossary of Terms

[glossary]
  - Identity theft — The unauthorized obtaining of personal information.
  - Identity fraud — The use of stolen personal information to impersonate someone or obtain money, credit, services or benefits.
  - Account takeover — Unauthorized control of an existing account, often through stolen credentials or password resets.
  - Fraud alert — A notice on a credit file asking prospective lenders to take extra steps to verify identity.
  - Multi-factor authentication — A sign-in method requiring more than one type of proof, such as a password and an app code.
  - Credit inquiry — A record that someone accessed a credit file. An unfamiliar application inquiry can be a fraud signal.
  - Recovery scam — A second fraud that promises to recover losses or erase records in exchange for money, access or personal information.
