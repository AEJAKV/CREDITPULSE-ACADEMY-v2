## Start here

[paragraph] Good records tell the story of a transaction without asking the owner to remember what happened eleven months ago. They connect money received or spent to an invoice, receipt, contract, log, bookkeeping entry and tax return. When those links agree, tax preparation becomes a review instead of a reconstruction.

[paragraph] This is the final Advanced Tax and Wealth course, and it brings the earlier lessons together through evidence. Retirement decisions need source documents. Estate files need current records. Self-employment and GST/HST calculations need transactions that can be traced. The record system is where every confident number earns the right to be confident.

[paragraph] This is general Canadian education, not personal tax, legal, accounting, payroll, privacy or records-management advice. Retention periods and record requirements vary by tax, entity, transaction, dispute and province or territory — confirm anything time-sensitive with the CRA, Revenu Québec where it applies, and qualified professionals. You'll follow one transaction through five links, aiming for a system another competent person could understand without opening a folder called "miscellaneous important things."

[chips]
  - Explain what makes a record useful to the CRA.
  - Build a five-link evidence chain for income and expenses.
  - Identify records needed for income tax, GST/HST, payroll, assets and vehicles.
  - Complete a simple monthly reconciliation.
  - Protect electronic records through readable formats, backups and access controls.
  - Apply the general six-year retention rule without treating it as universal.
  - Create a one-page records-control map.

[myths]
  - “A bank statement is enough proof.” — A statement can show that money moved. It may not show what was purchased, who supplied it, the business purpose, or the GST/HST information needed for an input tax credit. The statement is one witness, not the entire cast.

## Build the Five-Link Evidence Chain

[paragraph] A record is more than a receipt — it can be an invoice, agreement, statement, image, log, return, voucher, electronic file, or anything else that supports what happened. The useful question isn't "do I have paperwork," it's "can I trace this amount from the real transaction to the filed return?"

[ordered]
  - TRANSACTION — A sale, purchase, payment, refund, payroll event, asset purchase or transfer occurs.
  - SOURCE DOCUMENT — An invoice, receipt, contract, platform report, mileage log, deposit record or other evidence captures the event.
  - BOOKKEEPING ENTRY — The transaction gets recorded with the correct date, amount, account, tax treatment and business purpose.
  - RECONCILIATION — The entry is compared against bank, credit-card, platform, payroll or government-account records.
  - RETURN OR REPORT — The supported totals flow into an income tax return, GST/HST return, payroll remittance, information slip or financial statement.

[paragraph] If one link is missing, mark it for follow-up. Don't invent it — a note explaining what happened and what evidence remains is more useful than a beautifully categorized guess.

[spotlight] Give every transaction a stable reference number. Put the same number on the source document and the bookkeeping entry. Search then becomes a direct route instead of a scavenger hunt designed by your past self.

[heading] Records have to explain the amount

[paragraph] Income records should identify the date, amount and source. Expense evidence should normally identify the date, seller or supplier, buyer, description, amount and other required information. GST/HST claims can require the supplier's business number and purchaser details, depending on the amount and circumstances. The exact record depends on the transaction — a signed contract may explain a deposit, a vehicle log explains kilometres, an email may confirm a price change. Keep the evidence that makes the number understandable.

## Capture Records Where the Transaction Happens

[paragraph] Waiting until year-end makes small gaps expensive. Capture the record when the sale, purchase or payment occurs.

[paragraph] For income, keep sequential sales invoices, cash-register totals, e-commerce and marketplace reports, payment-processor statements, deposit records, contracts and credit notes. Record gross income before platform fees or other deductions — cash, property, services, foreign currency and crypto-assets can all need their own records and valuation.

[paragraph] For expenses, get a receipt or voucher and add the business purpose when it isn't obvious. A restaurant receipt can show food and tax while saying nothing about who attended or why the meeting related to business — add that context while the meeting is still a memory rather than a historical theory.

[paragraph] If a supplier doesn't provide a receipt, the CRA directs businesses to record the supplier's name and address, amount, date and transaction details in an expense journal, plus whatever other support exists. That doesn't guarantee deductibility — it preserves the facts for review.

[spotlight] Use a mobile capture routine with three steps: photograph, label, file. Include the date, supplier, amount and purpose in the filename or record. "IMG_8472" is a camera's opinion, not an accounting description.

[heading] Keep business and personal activity distinguishable

[paragraph] A separate business bank account and business credit card can reduce mixed transactions. When a cost has both business and personal use, keep the full source document plus the calculation supporting the claimed business portion — don't edit a receipt image to show only the claimed amount. Preserve the original and attach the calculation, so the audit trail shows the whole event and the method used to reach the reported part. Refunds, rebates, chargebacks, store credits and cancelled transactions need records too — keeping the original purchase while losing the refund creates an expense that no longer matches reality. Pair both sides.

## Close Each Month Before Opening the Next

[paragraph] Monthly closing turns a pile of documents into reliable records. Pick a fixed date and work through the accounts in the same order every time.

[paragraph] Start by reconciling every business bank and credit-card account — match the opening balance, transactions, transfers, fees and closing balance. Then reconcile payment processors and sales platforms, so gross sales, platform fees, refunds and net deposits all connect.

[paragraph] Review accounts receivable and accounts payable. Confirm which invoices remain unpaid and which bills are outstanding, and check that customer deposits, owner contributions, owner's withdrawals, loan proceeds and transfers aren't mistaken for sales or expenses.

[paragraph] If you're registered for GST/HST, compare taxable sales and tax charged with the GST/HST payable records, and review the purchase documents supporting your ITCs. If there are employees, reconcile gross payroll, deductions, employer contributions, net pay, remittances and payroll records.

[myths]
  - “Filing the return proves the records are complete.” — Electronic filing confirms that numbers were submitted. It doesn't certify that every sale was captured, every expense was supported, or every account was reconciled. A confirmation number is useful — it is not a bookkeeping diploma.

[spotlight] Keep a monthly close note — the accounts reconciled, missing items, corrections made, unusual transactions, and who reviewed the period. A two-minute note can save an hour of future explanation.

[heading] Preserve corrections

[paragraph] Correct errors through an audit trail. Don't delete a finalized invoice or overwrite a reconciled entry without a record of the change — use credit notes, adjusting entries and dated explanations according to the accounting system and professional advice. A correction should show the original amount, corrected amount, reason, date and person making the change. The records don't need to pretend nobody made a mistake — they need to show how the mistake was fixed.

## Noelle Rescues the Missing Quarter

[story] Meet Noelle
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ Noelle owns a small event-floral business in New Brunswick. Her sales run through invoices, e-transfers, card payments and a marketplace. She keeps paper receipts in envelopes and assumes the bank feed has captured everything else.
  ¶ During a GST/HST review, Noelle notices the spring marketplace deposits are lower than the sales reports. She first suspects missing revenue — then she follows the evidence chain instead of guessing.
  ¶ The marketplace report shows gross sales, refunds, commissions and HST. The bank shows only the net deposits. Her bookkeeping had recorded those net deposits as sales, so revenue and fees were both understated. Two refunds were also missing, and several purchase receipts didn't have enough information to support the ITCs claimed.
  ¶ Noelle doesn't patch the quarter with one mystery adjustment. She exports the original marketplace data, matches each payout to the bank, records gross sales and fees separately, attaches refund reports, and requests corrected supplier invoices where possible. Her bookkeeper reviews the entries and documents the correction — then Noelle builds a monthly close checklist so marketplace totals must reconcile before a period closes.

[heading] What actually got fixed?

[paragraph] The repaired quarter isn't flawless — it's traceable. Noelle can now explain the difference between a sale and a deposit without performing live mathematics in front of a government letter.

## Maintain Separate Tax and Business Files

[paragraph] One document can support more than one obligation, but the filing system should show where it's actually used. Build sections for each of these record groups.

[cards]
  - INCOME TAX & FINANCIAL RECORDS — Sales and income evidence, expense documents, journals, ledgers, bank and credit-card statements, loan agreements, contracts, year-end adjustments, financial statements, tax-return working papers, filed returns, notices of assessment, instalment records and correspondence. Property records need special care — purchase and sale documents, legal costs, improvements, CCA details and anything affecting adjusted cost base may need to be kept well past the ordinary period.
  - GST/HST RECORDS — Sales and purchase invoices, tax charged, place-of-supply support, exemption or zero-rating evidence, credit and debit notes, ITC calculations, elections, reporting-period working papers, filed returns, payment confirmations and CRA correspondence. Keep the working copy behind an electronically filed return too.
  - PAYROLL RECORDS — Employee identity, hours, remuneration, taxable benefits, pensionable and insurable amounts, CPP or QPP, EI, income tax withheld, employer contributions, remittances, TD1 forms, information slips, returns and letters of authority. This information is sensitive — a shared folder called 'everyone can edit' is not a control, it's an invitation written in cheerful software.
  - VEHICLES & MIXED-USE ASSETS — Trip dates, destinations, purposes, business kilometres, total kilometres, operating costs and odometer readings, plus purchase, lease, financing and sale documents. For home offices, phones, internet and other mixed-use items, keep the source document and the method used to calculate business use, applied consistently and documented when it changes.

[myths]
  - “My accountant or payroll company is responsible for keeping everything.” — A third party can maintain records, but the taxpayer or employer remains responsible for adequate, accessible records. Know what the provider holds, how to export it, and what happens when the service ends.

## Keep Electronic Records Readable, Restorable and Secure

[paragraph] Electronic filing doesn't remove the record-retention duty. If records are created or maintained electronically, the CRA can require them in an electronically readable and usable format even when paper copies exist.

[paragraph] Export data from bookkeeping, payroll, e-commerce, payment, scheduling, mileage and crypto-asset platforms regularly. Keep reports that preserve transaction-level detail, not only screenshots of dashboard totals — a screenshot can show what a screen looked like, but it may not give you the data needed to sort, trace or recalculate the entries.

[spotlight] Test the restore process. A backup is only a theory until a file can be recovered, opened and read. Pick one sample invoice, one bookkeeping export and one return working paper, then restore all three.

[paragraph] Use at least two protected backup locations, with one separated from the main device and business location. Encrypt sensitive records, use unique passwords and multi-factor authentication, limit permissions by role, and remove access promptly when a worker or provider leaves.

[paragraph] Keep a system map naming each platform, what records it holds, how often data is exported, backup location, file format, account owner and recovery method. Store access instructions securely, without placing passwords in an ordinary course worksheet.

[heading] Confirm where records are kept

[paragraph] The CRA generally requires records to be kept at the business or residence in Canada unless written permission allows another location. Records stored outside Canada and merely accessed online from here aren't considered kept in Canada under CRA guidance. Ask any cloud provider where data is actually stored, how it can be exported, and how long it stays available after cancellation — get professional advice when the system relies on storage outside Canada.

## Retain the Right Records for the Right Period

[paragraph] The general CRA rule is to keep required records and supporting documents for six years from the end of the last tax year they relate to. That's the starting point, not an automatic destruction date.

[paragraph] Different timing can apply. Late-filed returns can shift the six-year period. Records connected to an objection or appeal must be kept until the dispute and further appeal periods are resolved. The CRA can require longer retention, and historical ownership, property and corporate records may need to be kept indefinitely or under separate rules.

[paragraph] If a business closes, record duties can continue — corporate dissolution, non-incorporated businesses, trusts, estates, charities and other organizations all follow specific requirements, and provincial employment, sales-tax, privacy, limitation-period, professional, insurance and corporate laws may require longer retention still.

[myths]
  - “Everything can be destroyed exactly six years after the purchase date.” — The ordinary rule is measured from the end of the last tax year the record relates to, and several exceptions can extend it. Shredders are efficient employees. They still need authorization.

[spotlight] Add a "destroy after review" date, not an automatic deletion date. Before destruction, check for late filing, reassessment, objection, property history, payroll, GST/HST, provincial rules, litigation, insurance and written CRA directions.

[paragraph] Written CRA permission is required to destroy records before the applicable retention period ends — Form T137 can be used to request early destruction for relevant records. Permission from the CRA doesn't cancel duties imposed by another law or regulator.

[heading] Provincial and territorial variation

[paragraph] Federal CRA rules are only one layer. Quebec businesses may need records supporting Revenu Québec income tax, QST and source-deduction obligations. British Columbia, Saskatchewan and Manitoba businesses may need separate provincial sales-tax records, and payroll, workers' compensation, employment standards, corporate registries, professional regulators and privacy laws all vary across Canada. Build a jurisdiction list showing the province or territory of the business, employees, property, customers, registrations and professional licences — an online business can acquire provincial duties without acquiring a provincial office.

[heading] Key takeaway

[paragraph] Strong CRA records form an evidence chain: capture the transaction, preserve the source, record it accurately, reconcile the account, and connect the supported total to the return. Keep electronic records readable and restorable, retain them for the correct period, and preserve corrections instead of hiding them. The best record system isn't the one with the most folders — it's the one that answers the next reasonable question.

[spotlight] Create a yearly "proof package" after every return is filed — the final return, confirmation, working papers, reconciled trial balance or income-and-expense summary, major elections, tax payments, assessment, list of source folders, software exports, and unresolved follow-up items. Keep the detail in its normal folders; the proof package is just the map.

## Quick Check: Test What Stuck

[accordions]
  - What are the five links in the evidence chain? — The transaction, the source document, the bookkeeping entry, the reconciliation, and the filed return or report.
  - Why can a bank statement be insufficient support for an expense? — It may show that money moved without identifying the item, supplier, business purpose, or required GST/HST information.
  - What should a monthly close note record? — The accounts reconciled, missing items, corrections, unusual transactions, and who reviewed the period.
  - Why should data be exported from cloud accounting or platform services? — The business stays responsible for accessible records, and service access, formats or providers can change — exports preserve transaction-level evidence.
  - Why isn't the general six-year retention rule an automatic destruction date? — The period is measured from the relevant tax year, and late filing, disputes, property history, CRA directions, other laws and special entity rules can all extend it.

## Glossary of Terms

[glossary]
  - Audit trail — A traceable history connecting an original transaction, later entries, corrections and reported totals.
  - Backup — A separate copy of electronic information that can be restored after loss, damage or system failure.
  - Books and records — Accounting information and supporting documents needed to determine and verify tax obligations and entitlements.
  - Reconciliation — A comparison of two record sources, such as a ledger and a bank statement, to identify and explain differences.
  - Retention period — The length of time a record must remain available under the applicable rule.
  - Source document — Original evidence of a transaction, such as an invoice, receipt, contract, statement or log.
  - T137 — The CRA form used to request permission to destroy certain records before the required retention period ends.
  - Usable electronic format — A format that can be accessed, read and processed for review rather than merely viewed as an inaccessible backup or obsolete file.
