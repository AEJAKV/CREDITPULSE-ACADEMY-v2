## Start here

[paragraph] An overdraft is borrowed money used when a transaction goes over the available funds in a chequing account.

[paragraph] If the institution covers the payment, the account may become negative and the learner may owe the amount, interest and a service fee. It can prevent a short-term payment failure, but it does not create income.

[paragraph] In this Overdraft Incident Room, you will investigate four incidents and separate the immediate emergency from the repeating pattern. Then you will calculate the real 90-day cost and install controls before the next pre-authorized debit arrives. The incident is evidence, not a character reference.

[source] FCAC: Getting overdraft protection
  url: https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/banking/overdraft-protection.html

[spotlight] Overdraft is a seatbelt, not a transportation plan. It can soften one timing collision; repeated use means the cash-flow route needs repair.

[heading] The Incident-Room Rules

[cards]
  - NO SHAME — A negative balance is a financial event to diagnose, not proof that someone is irresponsible.
  - NO GUESSING — Record the posted transaction, negative amount, fee, interest, payment outcome and repayment date.
  - NO DOUBLE COUNTING — Separate the borrowed amount from the cost of borrowing and from any biller’s fee.
  - NO DELAY — Protect essentials, contact the institution or biller when needed, and repay the negative balance as soon as you can.

[myths]
  - “My overdraft limit is extra money available for spending.” — Overdraft is credit. The negative balance must be repaid, and fees or interest may apply. Treat the limit as emergency capacity, not as part of the household budget.

[transition] Every negative balance has a cause, and finding it stops the fees from repeating.

[heading] Overdraft, Decline and NSF Are Not Interchangeable

[cards]
  - COVERED BY OVERDRAFT — The institution pays the transaction within the approved limit. The account goes negative; interest and a monthly or pay-per-use fee may apply.
  - DECLINED — A debit-card purchase may not proceed. The learner still needs another way to pay, but an NSF fee is not automatically created by every declined purchase.
  - RETURNED / NSF — A cheque or pre-authorized debit may be returned when funds or protection are not enough. A bank fee and a separate biller consequence may apply, each under its own rules.

[myths]
  - “Overdraft and NSF mean the same thing because both start with not enough money.” — Overdraft generally means the institution covered the transaction and created a debt. An NSF event generally means the payment was not covered or exceeded protection. The fees, payment outcome and repair steps can differ.

[spotlight] The first question is not “What was the fee?” It is “Did the payment succeed?” A covered rent payment and a returned rent payment create different next actions.

## The Five-Step Incident Response

[ordered]
  - STOP — Stop the next avoidable charge. Pause non-essential purchases and review pending transactions, holds and scheduled debits. Do not create a second incident while investigating the first.
  - STABILIZE — Protect the payments that keep the household running. Confirm which housing, utilities, food, transportation, insurance and minimum debt payments must be protected before the next deposit.
  - TRACE — Name the specific trigger supported by the evidence. Match every incident to a cause: forgotten recurring charge, timing gap, variable spending, deposit hold, income shortfall, account error or fraud.
  - REPAIR — Restore the account and document every correction. Bring the account positive, contact billers when timing must change, challenge errors promptly and document any fee discussion or reversal.
  - REVIEW — Measure whether the repair works over the next 90 days. Compare 90-day costs, choose the most suitable protection method and set an incident-free target plus a dated review.

## The 90-Day Cost Board

[paragraph] Use your agreement and statements. Josephine’s illustration assumes four uses, a 21% annual rate, and negative balances lasting 2, 3, 1 and 5 days in that order.

[paragraph] Product terms vary; the arithmetic is training evidence, not a market quote.

[cards]
  - FICTIONAL PAY-PER-USE OPTION — $20.89 — $5 × 4 incidents = $20.00 in fees. Approximate interest: $0.89. Total 90-day overdraft-related cost: $20.89.
  - FICTIONAL MONTHLY OPTION — $15.89 — $5 × 3 months = $15.00. With the same approximate interest: $15.89. It is cheaper in this incident pattern, but only while the monthly charge and usage assumptions remain true.
  - NO-PROTECTION OUTCOME — NOT $0 — Do not enter $0 automatically. A payment may be declined or returned. Verify any NSF fee, biller return fee, late charge, service interruption and practical consequence.

[myths]
  - “Overdraft interest is tiny, so the total cost is tiny.” — Interest on a short negative balance may be small, but fixed monthly or per-use fees can dominate the total. Compare every cost over the same 90-day behaviour pattern.

[spotlight] For short, small overdrafts, the fixed fee may matter more than the interest. The correct comparison is fees plus interest plus outside consequences, not the rate by itself.

## Build Controls Before the Next Scheduled Debit

[ordered]
  - Raise the alert threshold. — Use an amount high enough to warn you before the next essential payment, not after.
  - Create a payment runway. — List every scheduled debit between today and the next deposit, including pending transactions.
  - Separate bill money. — Move funded bill amounts into a dedicated account or tracking category before daily spending begins.
  - Build a small account buffer. — Start with one frequent timing gap; do not count the buffer as spendable money.
  - Review the protection agreement. — Confirm the limit, annual rate, fee model, repayment treatment, cancellation process and what happens above the limit.

[spotlight] Federally regulated financial institutions send an electronic alert when an account balance falls below the chosen threshold. The default is $100, but the learner may customize the amount or opt out. A useful threshold reflects upcoming payments, not just zero.

[source] FCAC: Financial Consumer Protection Framework
  url: https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/banking/rights-new-protections/consumer-protection-framework.html

[myths]
  - “A low-balance alert prevents overdraft.” — An alert is a smoke alarm, not a firefighter. It creates time to review pending activity, transfer available funds, pause spending or contact a biller. It cannot create money or stop every already-authorized transaction.

[heading] Province Lens: Identify the Institution Before the Rulebook

[paragraph] Federal banks and federal credit unions follow the federal consumer framework. A provincial credit union follows its own province’s rules and complaint route. Start with the institution’s written agreement and internal complaint process.

[cards]
  - AB / BC — CUDGC for Alberta credit unions; BCFSA for BC-authorized credit unions.
  - NB / ON — FCNB for New Brunswick credit unions; FSRA for Ontario credit unions.
  - PEI / NL — PEI Justice and Public Safety or the applicable credit-union authority; Newfoundland and Labrador Regulatory Affairs Branch.

[heading] Before You Audit Your Own Overdraft Incidents

[myths]
  - “The new $10 NSF cap makes a returned payment harmless.” — The federal cap reduces the bank’s NSF fee, but the payment may still fail. A biller may apply a separate charge, mark the payment late, interrupt service or require another payment method. Repair the failed payment, not only the bank fee.

[spotlight] Since March 12, 2026, a federally regulated bank generally cannot charge more than $10 for an NSF event on a personal deposit account. It cannot charge another NSF fee to that account within two business days, and it cannot charge one when the overdraft is under $10.

## Glossary of Terms

[glossary]
  - Overdraft — A negative account balance created when your institution covers a transaction that goes past your available funds.
  - Overdraft protection — An agreed credit service that may cover eligible transactions up to an approved limit, with possible fees and interest.
  - NSF — Non-sufficient funds: there is not enough available money or protection to cover a payment that comes through.
  - Pre-authorized debit — Permission you give a biller to withdraw an agreed payment from your account.
  - Pending transaction — A transaction that has started but has not fully posted to the account yet.
  - Available balance — The amount you can use right now, after the institution applies posted activity and any holds.
  - Overdraft fee model — The agreement may charge you each time protection is used, or apply one recurring monthly fee instead.
  - Account buffer — Money you leave in the account on purpose, not counted as available for everyday spending.

## Check-in

1. HOW OFTEN — In the last 30 to 90 days, how many times did your balance go below zero or a payment fail?
2. WHAT HAPPENED — For each event, write the date, payment name, amount, any fee and whether the payment went through.
3. WHY IT HAPPENED — What was the main cause: timing, a recurring charge, spending, a held deposit, low income, an error or possible fraud?
4. TOTAL COST — Add the fees and interest. What did these events cost in total?
5. COMPARE YOUR OPTIONS — Over the next 90 days, which option may cost less: monthly overdraft, pay-per-use, linked savings or no protection?
6. NEXT SEVEN DAYS — Which essential payments are due before your next income, and what must you do now to cover them?
7. PREVENT ANOTHER EVENT — Choose an alert amount, a bill calendar and one small buffer or bill change.
8. YOUR DECISION — Will you keep, change or cancel overdraft protection? Briefly explain why and choose a review date.
