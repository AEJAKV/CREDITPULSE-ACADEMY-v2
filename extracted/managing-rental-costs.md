## Start here

[paragraph] A rental listing may say $1,650. Your budget may later introduce utilities, internet, parking, laundry, tenant insurance and moving costs, the supporting cast nobody invited to the photo shoot.

[paragraph] This workshop separates three questions that are often tangled together: What does the home cost each month? Can the household carry that cost through a difficult month? What rules govern rent changes in the province or territory? A budgeting benchmark is not a legal right, and a legal notice does not make an increase affordable.

[spotlight] The useful rent number is the true monthly housing cost: base rent plus recurring services, shared costs and a monthly share of irregular housing expenses.

[chips]
  - Build a complete monthly rental-cost picture.
  - Use the 35% benchmark as a planning signal, not a law or pass/fail rule.
  - Stress-test a possible rent increase and other changes.
  - Separate roommate arrangements from landlord-tenant rights.
  - Complete a Rent-First Plan using verified provincial information.

[myths]
  - “If I can afford the advertised rent, I can afford the apartment.” — The listed rent may exclude utilities, internet, parking, insurance, laundry, storage and irregular setup or moving costs. Price the full arrangement before signing.

[transition] An advertised rent and a true monthly cost are rarely the same number, and the gap has a name: everything the listing left out.
  detail: Pricing utilities, parking and irregular costs before signing is what keeps a tidy listing price from becoming a monthly surprise.

## Expose the True Monthly Housing Cost

[spotlight] FCAC presents rent plus household-related expenses of about 35% of gross household income as a general budgeting guideline. It is not a tenancy law, approval rule or guarantee of comfort.

[heading] Put every housing cost under the X-ray

[cards]
  - 01 — Base rent, due date, lease term and payment method.
  - 02 — Electricity, heat, water and other utilities not included in rent.
  - 03 — Internet, tenant insurance, parking, laundry, storage and access costs.
  - 04 — Moving, utility deposits, furniture, annual permits and seasonal utility spikes.
  - 05 — Divide estimated annual irregular costs by 12 so the money receives a job before the surprise arrives.

[spotlight] A $1,650 rent increased by 3% becomes $1,699.50, a $49.50 monthly change and $594 over a full year. Small percentages prefer annual totals to reveal their personality.

[myths]
  - “The 35% guideline proves a rental is affordable.” — It is only a starting signal. Net income, childcare, debt, transportation, accessibility, location and variable earnings may make a lower or higher share realistic for a particular household.

[source] FCAC: Renting your first apartment or house
  url: https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/renting-first-apartment.html

## Amara, Jules and the Apartment With Invisible Roommates

[story] Meet Amara and Jules
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ Amara Mensah and Jules Tremblay found a two-bedroom apartment in Moncton advertised at $1,620. They liked the bus route, the sunlight and the kitchen drawer that opened without negotiating with the stove. Their plan looked simple: split the rent and move in.
  ¶ The conflict appeared when Amara read the lease while Jules priced the move. Heat was included, but electricity and internet were not. Parking cost extra. The laundry machines accepted payment with the confidence of a boutique hotel. Jules also worked variable shifts, so an equal monthly split could still become a timing problem between paydays.
  ¶ They built a Rental Cost X-Ray. They added every recurring cost, divided estimated annual expenses by 12 and requested written answers about included services. They checked New Brunswick’s current rent-increase information rather than relying on a neighbour’s cousin, who was extremely certain and magnificently unsourced. Their private roommate agreement recorded the split, due dates, shared purchases, notice if one person planned to leave and a backup plan if a transfer was late.
  ¶ Their first total was higher than the listing but still workable after they removed a parking stall they did not need. Each payday, they transferred a share of next month’s housing cost into a separate rent-first account. They also kept a small housing buffer and saved photos, receipts, the lease and their condition report.
  ¶ Six months later, Jules lost two shifts in one pay period. The buffer covered the timing gap, the rent was paid in full and their roommate agreement guided repayment without turning the kitchen into a courtroom. The apartment had never been unaffordable; the advertised number had just been incomplete. Their conclusion was practical: inspect the total, verify the rules and plan the timing before the keys arrive.

[spotlight] A roommate agreement can clarify money between roommates, but it does not automatically replace the lease or provincial tenancy law. Know who is named on the lease and what each signature means.

[heading] What resolved the conflict?

[paragraph] Amara and Jules converted an attractive listing into a verified monthly total. They matched transfers to their pay schedules and separated their private sharing plan from the landlord-tenant relationship.

[transition] A missed shift and a rent-first buffer meant Jules's shortfall never became Amara's problem.
  detail: Funding the next due date before anything else is what let one lost paycheque stay a timing gap instead of a missed payment.
  extra: Build your own rent-first system next, one payday at a time.

## Build a Rent-First System

[ordered]
  - Fund the next due date first — Transfer a fixed amount from each payday into a separate housing bucket. If income varies, use a conservative income floor and top up in stronger weeks.
  - Stress-test change — Calculate the monthly and annual effect of an increase, utility spike, roommate departure or parking change. Decide what would be reduced, renegotiated or replaced.
  - Keep the paper trail — Save the signed lease, condition report, notices, receipts, emails and photos. Record dates and communicate important issues in writing.
  - Verify the legal layer — Rent-increase timing, notice, caps, exemptions and forms vary by province or territory and can change. Check the current government or tenancy authority before acting.

[spotlight] The 2026 rules are not one Canadian rule. British Columbia lists a 2.3% residential limit, Ontario a 2.1% guideline with exemptions, while Alberta generally regulates timing and notice rather than setting a general cap.

[spotlight] New Brunswick’s framework, Newfoundland and Labrador’s notice rules and PEI’s allowable-increase process differ again. Province, tenancy type, date and proper form all matter.

[myths]
  - “My landlord mentioned an increase, so the new amount must automatically be valid.” — A valid increase may require minimum timing, written notice, a prescribed form, an applicable limit or another condition. Verify the current rules and tenancy facts; get provincial guidance or legal help when needed.

## Glossary of Terms

[glossary]
  - Gross household income — Household income before tax and deductions.
  - True monthly housing cost — Base rent plus recurring and monthly allocated irregular housing expenses.
  - Fixed-term tenancy — A tenancy with an agreed start and end date, subject to provincial rules.
  - Periodic tenancy — A tenancy continuing by rental period, such as month to month.
  - Rent-increase notice — Formal notice that must meet the applicable timing, content and delivery rules.
  - Condition report — A written record of a rental unit’s condition, often supported by dated photos.
