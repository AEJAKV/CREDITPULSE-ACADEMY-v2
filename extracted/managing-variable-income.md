## Start here

[paragraph] Variable income changes from month to month.

[paragraph] It may come from commissions, tips, contract work, seasonal hours, self-employment, several jobs or a business whose customers have never heard of a tidy payday. The income itself isn’t the problem. The household needs a system for timing, separation and decisions.

[paragraph] In this Income Weather System, you will study 6 to 12 months of records and find your storm, typical and sunny income bands. Then you will set a safe household baseline, send income through a holding account and write rules for low and high months. The goal is to keep rent from depending on a record-breaking July, not to predict every cloud.

[source] FCAC: Making a budget
  url: https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/make-budget.html

[spotlight] Variable income is not a budgeting failure. A household with fixed expenses can live on changing income when it has a safe baseline, a cash buffer and written high- and low-month rules.

## Build a Three-Band Forecast

[cards]
  - LAYER 1 — STORM MONTH — LOW BUT REAL — A lower-income month that shows up in your records and could happen again. Use it to test whether you can still cover essential expenses and required payments.
  - LAYER 2 — TYPICAL MONTH — COMMON RANGE — The middle of your recent pattern. It is not your best month and not one unusually weak month. It helps with planning, but it is not automatically a safe baseline.
  - LAYER 3 — SUNNY MONTH — STRONGER INCOME — A higher-income month. Its extra money has jobs: future low months, tax or remittance bills, irregular costs, savings, debt and goals. It is not proof that every month has joined the celebration.

[myths]
  - “The average monthly income is always the safest amount to budget.” — One or two strong months can pull an average up. Look at your lowest reliable, typical and strongest months, then choose a baseline that covers essentials without counting on unusually high income.

[heading] Read the Forecast Before Choosing the Baseline

[paragraph] Maya gathers eight months of pay records and bank statements. She starts with net cash received after payroll deductions, not gross sales or invoices. If a source may not have enough tax withheld, she sets aside the confirmed amount before working out what is available for household spending.

[cards]
  - STORM BAND — $2,900–$3,250 — Lowest recorded month: $2,900 — Test essentials and write the low-month rule
  - TYPICAL BAND — $3,350–$4,500 — Middle pattern — Plan regular household decisions
  - SUNNY BAND — $4,700–$5,200 — Strongest recorded month: $5,200 — Fund future obligations and goals
  - BASELINE PAY — $3,100 — Fixed household transfer — Supported by the pattern plus an income buffer

[story] Meet Maya
  detail: MAYA’S FORECAST HAS ONE SEASON: OPTIMISM
  ¶ Maya is a hairstylist in Halifax. Her take-home pay moves with shifts, bookings and tips, and her recent months run from $2,900 to $5,200. Her goal is to pay every essential bill on time without treating each quiet month as an emergency. Until now she has planned from the $5,200 month, because it was memorable and arrived wearing excellent shoes.
  ¶ Then another $5,200 month lands. Maya sees $2,100 above her planned $3,100 baseline, and a weekend trip starts campaigning hard.
  ¶ But Maya remembers a few things. Annual licence fees are coming, January is always slow, and some of this income may not have enough tax withheld. The surplus has several fair claimants and only one chequing balance.
  ¶ Maya makes a choice. She gives every dollar of the $2,100 a job, and only $50 goes to flexible spending. The trip is not cancelled. It moves behind the obligations that keep next month from getting expensive. Restraint isn’t punishment. It is scheduling with better manners.
  ¶ The test comes fast. The next month brings $2,900. Under her old method, the $200 gap would have landed on a credit card. Now the weather system has to do actual weather, not just look attractive on a worksheet. A planned $200 transfer from the buffer keeps her baseline whole, and no borrowing is needed.
  ¶ Three months later, Maya has made every baseline transfer, kept tax-separated cash out of her spending total and logged two buffer top-ups. Her quarterly review shows that $3,100 still fits the evidence. She books the trip, paid from a named goal balance and not from the brief emotional availability of a sunny chequing account.

## The Holding-Account Workflow

[ordered]
  - 1. RECEIVE — Variable income lands in a separate holding account or a clearly separate savings bucket.
  - 2. SEPARATE — Before flexible spending, move aside any tax, remittance or business amount you worked out from reliable guidance. Don’t guess one percentage for everyone.
  - 3. PAY THE HOUSEHOLD — Move the same $3,100 baseline to the spending account on planned dates when the holding balance can cover it.
  - 4. HOLD THE DIFFERENCE — Leave the extra from higher months assigned to the income buffer and other named priorities. Don’t let the account balance pose as permission to spend.

[myths]
  - “Money left after this month’s bills is bonus money.” — A sunny-month balance may need to cover the next storm month, yearly costs, tax or remittance bills and late business expenses. Assign the extra money before you spend it.

[spotlight] A holding account separates when income arrives from when the household spends it. It creates a steadier personal payday without pretending the income itself is fixed.

## Give Every Sunny-Month Dollar a Job

[paragraph] A high-month waterfall is a priority order for money above the baseline. It is not a rigid legal rule. Households adjust it for urgent risks, agreements and goals.

[paragraph] The key is to decide the order before the strong month starts negotiating.

[heading] MAYA’S $2,100 SUNNY-MONTH SURPLUS

[paragraph] $500 tax/remittance + $500 income buffer + $300 irregular costs + $400 emergency fund + $250 high-interest debt + $100 long-term goal + $50 flexible spending.

[paragraph] Total assigned: $2,100.

[heading] GENERAL HIGH-MONTH WATERFALL

[paragraph] 1) Essentials and required payments; 2) verified tax/remittance reserve; 3) income buffer; 4) emergency fund; 5) irregular costs; 6) high-interest debt; 7) longer-term goals; 8) flexible spending.

[paragraph] Change the order when a real urgent risk calls for it.

[heading] Write the Storm-Month Rule Before the Storm

[ordered]
  - Protect first. — Cover housing, food, medicine, essential transport, insurance, utilities and required debt payments.
  - Use the income buffer as designed. — Maya moves $200 from the buffer so her household still gets the $3,100 baseline.
  - Pause lower priorities on purpose. — Cut flexible spending and optional goal contributions before you turn to expensive credit.
  - Escalate early. — If the gap will last, update the baseline, call billers or lenders before you miss payments, and get qualified help when you need it.

[myths]
  - “Every self-employed person should reserve the same percentage for tax.” — Tax and remittance needs depend on income, deductions, province or territory, filing history and other facts. Set cash aside, follow CRA information or notices, and get qualified tax advice for the amount. This course does not work out individual tax.

[source] CRA: Required tax instalments for individuals
  url: https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/payments/payments-cra/individual-payments/income-tax-instalments/who-pays-instalments.html

[spotlight] Tax set-aside money and the income buffer do different jobs. One protects money you may owe, and the other smooths household cash flow. Don’t count the same dollar in both places.

## Install the Controls That Keep the Forecast Honest

[cards]
  - BASELINE REVIEW — Review every three months and after any lasting change in income or expenses. Compare planned income with actual income and revise the bands when the pattern changes.
  - BUFFER FLOOR — Choose a target measured in baseline transfers or months of essential expenses. Build it a little at a time, and never count tax or bill money as buffer cash.
  - TRANSFER RULE — Use planned dates and amounts. If the holding balance can’t safely cover the transfer, switch on the storm rule instead of overdrawing the account.
  - RECONCILIATION — At month-end, compare your records with the forecast. Write down income received, amounts set aside, household transfers and buffer movement.

[heading] Four Forecast Failures to Catch Early

[cards]
  - Budgeting from the best month. — Strong income becomes a permanent spending promise.
  - Mixing every dollar together. — Tax, business, bill and personal money become impossible to tell apart.
  - Using credit as the buffer. — A timing problem turns into debt that charges interest.
  - Refusing to revise. — A baseline built from old records lives on long after your work pattern changes.

[spotlight] A credit limit is borrowing room, not an income reserve. A real income buffer is cash already set aside to carry the household through lower months.

[myths]
  - “Once the baseline is set, changing it means the plan failed.” — A baseline is a working estimate, not a tattoo. Review it when actual results keep differing, essential costs change or the income pattern shifts. Write down the reason and the new start date.

[heading] Province and Territory Lens

[paragraph] The forecast, holding-account and high-/low-month methods work across Canada. Tax, remittance, benefit and income-support details can differ by province or territory. Quebec has its own instalment thresholds in current CRA guidance. Check current official information for where you live, and don’t hard-code changing thresholds into a household worksheet.

## Quick Check: Read the Weather Signal

[accordions]
  - (Don’t use the average automatically. Test the lower pattern.) — One unusually strong month lifts the average.
  - (Apply the written high-month waterfall.) — Income exceeds the baseline.
  - (Use the buffer and the storm rule, and protect essentials first.) — Income is below the baseline.
  - (Set cash aside and check what you owe. Don’t guess one percentage for everyone.) — Tax was not fully withheld.
  - (Recalculate the bands and baseline.) — Actual results repeatedly miss the forecast.

[heading] Before You Build Your Own Income Weather System

[myths]
  - “A cash-flow system can make an unaffordable baseline work indefinitely.” — No system creates income that isn’t there. If your safe forecast can’t cover essential costs and required payments, you need a written gap plan: cut or reschedule costs, raise income where realistic, call creditors early and get qualified support.

[spotlight] A forecast works when it shows a gap early enough to act on. An honest warning is more useful than a cheerful baseline that needs new debt every month.

## Glossary of Terms

[glossary]
  - Variable income — Income that changes in amount or timing, such as commissions, tips, contract work, seasonal hours and self-employment income.
  - Household-available income — Money you can use for the household after deductions and planned set-asides. It is not the same as gross receipts.
  - Income band — A range used to group lower, typical or stronger income months.
  - Conservative baseline — A household transfer amount chosen from solid evidence, not from the strongest month.
  - Holding account — A separate account or bucket that receives variable income before planned household transfers.
  - Income buffer — Cash set aside to support the baseline during lower-income periods.
  - High-month waterfall — A written order of priorities for money above the baseline.
  - Storm-month rule — A response written ahead of time for income below the baseline, with priorities, pauses and steps to take if the gap lasts.
  - Tax/remittance reserve — Cash kept apart for a tax, contribution or remittance bill you have confirmed. The amount depends on your situation.
  - Reconciliation — Comparing the forecast with real income, transfers, expenses and reserve movements.
