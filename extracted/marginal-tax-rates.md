## Start here

[paragraph] You get offered more money. A promotion, a new job, a bonus.

[paragraph] And somewhere in the back of your mind, a voice says: wait, won’t this push me into a higher bracket? Won’t I actually end up with less? No. You won’t.

[paragraph] That fear has a name — it’s a misunderstanding of marginal tax rates — and it talks people out of raises, promotions, and side income every single year. This module exists to put it to rest, permanently.

## Picture shelves, not a single price tag

[paragraph] Forget the word bracket for a second. Picture shelves instead.

[paragraph] Your income gets stacked onto shelves, starting from the bottom. Each shelf has its own tax rate. The bottom shelf has the lowest rate. Each shelf above it costs a little more.

[paragraph] Here’s the part that matters most: your tax rate for a shelf only applies to the income sitting on that shelf. Not your whole income. Just that slice.

[paragraph] So when someone says “I’m in the 20.5% bracket,” they don’t mean 20.5% of everything they earn. They mean their top dollars — the ones sitting on that particular shelf — are taxed at that rate. Every dollar below it is still taxed at the lower rates of the shelves underneath.

We’ll leave the province-by-province detail for another module — for now, the shelf idea is what matters.

[myths]
  - “If I earn $60,000, all of it gets taxed at 20.5% because that’s my bracket.” — Only about $1,500 of it does — the sliver sitting above $58,523. The other $58,523 is still taxed at 14%, the same as someone earning exactly that amount. Your bracket describes your last dollar, never your whole income.

## Let’s actually do the math

[paragraph] Numbers convince better than explanations. Here’s someone earning $70,000 in 2026, federal tax only.

[chips]
  - First $58,523, taxed at 14%. That’s $8,193.22.
  - Remaining $11,477, taxed at 20.5%. That’s $2,352.79.
  - Total federal tax: $10,546.01, before any credits.

[paragraph] Notice what didn’t happen. The whole $70,000 was not taxed at 20.5%. Only the top slice was. That’s the entire idea, in one example.

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: MARGINAL RATE VS. EFFECTIVE RATE, THE TWO NUMBERS PEOPLE MIX UP

[spotlight] In that example, this person’s marginal rate is 20.5%. That’s the rate on their next dollar earned — the rate that matters for decisions like taking on overtime or a side gig. But their effective rate — total tax divided by total income — works out to about 15%. That’s the rate that actually describes how much of their whole paycheque went to tax. Both numbers are real. They just answer different questions. Marginal rate answers “what happens if I earn one more dollar?” Effective rate answers “overall, what share of my income did I actually pay?” Mixing them up is where the raise-anxiety myth comes from in the first place.

## Where this actually changes a decision

[paragraph] Understanding your marginal rate isn’t just trivia. It’s the number behind several real financial choices.

[paragraph] RRSP contributions. An RRSP contribution reduces your taxable income at your marginal rate — the rate on your top shelf, not your average rate. Someone earning enough to sit on the 29% shelf gets a bigger tax saving per dollar contributed than someone sitting on the 14% shelf. This is why RRSP contributions are often more valuable in your highest-earning years.

[paragraph] A raise or a bonus. Once you know only your top slice moves to a new rate, the anxiety mostly disappears. A raise that pushes a small sliver of your income onto a higher shelf still leaves you with more money overall. Always.

[paragraph] A side gig or freelance income. Extra income stacks on top of what you already earn. If your day job already fills your lower shelves, side income often lands on a higher shelf right away. Worth knowing before you spend it, so a tax bill doesn’t surprise you the following spring.

[myths]
  - “Everyone should max out their RRSP as early as possible to save on tax.” — The tax saving is bigger in years you sit on a higher shelf. Someone in a lower-earning year, like a first job or a parental leave, may get more value contributing later, once their income — and their marginal rate — has climbed. Timing the contribution to your shelf can matter as much as the amount.

## Before you turn down extra income out of tax fear

[chips]
  - I know which shelf my current income mostly sits on.
  - I understand that only new income above my current shelf moves to a higher rate.
  - I know my marginal rate is not the same as my effective rate.
  - I haven’t confused a raise with a pay cut — a raise always leaves you with more, never less.
  - I’ve checked whether my province adds its own shelves on top, since the full picture includes both.

## Renata almost said no

[story] Renata almost said no
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ Renata was offered a promotion that would push her salary from $55,000 to $62,000. Good news, on paper.
  ¶ Her uncle, over dinner, told her to think carefully — he’d heard that a raise like that could bump her into a higher bracket and she might actually take home less. He meant well. He was also wrong.
  ¶ Renata almost turned the promotion down. Instead, she asked a coworker who’d taken a similar jump the year before. The coworker walked her through the shelf idea in about two minutes, on a napkin, at lunch.
  ¶ Only the $3,477 above $58,523 would move to the 20.5% shelf. Everything below kept its 14% rate. Renata took the promotion. Her paycheque grew, exactly as it should have — just not by quite as much as $7,000 flat, which is a very different thing from taking home less.

[takeaway] Tax brackets work like shelves. Only the income sitting on a given shelf is taxed at that shelf’s rate — everything below keeps its lower rate. Your marginal rate is the rate on your next dollar. Your effective rate is your overall average. A raise never leaves you with less money, even if a slice of it lands on a higher shelf.

## Glossary

[glossary]
  - Marginal tax rate — The rate on your next dollar of income. Describes only your top shelf.
  - Effective tax rate — Your total tax divided by your total income. Your real overall average.
  - Tax bracket — A range of income taxed at one rate. Higher brackets never apply retroactively to income below them.
  - Taxable income — Your income after deductions, like RRSP contributions, are subtracted. What the shelves actually apply to.
