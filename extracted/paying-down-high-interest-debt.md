## Start here

[paragraph] High-interest debt sends more of every payment to interest and leaves less to pay down the balance.

[paragraph] Throwing money at every balance at random won’t work. Settle urgent accounts, keep required payments on track, stop new borrowing and send extra money to one target you have checked.

[paragraph] In this High-Interest Debt Triage Room, you will build a Debt Emergency Inventory and sort each balance by danger, damage and drain. The aim is a plan that works on an ordinary payday, not a heroic one that survives until about Wednesday.

[source] FCAC: Paying back your debt
  url: https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/debt/paying-debt.html

[spotlight] The highest interest rate usually deserves the extra payment. An urgent housing, utility, secured-payment or legal problem may still need fixing first.

[heading] The Three-Part Triage Map

[cards]
  - LAYER 1 — DANGER — Spot past-due essentials, secured debts, collection or legal notices, and any account about to lose service, be repossessed or face another serious result. Check the deadline and call the creditor soon.
  - LAYER 2 — DAMAGE — Keep paying the required minimum or agreed amount on every account you can. A missed payment can bring fees, higher rates, collection calls or damage to your credit report.
  - LAYER 3 — DRAIN — Once urgent accounts and required payments are covered, send extra money to the high-interest target you have checked. Stop adding new purchases to it while you pay it down.

[myths]
  - “The fairest plan is to split every extra dollar equally among all debts.” — Equal isn’t always efficient. Once urgent accounts and required payments are covered, putting extra money on one high-interest target usually cuts interest faster than spreading it thin.

[heading] Triage Order Is Not Simply Balance Order

[ordered]
  - FIRST — Tara calls the utility before the notice deadline and gets a written two-payment plan: $120 this month and $120 next month.
  - SECOND — She keeps every required debt payment on track and checks the due dates. If the budget can’t cover them, she calls creditors before the due date instead of waiting for a missed-payment alert.
  - THIRD — For two months, $105 of her $225 extra goes to the 29.99% store card after the $120 utility payment. In month three, the full $225 goes to the store card.

[myths]
  - “The biggest balance must be the most expensive debt.” — Balance affects how many dollars of interest you pay, but the rate shows how hard each dollar is priced. Look at balance, rate, status and consequence together. Size alone doesn’t set the order.

[spotlight] A smaller balance with a high rate can be a great first target. It is expensive, and it may be gone sooner once urgent accounts are settled.

## The Five-Step High-Interest Response

[cards]
  - LAYER 1 — VERIFY — Write down the creditor, balance, yearly rate or APR, minimum, due date, status, whether it is secured, and any notice or special penalty. Mark what you don’t know for a call to the creditor.
  - LAYER 2 — STABILIZE — Protect housing, utilities and other essentials. Deal with past-due and secured accounts. Get payment plans in writing when you can.
  - LAYER 3 — HOLD THE LINE — Make the required payments you can afford, pause new charges, remove saved card details and stop optional repeat charges from hitting the target account.
  - LAYER 4 — TARGET — Send every planned extra dollar to one high-interest balance. Make it automatic or tied to payday when you can, but keep enough cash for essentials.
  - LAYER 5 — ROLL FORWARD — When the target is paid off, move its old required payment plus the extra amount to the next target. Check rates and account status each month.

## The Approximate Monthly Interest Scan

[paragraph] A quick training estimate is balance × annual rate ÷ 12.

[paragraph] Statements may work out interest daily, use average daily balances, compound differently, or include purchases and fees. Use your statement for the real charge.

[cards]
  - STORE CARD — $1,050 × 29.99% ÷ 12 ≈ $26.24 — An estimate for one month, before new activity and payment timing.
  - CREDIT CARD — $2,800 × 22.99% ÷ 12 ≈ $53.64 — Its lower rate still costs more dollars because the balance is bigger.
  - LINE OF CREDIT — $3,600 × 11.50% ÷ 12 = $34.50 — Check the current variable rate before you trust the estimate.

[myths]
  - “Making the minimum payment means the debt is being handled efficiently.” — A minimum keeps an account current, but it may pay down the balance slowly and stretch out the interest. Pay more than the minimum when you safely can, and keep your other required payments current.

[spotlight] A payment is not the same as paying down the balance. Interest and fees may take their share first. The statement shows what reached the balance.

[source] FCAC: Paying off your credit card
  url: https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/credit-cards/pay-off-credit-card.html

## Reduce the Rate Without Rebuilding the Debt

[ordered]
  - Ask the current creditor. — Ask for a lower-rate product, a hardship option or a payment plan. Get the rate, fees, term, effect on the account and total cost in writing.
  - Compare consolidation carefully. — A lower rate may save money, but a longer term, fees or new borrowing can raise the total cost. Compare the same balances and the same payoff date.
  - Protect collateral. — Moving unsecured debt onto a secured product may put your home or another asset at risk. A lower rate is not free protection.
  - Close the refill path. — If consolidation pays off a card, decide how you will control the empty account. That way you don’t end up with the new loan plus a rebuilt card balance.

[myths]
  - “Consolidation always saves money because one payment is simpler.” — One payment may be easier to manage, but real savings need a lower total cost, not just a lower monthly payment. Check the rate, fees, term, total repayment, collateral and whether old balances can come back.

[spotlight] A lower payment from a longer term can feel like relief while quietly buying you more years of interest. Compare the payoff date as carefully as the payment.

[source] FCAC: Debt consolidation
  url: https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/debt/debt-consolidation.html

[heading] When the Numbers Need More Than a Worksheet

[paragraph] Get qualified help early if required payments don’t fit after essentials, accounts stay past due, collection or legal action is under way, or new borrowing is paying old debts. Credit counsellors can talk through debt management plans, but creditors choose whether to accept them, and fees matter. Only Licensed Insolvency Trustees can run consumer proposals and bankruptcies.

[heading] Province Lens: Collection Rules Are Local

[paragraph] Collection rules differ in Alberta, British Columbia, New Brunswick, Ontario, Prince Edward Island, and Newfoundland and Labrador. Check who the collector is, what the debt is and how much is owed. Keep records and use your province’s current authority. Consumer proposals and bankruptcies are federal processes run by Licensed Insolvency Trustees.

[heading] Before You Build Your Own Debt Emergency Inventory

[myths]
  - “Asking for debt help means I have failed, and every debt-relief company offers the same protection.” — Getting help early is a smart way to control risk. Providers have different roles and authority. Check credentials, services, fees and promises, and avoid anyone who guarantees a quick credit-score fix or asks for money for a result they can’t legally deliver.

[spotlight] A debt-management plan is not the same as a consumer proposal or bankruptcy. Ask who regulates the provider, which creditors have to agree and what happens to debts outside the plan.

[heading] High-Interest Debt Triage Closing Checklist

[ordered]
  - Every debt is verified. — Balance, rate, required payment, due date, status, security and notices come from current records.
  - Immediate danger is under control. — Risks to essential services, secured debts, collections and legal matters each have a dated contact or a written plan.

[takeaway] Settle urgent problems and protect required payments first.

## Glossary of Terms

[glossary]
  - High-interest debt — Debt with a rate so high that it costs a lot to carry and slows how fast you pay down what you owe.
  - Principal — The amount you owe, before future interest and fees.
  - Minimum payment — The smallest payment the creditor requires each period. It is not meant to pay the debt off fast.
  - Past due — A required payment that did not arrive by its due date.
  - Secured debt — Debt backed by something you own, which you could lose if you break the agreement.
  - Unsecured debt — Debt that isn’t backed by a specific asset, such as many credit cards and personal loans.
  - Target debt — The one balance that gets the planned extra payments once urgent items and required payments are covered.
  - Debt consolidation — Rolling several debts into one loan or product, ideally at a lower total cost and a term you can handle.
  - Debt management plan — An informal repayment plan set up through a credit counsellor. Creditors choose whether to take part.
  - Licensed Insolvency Trustee — A federally regulated professional who is allowed to run consumer proposals and bankruptcies in Canada.

## Check-in

1. DEBT ROSTER — List each creditor, current balance, rate or APR, required payment, due date, account status and secured or unsecured type.
2. DANGER SCREEN — Which account has a past-due essential, secured-asset, collection, legal or other urgent consequence? Record the deadline and contact step.
3. PAYMENT FLOOR — Add all required payments plus any written arrangements. Does the amount fit after housing, food, utilities, transportation and other essentials?
4. INTEREST SCAN — Estimate balance × annual rate ÷ 12 for each revolving debt. Then record the actual statement interest for comparison.
5. TARGET — After urgent items and required payments, which verified high-interest debt receives the extra amount? Why does it rank first?
6. 30-DAY ATTACK — State the exact extra payment, payday transfer date, spending blocks and one expense or income move funding the plan.
7. RATE-REDUCTION TEST — Record one creditor, consolidation or counselling option. Compare rate, fees, term, total repayment, collateral and refill risk.
8. ESCALATION LINE — What warning triggers qualified help? Name the creditor, counsellor, provincial authority or Licensed Insolvency Trustee to contact and a review date.
