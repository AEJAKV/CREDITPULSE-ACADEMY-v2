## Start here

[paragraph] You do not need to remember any of the last eight modules. That is not a joke at your expense, or ours.

[paragraph] You need to remember three or four tools that will ask you the questions instead.

[paragraph] A benefit finder is a short questionnaire, age, province, income range, household situation, a few checkboxes, that returns a personalized list of programs you may be eligible for, federal and provincial together. None of them replace this course. What they do is turn everything the last eight modules taught you into a habit you can repeat every year in fifteen minutes, long after the details of Module 1.3 have faded.

## Four tools, and what each one is actually good for

[paragraph] Eight modules of programs, layers, lanes and instruments is a great deal to carry around, and nobody is expected to hold it all in their head at tax time, during a renovation, or the week after a layoff. What is worth carrying around is much shorter.

[cards]
  - The federal Benefits Finder — Run by the Government of Canada at canada.ca, this is the broadest net. Answer a short questionnaire and it returns a personalized list spanning federal, provincial and territorial programs. No account required, and it does not track you beyond the session. A reasonable place to start, precisely because it does not assume you already know which government is responsible for what.
  - Your provincial government’s own benefits page — Every province and territory maintains one, usually found by searching your province’s name plus benefits or find services. British Columbia calls its version a Benefits Connector. These tend to be more current on provincial specifics than the federal tool, because the province is the one updating its own programs.
  - 211 — Not a website first. A phone number. Dial 2-1-1 anywhere in Canada and reach a live person who knows local, provincial and some federal programs, including the ones that never make a government homepage: a municipal home repair program, a food bank’s income supports, a legal clinic’s hours.
  - The Benefits Wayfinder — Built by Prosper Canada, a national charity, not a government department. Designed in plain language for people on low and modest incomes, usable alone or with a front-line worker. Where the federal tool is broadest, this one is built for depth on exactly the population this course spends the most time on.

[myths]
  - “These tools all show the same results, so it does not matter which one I use.” — They pull from overlapping but not identical sources. A provincial tool will often surface a local program the federal one never mentions, while the federal tool catches cross-border programs a provincial one has no reason to list. Running more than one is not redundant. It is closer to how you would search for anything else you actually wanted to find completely.

[paragraph] Quick gut check: if the last time you ran one of these was more than a year ago, that is the whole lesson of this module, right there.

## The habit that matters more than any tool

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: RUN MORE THAN ONE, AND RUN THEM AGAIN NEXT YEAR

[spotlight] The single most valuable habit in this module is not any individual tool. It is treating the exercise as recurring rather than one-time. Eligibility changes constantly and quietly: a birthday that crosses an age threshold, a change in household income, a new child, a diagnosis, a move to a different province, a program that launched last month that did not exist when you last checked. Someone who ran a benefits finder three years ago and concluded they qualified for nothing is not wrong about three years ago. They may be very wrong about right now. Set a recurring reminder, tax season is a natural anchor since you already have your income figures in front of you, and run the federal tool plus your provincial one every year, even in years where nothing feels like it has changed. The tools take fifteen minutes. Missing a program for three straight years because nobody re-checked costs considerably more than that.

## What a benefit finder cannot do

[paragraph] It returns a list, not an approval. Every result still requires you to read the actual eligibility rules, gather documents, and apply through the real program, everything this course has spent nine modules teaching you to do properly. Treat the finder as the map, not the destination.

[paragraph] It also cannot know what it was not told. If a questionnaire asks about income and you round generously, or skip a question about disability status because it feels intrusive, the results reflect the version of your situation you gave it, not your actual situation. These tools do not verify anything and, per their own privacy statements, generally do not retain your answers, which means there is little reason to under-report on a form that cannot be checked against you later, and every reason to answer completely.

[heading] Running a benefit-finder session properly

[chips]
  - I have my rough household income and family situation ready before I start, so I am not guessing mid-questionnaire.
  - I am running both the federal tool and my province’s own tool, not just one.
  - I am answering every question completely, including ones that feel personal.
  - I am treating every result as a lead to verify, not a confirmed benefit.
  - I have a note of today’s date, since results reflect programs open right now.
  - I have set a reminder to do this again next year.

## Walter, a library computer, and the list he did not expect

[story] Walter and the library computer
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ Walter is seventy-three, lives alone outside Saskatoon, and has never in his life described himself as someone who applies for things. He worked, he paid his taxes, and when his wife passed three years ago he assumed whatever support existed for that had already found him, the way his pension had.
  ¶ His niece sat with him at the library one Saturday, mostly to help him print something else, and asked on a whim whether he had ever run the federal Benefits Finder. He had not heard of it. Fifteen minutes and a short questionnaire later, the list included two things he recognized and three he did not, among them a provincial home support program for seniors and a survivor-related supplement he had never been told about, because nobody mails a letter announcing a benefit exists. You have to go looking, or answer a questionnaire that goes looking for you.
  ¶ He called 211 the following Monday to confirm the provincial program was real and current, which it was, and applied within the week.

[takeaway] Run the federal Benefits Finder and your province’s own tool, not just one, and call 211 for anything local a web form would not know about. Answer every question completely, since the tools cannot check anything you do not tell them. Treat every result as a lead to verify against the real program, and run the whole exercise again next year, because eligibility changes quietly and constantly.

## Glossary

[glossary]
  - Benefits Finder — The federal government’s own questionnaire tool at canada.ca, returning federal, provincial and territorial results together.
  - Benefits Connector — British Columbia’s version of a provincial benefit-finder tool.
  - Benefits Wayfinder — A plain-language finder built by the charity Prosper Canada, aimed at people on low and modest incomes.
  - 211 — A free phone and, in many provinces, web service connecting callers to local, provincial and some federal social and government services.

[lists]
  - WHERE TO GET HELP — 211: dial 2-1-1 anywhere in Canada for a live person and the current local list.; Canada.ca Benefits Finder: the federal government’s own tool, no account required.; Your province’s own benefits page: search your province’s name plus benefits or find services.; Benefits Wayfinder: run through Prosper Canada, usable alone or with help from a community agency.

[paragraph] This module deliberately names tools rather than dollar amounts, since a finder’s job is to surface current programs. The figures live inside each result, not in the tool itself.
