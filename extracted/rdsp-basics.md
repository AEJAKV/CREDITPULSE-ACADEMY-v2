## Start here

[paragraph] The Registered Disability Savings Plan pays more government money, per dollar contributed, than any other account in this course.

[paragraph] At certain income levels, a contribution of $1,500 can attract $3,500 in government grants. That’s more than double your money, immediately, before any investment growth at all.

[paragraph] And yet fewer than a third of eligible Canadians have one open. Not because it’s a bad deal — because most people have never had it explained to them.

## Who can open one

[paragraph] There’s one gate, and everything else follows from it: the beneficiary must be approved for the Disability Tax Credit.

[paragraph] That approval comes through Form T2201, which a medical practitioner completes, confirming a severe and prolonged impairment in physical or mental functions. Without it, an RDSP can’t be opened at all.

[paragraph] If someone in your family has a long-term impairment and nobody has ever applied for the Disability Tax Credit, that application is the first step — and it unlocks far more than just this one account.

[paragraph] Beyond that: the beneficiary must be a Canadian resident, and under 60 when the plan is opened.

[myths]
  - “The Disability Tax Credit is only for people who can’t work at all.” — The test is whether an impairment is severe and prolonged, and markedly restricts daily functions — not whether someone is employed. Many people who work full-time qualify. Applications are also commonly declined on a first, thin submission and approved on a second one with more specific medical detail.

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: YOU DO NOT NEED TO BE A LEGAL GUARDIAN TO OPEN ONE

[spotlight] One more barrier worth clearing up before it stops anyone. For an adult beneficiary who is not contractually competent to manage their own affairs, opening an RDSP used to mean going through formal legal guardianship or trusteeship first — a slow, expensive process that stopped a lot of families before they started. Since 2012, a qualifying family member, typically a parent, spouse or common-law partner, can act as the plan holder instead, without that legal step, provided nobody else already has legal authority in place. This provision has been extended repeatedly and is worth checking as current when you apply, since it directly determines whether a parent can simply open the account themselves this month, or needs to start a legal process first. Ask the financial institution about the qualifying family member rule specifically before assuming guardianship is required.

## Two kinds of government money

[paragraph] The RDSP has two separate federal programs attached, and they work differently. It’s worth keeping them straight.

[paragraph] The grant — matches what you put in. The Canada Disability Savings Grant matches your contributions. How generously depends on family income, but at lower income levels the match can reach three dollars for every one you contribute on the first portion. The maximum grant in a single year is $3,500. Over a lifetime, $70,000.

[paragraph] The bond — requires nothing from you. The Canada Disability Savings Bond is different. It pays into the account based on family income alone, with no contribution required at all. Up to $1,000 a year, to a lifetime maximum of $20,000. For a lower-income family who genuinely cannot spare money to contribute, simply having the account open can be worth real money.

[cards]
  - Contribution needed? — Grant (CDSG): Yes — it matches what you put in — Bond (CDSB): No — paid on income alone
  - Maximum per year — Grant (CDSG): $3,500 — Bond (CDSB): $1,000
  - Lifetime maximum — Grant (CDSG): $70,000 — Bond (CDSB): $20,000
  - Available until — Grant (CDSG): End of the year the beneficiary turns 49 — Bond (CDSB): End of the year the beneficiary turns 49

## Open the account, even if you can’t contribute

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: OPEN THE ACCOUNT, EVEN IF YOU CAN’T CONTRIBUTE

[spotlight] This is the point most worth carrying away from this module. The bond requires no contribution. If family income qualifies, the government deposits money simply because the account exists and the beneficiary is eligible. Families sometimes decide an RDSP isn’t for them because money is tight and regular contributions feel impossible. But that’s precisely the situation the bond was built for. Deciding not to open the account because you can’t contribute is the one choice that guarantees receiving nothing. Opening the account costs nothing. Leaving it unopened can cost thousands.

## Missed years can often be caught up

[paragraph] Grant and bond entitlements carry forward for up to 10 years.

[paragraph] So a family who qualifies now, but didn’t open an account earlier, may be able to claim entitlements from previous years by contributing more now. This is why opening the account sooner rather than later matters — every year that passes is a year of older entitlements aging toward expiry.

[paragraph] The CRA issues a Statement of Entitlement each February showing exactly what’s available. That document is the clearest picture of what a family can still claim.

[myths]
  - “If we didn’t open an RDSP when the diagnosis happened, we’ve missed our chance.” — Entitlements carry forward for up to 10 years. A family opening an account today may be able to claim several years of missed grants and bonds by contributing now. It’s often far from too late — but the window does move, so acting sooner captures more.

## Taking money out: the rule to plan around

[paragraph] Money can be withdrawn for any purpose that benefits the beneficiary. There’s no requirement to spend it on anything specific.

[paragraph] But there’s a holdback rule worth understanding before you plan around withdrawals. If you take money out within 10 years of receiving a grant or bond, some of that government money has to be repaid — generally three dollars for every one dollar withdrawn, up to the total received in that 10-year window.

[paragraph] This isn’t a reason to avoid an RDSP. It’s a reason to treat it as the long-term account it was designed to be, and to plan withdrawals with that 10-year timeline in mind. A financial advisor familiar with RDSPs is genuinely useful here.

## The Alvarez family’s second conversation

[story] The Alvarez family’s second conversation
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ The Alvarez family had heard of the RDSP. Their son had been approved for the Disability Tax Credit two years earlier, and someone at the clinic had mentioned the account in passing.
  ¶ They didn’t open one. Money was tight, and the whole thing sounded like it required regular contributions they simply couldn’t commit to. It felt like an account for families with more room in their budget than they had.
  ¶ A social worker at their son’s program brought it up again the following year, and asked one question that changed things: had anyone explained the bond to them? Nobody had. Their family income fell below the threshold, which meant government money would go into the account whether or not they ever contributed a dollar.
  ¶ They opened it that month. Because entitlements carry forward, several previous years of bond payments were still claimable. The account started with meaningful money in it, none of which had come out of a budget that couldn’t spare it.

[takeaway] The RDSP pays more government money per dollar contributed than any other account in this course. Disability Tax Credit approval is the gate — everything follows from it. The grant matches contributions, but the bond requires no contribution at all, so open the account even if you can’t contribute. Entitlements carry forward up to 10 years, and withdrawals within 10 years of a grant trigger repayment, so plan for the long term.

## Glossary

[glossary]
  - RDSP — Registered Disability Savings Plan. A long-term, tax-deferred savings account for someone approved for the Disability Tax Credit.
  - DTC — Disability Tax Credit. Certification of a severe and prolonged impairment, established with Form T2201. Required to open an RDSP.
  - CDSG — Canada Disability Savings Grant. Government matching on contributions, up to $3,500 a year.
  - CDSB — Canada Disability Savings Bond. Government money paid based on family income, with no contribution required.
  - Holdback — The rule requiring repayment of grants and bonds received in the 10 years before a withdrawal.
  - Statement of Entitlement — The CRA’s annual summary showing available grant and bond room, issued each February.
