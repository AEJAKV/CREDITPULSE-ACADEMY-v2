## Start here

[paragraph] Reselling can begin with one useful product and a sensible margin. It can also begin with 80 novelty mugs arriving at your door like a ceramic intervention. This course builds the first version.

[paragraph] A reseller buys or acquires products and sells them for more than the full cost of getting them ready for a customer. The work is not simply finding a low purchase price. It includes checking demand, authenticity, safety, condition, fees, packaging, delivery, returns and records. A profitable listing is a calculation—not a hopeful adjective.

[spotlight] Choose one narrow category first. Familiarity makes it easier to recognize normal prices, defects, counterfeits, recalls and slow-moving stock.

[chips]
  - Select a product category and buyer.
  - Verify demand, authenticity and safety.
  - Calculate profit after every unit cost.
  - Run a small test batch with a stop rule.
  - Complete a Product-to-Profit Test Card.

[myths]
  - “Anything bought cheaply can be resold profitably.” — A bargain without demand is inventory wearing a party hat. Profit depends on what actually sells, how quickly it sells and what the full transaction costs.

## Start With Evidence, Not a Shopping Mood

[paragraph] A shopping mood buys inventory. Evidence buys a business.

[cards]
  - DEFINE THE BUYER — Name the person, need and price range. A category such as tested entry-level kitchen appliances is easier to research than “things people might enjoy.”
  - STUDY COMPLETED DEMAND — Compare recent sold results where available, not only asking prices. Note typical condition, selling time, delivery method and seasonal changes.
  - VERIFY THE SOURCE — Keep purchase records and check brands, model numbers, packaging and serial information. If authenticity is doubtful, do not advertise the item as genuine.
  - CHECK SAFETY — Search Health Canada recalls and current product requirements. Used products are still consumer products; a recalled or dangerous item does not become safe because it has a charming vintage description.

[spotlight] Health Canada’s consumer-product rules can apply to used goods. Never sell a recalled product unless the corrective action has been completed and resale is permitted.

[spotlight] Counterfeit goods can create legal, safety and refund problems. A low price and a heroic story about a cousin’s warehouse are not proof of authenticity.

[myths]
  - “If another seller lists it for $100, mine is worth $100.” — A listing shows an invitation, not a completed sale. Compare actual sold evidence, condition and time-to-sale before choosing a price.

[source] Health Canada: Industry guide to second-hand products
  url: https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/consumer-product-safety/reports-publications/general-safety/guidance-second-hand-products-industry.html

## Find the Profit Hidden Behind the Price Tag

[paragraph] Estimate unit profit by starting with the expected selling price, then subtract purchase cost, platform and payment fees, cleaning or repair, packaging, shipping or delivery, advertising and a reasonable allowance for returns or losses.

[ordered]
  - S — e
  - T — e
  - W — r
  - D — e

[spotlight] Revenue is the money received; profit is what remains after costs. Your bank balance may look enthusiastic before fees, inventory and tax have had their turn.

[spotlight] Inventory records support cost-of-goods-sold calculations. Track the item, purchase date, source, cost, sale date, selling price and related expenses.

[myths]
  - “Small online sales do not need to be reported.” — CRA says income earned from peer-to-peer sales must be reported. Use Form T2125 for self-employment business income and keep supporting records.

[spotlight] Key takeaway: A repeatable resale business begins only after demand, legitimacy, safety and full unit economics are verified. Test small, record actual results and reorder from evidence—not excitement.

## Priya's Thirty-Tumbler Test

[story] Meet Priya
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ Priya Desai saw insulated tumblers listed for $38 and bought 30 at $19 each. By Tuesday, the boxes had formed a small municipal council in her hallway. Her spreadsheet, unfortunately, had only two columns: “cost” and “future riches.”
  ¶ The conflict appeared after her first sales. Payment fees, packaging and delivery reduced the margin; two lids leaked; and the $38 competitors were mostly asking prices, not sold prices. Priya also lacked clear supplier documents for the brand claim.
  ¶ She paused the listings, refunded the affected customers and verified the product source and safety. For her next category, she bought six unbranded desk organizers, calculated every cost, set a 30-day stop rule and recorded actual selling time. Four sold at the target margin, so she reordered ten—not 100.
  ¶ Priya did not abandon reselling. She replaced warehouse optimism with a controlled experiment, and her hallway returned to civilian government.

[spotlight] A test batch answers three questions cheaply: will it sell, at what true profit and with what problems? Reordering is earned by results.

## Quick Check: Choose the Best Next Move

[accordions]
  - Why should a reseller study sold results instead of asking prices? — A listing price is only an invitation to buy; sold results show what buyers actually paid, in what condition and how quickly.
  - Which costs belong in a true unit-profit calculation? — Purchase cost, platform and payment fees, cleaning or repair, packaging, shipping or delivery, advertising and a reasonable allowance for returns or losses.
  - What should happen before a used product is offered for sale? — Its safety, recall status and authenticity should be checked—a recalled or dangerous item is not safe simply because it looks vintage.
  - Why does a small test batch reduce risk? — It answers whether the product will sell, at what true profit and with what problems, before committing to a larger order.
  - When can GST/HST registration become required? — Generally after taxable supplies exceed $30,000 in one calendar quarter or across four consecutive calendar quarters.

## Glossary of Terms

[glossary]
  - Cost of goods sold — Inventory cost connected with products sold during the period.
  - Gross revenue — Total sales before expenses.
  - Unit profit — Expected sale price minus all costs attached to one item.
  - Sell-through — Share of available inventory sold during a period.
  - Test batch — Small first order used to measure real demand and problems.
  - Recall — Official action concerning a product with a safety issue.
  - Counterfeit — Unauthorized imitation presented as a genuine product.
