## Start here

[paragraph] Nowhere else in personal finance can you put in a dollar and have the government hand you back twenty cents, guaranteed, no market risk involved.

[paragraph] That’s exactly what happens with an RESP — a Registered Education Savings Plan — for anyone saving toward a child’s future education. And yet a meaningful share of eligible families never claim it, simply because nobody explained how the matching actually works.

[paragraph] This module fixes that.

## The basic shape of an RESP

[paragraph] An RESP is an account you open for a child — usually a parent or grandparent does this. You contribute money. It grows inside the account, sheltered from tax. And the government adds free money on top, through a grant called the CESG.

[paragraph] When the child heads off to college, university, or an apprenticeship program, the money comes out to help pay for it.

[paragraph] Contributions themselves aren’t tax-deductible, unlike an RRSP. But growth inside the account is tax-sheltered, and the CESG is where the real value lives.

## The CESG: the twenty cents on every dollar

[paragraph] The Canada Education Savings Grant matches 20% of what you contribute, up to a limit.

[paragraph] Contribute $2,500 in a year, and the government adds $500 on top. That’s the sweet spot — the most CESG you can earn in a single year from a normal contribution.

[paragraph] There’s a lifetime cap too. Once a child has received $7,200 in CESG, the grants stop, no matter how much more you contribute after that.

[myths]
  - “Contributing a big lump sum early gets you more grant money faster.” — It’s the opposite. The CESG only matches the first $2,500 contributed per year. Deposit $15,000 in one year, and you still only earn $500 in grant — the same as depositing $2,500. The other $12,500 earned nothing. Spreading contributions out over several years is how you actually capture the full $7,200.

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: THE EXTRA GRANT LAYERED ON TOP OF THE 20%

[spotlight] The base 20% isn’t the whole match for every family. Households below certain income thresholds qualify for the Additional CESG — an extra 10% or 20% on top of the base rate, but only on the first $500 contributed in a year. For a lower-income family, that can push the effective match on that first slice to 30% or 40%, not the usual 20%. It doesn’t change the $7,200 lifetime cap or the $500-per-year base grant most families plan around, but it means the first $500 you put in each year can be worth noticeably more than the standard math suggests. Check your eligibility with the RESP provider when you open the account — it’s applied automatically once income is on file, not something you have to separately request each year.

## Behind on contributions? There’s a catch-up rule

[paragraph] Missed a few years, or started late? The unused grant room doesn’t just vanish.

[paragraph] It carries forward. You can catch up by contributing more in a single year — up to $5,000 — which earns up to $1,000 in CESG that year instead of the usual $500. One year of current room, plus one year of carried-forward room, combined.

[paragraph] You can’t catch up faster than that, even with years of room built up. The most CESG any single year can earn is $1,000, regardless of how far behind you are.

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: FREE MONEY WITH NO CONTRIBUTION REQUIRED

[spotlight] There’s a second grant worth knowing about, separate from the CESG, and it requires nothing from you except opening the account. The Canada Learning Bond is aimed at lower-income families and pays money into an RESP without any contribution needed at all. If your family income falls under the threshold, the government deposits money simply because the account exists and the child qualifies. This is the RESP equivalent of money sitting on the sidewalk. Families sometimes assume RESPs are only worth opening if they can contribute regularly — but for lower-income households, opening the account alone can be worth real money, whether or not a single dollar of your own goes in.

## What happens if the child doesn’t pursue further education

[paragraph] This is the question that stops some families from opening an RESP in the first place. It’s worth answering clearly.

[paragraph] Your own contributions always come back to you, tax-free, regardless of what happens. That part is never at risk.

[paragraph] The government grants are different — if the beneficiary doesn’t end up pursuing a qualifying program, the CESG and other grants have to be repaid to the government. Investment growth can sometimes be transferred into your own RRSP instead, if you have the room, or withdrawn with additional tax owing.

[paragraph] In practice, RESPs are flexible. A qualifying program covers a wide range — university, college, trade school, and apprenticeships. Plans can also be transferred between siblings in many cases. The all-or-nothing fear is usually bigger than the real risk.

[myths]
  - “If my child doesn’t go to university, I lose everything I put into the RESP.” — Your own contributions come back to you either way, tax-free. It’s only the government grants that need repaying if the child never pursues a qualifying program — and “qualifying” covers far more than a four-year degree, including trades and apprenticeships.

## Getting the most from an RESP

[chips]
  - I’ve opened an RESP for my child, even if I can only contribute a small amount right now.
  - I’m contributing close to $2,500 a year, if I can, to capture the full annual CESG.
  - I know my family’s income might qualify for the Canada Learning Bond, at no contribution required.
  - If I’m behind on contributions, I know the catch-up rule and its $1,000-per-year cap.
  - I’m not assuming a lump-sum contribution is more efficient than spreading it out — it isn’t, for CESG purposes.

## The Okafor family’s first RESP year

[story] The Okafor family’s first RESP year
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ When their daughter was born, the Okafors received a small windfall from a relative — six thousand dollars, earmarked loosely for “the baby’s future.” Their instinct was to deposit the whole amount into a new RESP right away, get it working, and move on.
  ¶ A friend who worked in banking asked one question before they did: how much of that six thousand did they plan to contribute every year after this one? The Okafors hadn’t really thought that far ahead.
  ¶ She walked them through the CESG math. Depositing all six thousand in year one would earn $500 in grant — the annual cap — and leave $3,500 sitting there having earned nothing extra. Spreading it instead, at $2,500 this year and holding the rest for future years, would eventually pull in more total grant money over time.
  ¶ They split it: $2,500 in the first year, and the rest held back to fund $2,500 contributions in each of the following two years. Small change in behaviour. Real difference in how much free government money their daughter’s education fund actually captured.

[takeaway] The CESG matches 20% of RESP contributions, up to $500 a year and $7,200 over a child’s lifetime — but only on the first $2,500 contributed annually, so spreading contributions out captures more grant than one large deposit. Lower-income families may also qualify for the Canada Learning Bond with no contribution required. And your own contributions are never at risk, even if the child’s path changes.

## Glossary

[glossary]
  - RESP — Registered Education Savings Plan. A tax-sheltered account for a child’s future education.
  - CESG — Canada Education Savings Grant. A 20% government match on RESP contributions, up to annual and lifetime limits.
  - Canada Learning Bond — A grant for lower-income families, paid with no contribution required.
  - Subscriber — The person who opens and contributes to the RESP, usually a parent or grandparent.
  - Beneficiary — The child the RESP is set up for.
  - Qualifying program — An eligible post-secondary path, including university, college, trade school, and apprenticeships.
