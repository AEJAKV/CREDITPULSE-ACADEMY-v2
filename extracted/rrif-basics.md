## Start here

[paragraph] An RRSP is for building up. A RRIF is for drawing down.

[paragraph] They hold the same kinds of investments. They both shelter growth from tax. The difference is direction: with an RRSP you put money in, and with a RRIF you must take money out.

[paragraph] At the end of the year you turn 71, that switch happens whether you’re ready or not. Understanding it in advance is what separates a smooth transition from an expensive one.

## The deadline nobody can extend

[paragraph] By December 31 of the year you turn 71, your RRSP has to become something else. You have three options.

[chips]
  - Convert it to a RRIF. What most Canadians do. You keep your investments, keep the tax shelter, and start taking mandatory withdrawals.
  - Buy an annuity. Guaranteed income for life, but you give up flexibility and control.
  - Withdraw everything in cash. Almost never the right choice. The entire balance becomes taxable income in one year, usually at the highest rate you’ll ever pay.

[paragraph] Miss the deadline entirely and the third option happens by default. That’s the outcome worth planning to avoid.

[myths]
  - “A RRIF is a completely different investment than my RRSP.” — It’s the same account contents, under new rules. Your stocks, funds and GICs can carry straight over. What changes is that contributions stop, and mandatory annual withdrawals begin.

## The mandatory minimum, and how it grows

[paragraph] Every year, you must withdraw at least a set percentage of your RRIF’s value as of January 1.

[paragraph] The percentage is set by federal regulation and rises with your age. At 71 it’s 5.28%. At 75, 5.82%. At 80, 6.82%. By 95 and beyond, it’s 20%.

[paragraph] There’s no maximum — you can always take out more than the minimum. And in the calendar year you actually open the RRIF, there’s no required withdrawal at all.

[paragraph] Every dollar withdrawn is fully taxable as regular income in the year you take it. That’s the key thing to plan around — and it’s what the rest of this module is about.

## Why mandatory withdrawals can cost you twice

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: WHY MANDATORY WITHDRAWALS CAN COST YOU TWICE

[spotlight] RRIF withdrawals count as income. That matters beyond just the tax bill itself. Old Age Security starts getting clawed back once your net income crosses a threshold — roughly $91,000 in 2026. Large mandatory RRIF withdrawals, stacked on top of CPP and any pension income, can push a retiree over that line and trigger an extra 15% recovery tax on their OAS. The frustrating part is that the withdrawal wasn’t optional. The minimum is mandatory, and the percentage climbs every year regardless of whether you need the money. This is why many Canadians plan their drawdown long before 71 — taking money out of an RRSP during lower-income early retirement years, when the tax cost is smaller, to reduce the balance that will eventually force those larger mandatory withdrawals.

## Three things worth knowing before you convert

[paragraph] You can use your younger spouse’s age. If your spouse is younger, you can elect to base your minimum withdrawals on their age instead of yours. Lower percentage, smaller mandatory withdrawal, more money left growing tax-deferred. This election is made when the RRIF is set up, so it’s worth raising before conversion rather than after.

[paragraph] Converting at 65 opens two doors. You don’t have to wait until 71. Converting some or all of an RRSP at 65 can qualify you for the pension income tax credit, and allows pension income splitting with a spouse — both of which can reduce a household’s total tax.

[paragraph] Withdrawing more than the minimum is sometimes smart. In a low-income year, taking out more than required and moving the surplus into a TFSA can smooth your lifetime tax bill. Money in a TFSA grows tax-free and, unlike a RRIF withdrawal, doesn’t count as income later.

[myths]
  - “I should always withdraw the bare minimum to keep taxes low.” — Not always. Minimum withdrawals leave a larger balance behind, which forces bigger mandatory withdrawals later — and whatever remains at death is generally taxed heavily unless it goes to a spouse. Sometimes taking more in a low-income year costs less over a lifetime than taking the minimum every year.

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: WHAT HAPPENS TO A RRIF WHEN YOU DIE

[spotlight] There’s a fourth thing worth knowing, and it rarely comes up until it’s urgent. Without any planning, a RRIF’s full value generally gets added to your income on your final tax return the year you die — often the single largest tax bill your estate will ever face. There’s a fix, and it mirrors the TFSA successor idea from Module 2.5. Name a spouse or common-law partner as successor annuitant, not merely a beneficiary, and the RRIF simply continues in their name. No deemed withdrawal, no tax triggered, nothing collapses — the account just carries on as though ownership quietly changed hands. If there’s a financially dependent child or grandchild with a disability instead, the money can often roll into their RDSP or RRSP rather than becoming an immediate taxable lump sum. Check the designation on file with your RRIF provider; a plain beneficiary and a successor annuitant sound similar and are treated very differently.

## Approaching conversion

[chips]
  - I know my RRSP must be converted by December 31 of the year I turn 71.
  - I understand every RRIF withdrawal is fully taxable as income.
  - I’ve considered whether converting earlier, at 65, would help with pension splitting or the pension income credit.
  - If my spouse is younger, I know I can base minimum withdrawals on their age — but only if elected at setup.
  - I’ve thought about whether large mandatory withdrawals later could push me into OAS clawback territory.

## Bernard’s early start

[story] Bernard’s early start
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ Bernard retired at sixty-three with a healthy RRSP and no immediate need to touch it. His instinct was to leave it alone — let it grow, untouched, until the rules forced him to act at seventy-one.
  ¶ His advisor ran the numbers differently. Between sixty-three and seventy, Bernard’s income would be relatively low: no employment income, CPP not yet started, OAS still ahead. Those were the cheapest years he would ever have to pull money out of an RRSP.
  ¶ Leaving the balance untouched meant a much larger account at seventy-one, which meant larger mandatory withdrawals every year afterward, stacked on top of CPP and OAS — and quite possibly enough to trigger the OAS clawback in his late seventies.
  ¶ Bernard started drawing modest amounts at sixty-four, paying tax at a low rate, and moving what he didn’t need into his TFSA. By seventy-one his RRIF was smaller, his mandatory minimums were manageable, and his OAS stayed intact. Nothing dramatic happened. He just started seven years earlier than the rules required.

[takeaway] Your RRSP must become a RRIF, an annuity, or cash by December 31 of the year you turn 71 — and cashing out is almost never right. RRIF withdrawals are mandatory, rise as a percentage with age, and are fully taxable as income. Because they can push you into OAS clawback territory, many Canadians benefit from drawing down an RRSP in lower-income years well before 71.

## Glossary

[glossary]
  - RRIF — Registered Retirement Income Fund. The account an RRSP converts into, with mandatory annual withdrawals.
  - Minimum withdrawal — The percentage of your January 1 balance you must take out each year, rising with age.
  - OAS clawback — The recovery tax on Old Age Security once net income crosses a threshold.
  - Younger-spouse election — Basing your minimum withdrawals on a younger spouse’s age, reducing the required amount.
  - Pension income splitting — Sharing eligible pension income with a spouse to reduce a household’s total tax.
