## Start here

[paragraph] An RRSP is a trade.

[paragraph] You give up some income now. In exchange, you get a tax break today, and decades of tax-free growth in the meantime. The bill only comes due later — usually in retirement, when your income, and your tax rate, are often much lower.

[paragraph] That’s the whole idea. Everything else in this module is detail.

## The mechanics, one step at a time

[paragraph] You contribute, and your taxable income drops. Money you put into an RRSP is deducted from your income for that year. Remember the shelf idea from Module 2.2? An RRSP contribution effectively removes income from your top shelf — the one taxed at your highest rate.

[paragraph] It grows, untouched by tax. Inside the account, your investments grow completely tax-sheltered. No tax on the growth, the dividends, or the interest, year after year, for as long as the money stays in the account.

[paragraph] You pay tax when you take it out. This is the part people sometimes forget. An RRSP isn’t tax-free — it’s tax-deferred. When you withdraw, that money gets added to your income and taxed at whatever rate applies then.

[paragraph] The bet built into an RRSP is simple: your tax rate in retirement will likely be lower than it is during your working years. For most people, that bet pays off.

[myths]
  - “An RRSP is basically the same as a TFSA, just with a different name.” — They work in opposite directions. An RRSP gives you a deduction now and taxes you later. A TFSA gives you no deduction now, but never taxes you again. Confusing the two can lead to a nasty surprise — like assuming an RRSP withdrawal is tax-free, when it isn’t.

## How much room you actually have

[paragraph] Every year, you build new RRSP room — 18% of what you earned the previous year, up to a yearly cap. For 2026, that cap is $33,810.

[paragraph] Most people never get close to that cap. It only matters once your earned income crosses roughly $188,000 the year before.

[paragraph] Room that goes unused doesn’t disappear. It carries forward, forever, stacking up year after year if you don’t use it.

## The 60-day window everyone half-remembers

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: THE 60-DAY WINDOW EVERYONE HALF-REMEMBERS

[spotlight] Contributions made in the first 60 days of a new year come with a choice. You can apply them to last year’s tax return, or this year’s — whichever helps more. This matters most for anyone who got a late-year bonus, or whose income jumped partway through the year. Contribute in January or February, and you can still shrink last year’s taxable income, even though the calendar has already turned. The deadline for the 2025 tax year, for example, lands in early March 2026. Miss it, and that contribution simply applies to the current year instead — not a disaster, just a missed opportunity to choose.

## When an RRSP contribution is worth the most

[paragraph] Not every year is equal. Because the deduction is worth more on a higher shelf, an RRSP contribution saves you more tax in a high-earning year than in a low one.

[paragraph] This means timing matters as much as the amount. Someone in an early career, low-income year might get more value saving in a TFSA instead, and using RRSP room later — once their income, and their shelf, has climbed.

[paragraph] There’s no universal right answer here. It depends entirely on where you sit now, and where you expect to sit later.

[myths]
  - “You should always contribute to your RRSP as early as possible in your career.” — Not necessarily. The deduction is worth more in higher-earning years. Someone on a low shelf early in their career may get more value building room and using it later, once their income — and their tax rate — has grown.

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: THE SPOUSAL RRSP NOBODY EXPLAINS

[spotlight] The shelf idea doesn’t stop at one person. If you have a spouse or common-law partner, you can contribute to a spousal RRSP — a plan registered in their name, funded from your own contribution room. You still get the deduction. They own the account, and they’re the one taxed on it, years from now, when it’s withdrawn. For a couple with a large income gap, this is a quiet way to even out retirement shelves: instead of one large RRSP taxed entirely in the higher earner’s hands later, both partners build a balance and both draw income at a lower rate in retirement than one person alone would pay on the combined total. There’s a wrinkle worth knowing — withdraw too soon after a spousal contribution, generally within three calendar years, and the money can get taxed back in the contributor’s hands instead. Used with that timing in mind, it’s one of the more overlooked ways a couple lowers their household tax bill over a lifetime, not just one return.

## Before you contribute

[chips]
  - I know my current RRSP room, checked in my CRA account, not estimated.
  - I know roughly which shelf I’m on this year, and whether that makes an RRSP deduction especially valuable right now.
  - I understand withdrawals are taxed as income later — this isn’t free money, just deferred tax.
  - If I’m contributing in January or February, I know I can choose which tax year it applies to.
  - I haven’t confused my RRSP with my TFSA — they work in opposite directions.

## Priya’s two decisions

[story] Priya’s two decisions
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ Priya started her first job at twenty-two, earning $42,000. A well-meaning uncle told her to start maxing out her RRSP immediately — the earlier, the better, he said.
  ¶ She put in $3,000 that first year. The deduction barely moved her tax bill — she was sitting on the lowest shelf already, and there wasn’t much tax there to shield.
  ¶ Eight years later, promoted twice, earning $95,000, she remembered that uncle’s advice and nearly skipped contributing that year too — assuming she’d already done her RRSP thing back at twenty-two. Her financial advisor pointed out the opposite was true: this was exactly the year the deduction would do real work, since a chunk of her income now sat well above the lowest shelf.
  ¶ She contributed $8,000 that year. The tax saved was more than double what the same dollar amount would have saved her at twenty-two. Same account. Same rules. Completely different value, because the shelf underneath it had changed.

[takeaway] An RRSP gives you a tax deduction now and taxes you later, ideally at a lower rate in retirement. Room builds every year and never expires. The deduction is worth more in high-earning years, so timing your contributions to your highest shelves often beats simply contributing early and often.

## Glossary

[glossary]
  - RRSP deduction limit — The total amount you can contribute and deduct, combining this year’s new room with any unused room carried forward.
  - Earned income — Employment and self-employment income, the basis for calculating new RRSP room. Investment income doesn’t count.
  - Pension adjustment — A reduction to your RRSP room if you also belong to a workplace pension plan.
  - Tax-deferred — Not tax-free — tax is postponed until withdrawal, not eliminated.
