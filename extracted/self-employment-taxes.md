## Start here

[paragraph] Self-employment can mean freedom, extra income, or a business that quietly grows into a full-time job. It also changes how tax reaches the government — there's no employer handling deductions in the background anymore. You're the one reporting income, backing up expenses, planning for tax and CPP or QPP, and keeping records that still make sense months later.

[paragraph] That sounds heavier than it needs to be. This course isn't trying to turn you into an accountant. It's a simple system for capturing the right information, setting money aside, and knowing when it's worth paying a professional.

[paragraph] This is general Canadian education for sole proprietors and self-employed individuals, not personal tax, legal, accounting or business advice. Rules, deadlines and provincial requirements change — confirm anything time-sensitive with the CRA, Revenu Québec where it applies, and a qualified professional. You'll build one clean business tax file as you go, aiming to replace the annual shoebox ceremony with a routine that takes minutes, not a weekend and three increasingly worried coffees.

[chips]
  - Tell gross business income apart from net business income.
  - Identify the common records used to fill out Form T2125.
  - Separate personal spending from reasonable business expenses.
  - Explain why income tax and CPP or QPP both need advance planning.
  - Recognize when tax instalments or GST/HST registration might apply.
  - Build a simple monthly record routine.
  - Identify questions that actually need professional advice.

[myths]
  - “A side hustle is too small to report.” — Business income doesn't get a costume labelled 'hobby money' just because it landed after regular work hours. Report it, then work out which supported expenses are deductible.

## Know What the Business Earned

[paragraph] Self-employment income can come from consulting, contracting, freelancing, online sales, repair work, caregiving, creative work, delivery, commissions — pretty much any activity carried on for profit. A business doesn't become invisible just because it's part-time, paid through an app, or run from a kitchen table.

[paragraph] Start with two different numbers. Gross income is everything the business brought in before expenses. Net income is what's left after eligible business expenses come off. Gross shows the size of the activity; net is generally what gets used for taxable income and self-employed CPP contributions.

[paragraph] Record every sale or fee earned — invoices, platform statements, payment processor reports, bank deposits, cash-sale records. If a client pays in another currency, keep the original invoice, the Canadian-dollar amount you reported, and the exchange-rate source or method.

[paragraph] Don't report only what landed in the bank after a platform took its cut. Record the full customer payment as income, and the supported platform fee as its own expense. Netting the two together hides the real transaction.

[spotlight] A separate business bank account isn't always legally required for a sole proprietor, but it makes records much clearer — one less argument with yourself about whether a hardware-store charge was for shelving or a suspiciously ambitious barbecue.

[heading] Employee or self-employed?

[paragraph] The label in a contract isn't the whole answer. What actually matters is the working relationship — control over the work, who owns the tools, opportunity for profit, risk of loss, integration, and whether you can hire help to do it. Quebec runs its own related framework for provincial purposes.

[paragraph] Getting this wrong can affect income tax, CPP or QPP, Employment Insurance, payroll obligations and employment rights. If it's unclear, ask for professional advice or request an official ruling. A confident title on an invoice doesn't settle the law.

## Give Incoming Money Four Destinations

[paragraph] A sole proprietor and the business are generally the same taxpayer. Moving money from the business account to your personal account is normally just an owner's withdrawal, not a deductible salary expense — the business result comes from income and eligible expenses, not from how much you transferred home.

[cards]
  - OPERATING MONEY — Pays the ordinary costs of running the business — supplies, tools, ads, the everyday bills.
  - TAX RESERVE MONEY — Sets aside what income tax and CPP or QPP will eventually need. This is the one that keeps a good year from turning into a rough April.
  - GST/HST MONEY — Once you're registered, this holds tax collected from customers. It was never yours to spend — you're just holding it.
  - OWNER MONEY — What's actually available for personal use, after the first three lanes have already been considered.

[paragraph] These don't need to be four separate bank accounts. They do need to be four separate amounts in your records. Collected GST/HST is never profit. Tax reserve money shouldn't turn into emergency marketing money because an ad promised to "change everything by Tuesday."

[paragraph] Set up a regular transfer to the tax reserve every time a customer pays you. The right percentage depends on total income, your province or territory, deductions, credits and whether other employment already withheld tax — a qualified tax professional can help estimate it. The habit that matters is reserving the money before the personal-spending decision gets made.

## Claim the Business Part, Not the Hopeful Part

[paragraph] Form T2125, Statement of Business or Professional Activities, is what sole proprietors and partners commonly use to report business or professional income and expenses with the T1 return. It organizes gross income, expense categories and net income.

[paragraph] The general idea is simple: a reasonable current expense incurred to earn business income may be deductible, subject to specific limits and rules. Personal expenses aren't deductible. Capital property — equipment expected to provide a lasting benefit — usually gets handled through capital cost allowance instead of being deducted all at once.

[paragraph] Common categories include advertising, business insurance, office costs, professional fees, supplies, eligible travel, bank charges, interest, rent, wages, delivery costs, and phone or internet use. The category name doesn't make a claim valid — the business purpose and the supporting record do.

[cards]
  - MIXED PERSONAL AND BUSINESS USE — When a cost serves both purposes, claim only the supported business portion. For a phone or internet plan, use a reasonable method based on actual business use, and write the method down so next year's calculation doesn't depend on memory and optimism.
  - CURRENT EXPENSES VS. CAPITAL PROPERTY — An ordinary supply usually gets used up in the current business cycle. Capital property normally lasts longer and supports the business over time — a printer cartridge and the printer itself can get different tax treatment. Record the date, description, seller, cost, tax paid and business use, and let the facts decide.

[myths]
  - “If I have a receipt, the expense is deductible.” — A receipt proves money changed hands. It doesn't prove the purchase had a business purpose. A very crisp receipt for a personal television is still a personal television.

[paragraph] Business-use-of-home expenses follow their own conditions and calculations — the workspace may need to be your principal place of business, or used only to earn business income and regularly meet clients, customers or patients. Use the T2125 calculation and get advice before claiming a room simply because a laptop once visited it.

[paragraph] Vehicle claims need evidence too — total kilometres, business kilometres, trip dates, destinations, business purposes, plus operating receipts. Driving from home to a regular place of business may be personal commuting, while travel between business locations can be treated differently. The facts matter.

[spotlight] The CRA permits a simplified vehicle-logbook approach in qualifying circumstances, but only after a full base year has been established. Don't start with the shortcut — confirm you meet the conditions and keep the base-year evidence first.

## Malik Creates Tax Friday

[story] Meet Malik
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ Malik repairs bicycles on weekends and earns design income during the week. Customers pay by e-transfer, cash, and an online platform. In his first year, he watched the bank balance and assumed it was profit — by spring he had income showing up in three places, receipts scattered across five, and nothing set aside for tax.
  ¶ He didn't rebuild the business around complicated software. He opened a separate chequing account, made digital folders for sales and expenses, and picked the first Friday of every month as Tax Friday. On that day, he'd download platform statements, match deposits to invoices, photograph paper receipts, log his kilometres, and move money into a tax reserve.
  ¶ He also went back through his expense claims. Personal meals came out. His phone and vehicle costs got split into actual business and personal portions. When he bought professional repair equipment, he flagged it for review instead of assuming the whole cost was deductible right away.
  ¶ At year-end, Malik still needed a tax professional — but he showed up with organized records and specific questions instead of a shoebox. The meeting was about decisions, not archaeology.

[heading] What actually changed?

[paragraph] The new system took less than an hour a month. More importantly, his bank balance stopped pretending to be four different numbers at once.

## Plan for Tax Before the Deadlines Arrive

[paragraph] Self-employed individuals generally get a later filing deadline than most individual taxpayers, but the balance owing is usually due earlier. For the 2025 return, the standard self-employed filing deadline was June 15, 2026, while payment of a balance owing was due April 30, 2026. These dates shift — verify the current year's before acting.

[myths]
  - “Self-employed people can wait until the June filing deadline to pay.” — Filing and payment run on different clocks. A later filing deadline doesn't normally move the balance-due date.

[paragraph] File on time even if you can't pay in full. Interest and late-filing consequences are separate issues, and the CRA may offer payment arrangements. Silence is not a payment strategy.

[heading] CPP or QPP contributions

[paragraph] An employee and employer normally split CPP contributions. A self-employed person generally pays both portions through their personal income tax return, based on net business income within the applicable limits. Quebec residents generally use QPP rules instead — one reason a tax reserve needs more room than a rough income-tax estimate alone might suggest.

[spotlight] Self-employed CPP is calculated on net business income, not on every dollar deposited. Expenses can reduce net income, but they don't turn an unnecessary purchase into free money — spending one dollar to avoid tax on part of that dollar is still spending.

[heading] Income tax instalments

[paragraph] The CRA may require quarterly instalments when not enough tax gets withheld during the year and the net-tax-owing tests are met. Instalments are advance payments toward the year's tax, not an extra tax. If the CRA sends a reminder, review it — the notice can offer prior-year or current-year estimate options, and interest may apply if a lower current-year estimate turns out too low. Keep proof of every payment.

[spotlight] Add income-tax instalment dates to the business calendar separately from GST/HST filing dates. Two government deadlines can share a month without becoming the same obligation — calendars enjoy this sort of practical clarity.

[paragraph] Income tax registration and GST/HST registration are two different questions. A self-employed person can owe income tax without being registered for GST/HST — registration depends on the type of supplies, revenue tests, location and special rules. That decision gets its own course.

## Build Records That Answer Three Questions

[paragraph] A useful record system should answer three things.

[chips]
  - What income did the business earn?
  - What supported costs were incurred to earn it?
  - What tax payments, instalments or amounts collected connect to the period?

[paragraph] Create a folder for each tax year, and inside it separate sales, expenses, bank and payment-processor statements, vehicle logs, home-office calculations, equipment purchases, contracts, tax payments and filed returns. Use filenames that include a date, the supplier or customer, and the purpose.

[paragraph] Reconcile the records against the business bank and platform statements every month. A missing receipt is easier to chase down after thirty days than after eleven months. If a supplier never gave you a receipt, record their name and address, the amount, date and transaction details in an expense journal, and keep whatever other proof you have.

[myths]
  - “Bookkeeping software makes the records correct.” — Software can total what it's given. It can't know a deposit is missing, a personal purchase got mislabelled, or the same receipt got uploaded twice. Reconciliation is the part where a human checks whether the tidy screen matches real life.

[paragraph] The CRA generally wants business records and supporting documents kept for six years from the end of the last tax year they relate to, with special rules in some situations. Electronic records need to stay readable and accessible — a faded receipt photographed today beats a blank strip of thermal paper next year.

[spotlight] Cloud storage is a backup location, not a filing method by itself. A folder called "Tax stuff FINAL final 2" may be emotionally honest, but it won't explain which invoice belongs to which sale.

[paragraph] Protect the sensitive stuff — strong passwords, multi-factor authentication, limited access to full account numbers, SINs, customer data and tax documents. A bookkeeper or software provider can help maintain records, but you're still the one responsible for having them ready when required.

## What Changes by Province or Territory

[paragraph] Federal income tax and CPP apply across most of Canada, but provincial or territorial income tax changes the final number. Quebec residents generally deal with Revenu Québec for provincial income tax and QPP, often with separate forms and deadlines alongside the federal ones.

[paragraph] Business registration, licences, workers' compensation, payroll and sales taxes can vary too. British Columbia, Saskatchewan, Manitoba and Quebec run their own provincial sales-tax systems separate from GST. Other provinces use HST, while Alberta and the territories skip a general provincial sales tax entirely. The product or service, the customer's location and your business presence can all change the answer.

[paragraph] Record your main business location, every province or territory where you regularly work or sell, and whether you have employees, inventory or property elsewhere. Take that list to a qualified adviser — a business can cross a tax boundary online without anyone packing a suitcase.

[heading] Key takeaway

[paragraph] Self-employment tax becomes manageable once money and records get clear jobs. Capture gross income, claim only the supported business portion of expenses, reserve money for income tax and CPP or QPP, watch for instalment and GST/HST obligations, and close the records every month. Good organization doesn't remove tax — it removes the avoidable surprises.

[spotlight] Close each month before starting the next one — confirm sales, categorize expenses, review unpaid invoices, update mileage, transfer the tax reserve, and note one question for follow-up. A twelve-minute checklist used regularly beats a beautiful annual one used never.

## Quick Check: Test What Stuck

[accordions]
  - What's the actual difference between gross and net business income? — Gross is total business income before expenses. Net is what's left after eligible business expenses come off.
  - Why isn't an owner's personal withdrawal a deductible salary expense for a sole proprietor? — A sole proprietor and the business are generally the same taxpayer — the business result comes from income and eligible expenses, not from how much got transferred to the owner.
  - What does a receipt actually prove, and what doesn't it prove? — It proves a purchase happened. It doesn't by itself prove a business purpose, or that the full amount is deductible.
  - Why can the filing deadline and the payment deadline cause confusion? — Self-employed individuals may get a later return-filing deadline while still owing a balance by the ordinary payment deadline — the two clocks run separately.
  - Name three records that can support a business vehicle claim. — Examples include total kilometres, business kilometres, trip dates, destinations, business purposes, and operating-expense receipts.

## Glossary of Terms

[glossary]
  - Capital cost allowance (CCA) — The tax system used to deduct the cost of eligible depreciable property over time.
  - Current expense — An ordinary expense generally connected to earning income in the current business period, subject to tax rules.
  - Gross business income — Total business revenue before eligible expenses are deducted.
  - Income tax instalment — A periodic advance payment toward income tax that may be required when insufficient tax is withheld during the year.
  - Mixed-use expense — A cost that serves both business and personal purposes.
  - Net business income — Business income remaining after eligible expenses; it is used in calculating taxable income and self-employed CPP contributions.
  - Owner's withdrawal — Money a sole proprietor takes from the business for personal use; it is generally not a deductible business expense.
  - Self-employed person — An individual carrying on business on their own account rather than working as an employee in that activity.
  - Sole proprietor — An individual who owns an unincorporated business and generally reports its results on a personal tax return.
  - T2125 — The CRA form commonly used to report business or professional income and expenses.
