## Start here

[paragraph] You would never leave the house in a Canadian February wearing just a sweater. A senior renovation works the same way. One program is never the whole outfit, no matter how good that one program is.

[paragraph] Most people find one program, apply, and stop. That is usually where the money gets left behind.

[heading] What this course does

[paragraph] There are three kinds of help: money back at tax time, grants and loans from your province or city, and a certificate that quietly unlocks more of both. This module walks through all three, in the order that actually works.

## Layer one: money back at tax time

[paragraph] Two federal tax credits help pay for a renovation. People mix them up constantly, so here they are, one at a time.

[paragraph] The Home Accessibility Tax Credit (HATC) helps with permanent safety changes: a walk-in shower, a ramp, grab bars, a stair lift, wider doorways. It is for anyone 65 or older, or anyone approved for the Disability Tax Credit. A family member who pays for the work can claim it instead.

[paragraph] The Multigenerational Home Renovation Tax Credit (MHRTC) is a different animal. It helps pay to build a small, separate apartment, complete with its own front door, kitchen, bathroom and bedroom, so a senior can live with family. A nicely finished spare room does not count. It needs its own door.

[paragraph] Both credits show up the spring after the work is done, not when you pay the contractor. That gap between paying and getting paid back is where most of the trouble starts.

[cards]
  - What it pays for — Home Accessibility (HATC): Permanent safety and accessibility changes — Multigenerational (MHRTC): Building a self-contained secondary unit
  - Who it is for — Home Accessibility (HATC): Age 65+, or DTC-eligible, or a paying family member — Multigenerational (MHRTC): A relative building the unit for a senior or DTC-eligible adult
  - Refundable? — Home Accessibility (HATC): No, only lowers tax you owe — Multigenerational (MHRTC): Yes, paid even if you owe no tax
  - When it arrives — Home Accessibility (HATC): The spring after you file — Multigenerational (MHRTC): The spring after you file, once the work is done

[myths]
  - “These are grants, they will pay for my renovation.” — Neither one pays a contractor. Both are tax credits claimed after the fact, and the amount depends on your tax rate, which has moved in the last two years. Budget the full cost up front and treat the credit like a rebate arriving next spring.

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: THE ONE WORD THAT MATTERS MOST

[spotlight] Refundable and non-refundable are not fine print. They decide whether a senior gets money, or gets nothing. A non-refundable credit can only lower tax you already owe, and many people living on Old Age Security and the Guaranteed Income Supplement owe little or none, so the Home Accessibility Credit may be worth far less than it sounds. A refundable credit, like the Multigenerational credit, pays out regardless. So it often makes more sense for the person actually paying the bill to claim the non-refundable one, and for the senior’s name to go on the refundable one. Decide this before the invoice is written, not after.

## Layer two: where the real grants live

[paragraph] This is the layer most people never find, because it has no single name and no single website. These are the programs that pay you before the work starts, usually as a grant or a loan you never have to repay, and most of them check your income first.

[paragraph] Some are run by the province. Many are actually run by your city or region on the province’s behalf, which is why the same program can look different one town over. A couple are even delivered by a non-profit instead of a government office.

[lists]
  - British Columbia — A rebate for accessibility work, plus a renovation tax credit for seniors.
  - Alberta — The Seniors Home Adaptation and Repair Program. Read carefully: it is a loan against your home, not a grant.
  - Saskatchewan — A home renovation tax credit open to homeowners generally, not only seniors.
  - Ontario — Ontario Renovates, through your city or region: part grant, part loan. First-come, until the money runs out.
  - Quebec — Programme d’adaptation de domicile and RénoRégion, applied for through your municipality.
  - Atlantic Canada — New Brunswick, Nova Scotia, PEI and Newfoundland and Labrador each run their own seniors’ repair programs.
  - The Territories — Yukon and the Northwest Territories run seasonal programs. Miss the window, and you wait a year.

[paragraph] Two habits beat memorizing this list: search “home adaptation program” with your city’s name, and call 211. They keep the real, current local list.

[myths]
  - “Grants are only for people with almost no income.” — Grant programs almost always check income, but the cut-off is often higher than people expect, well above what many fixed-income seniors receive. The tax credits do not check income at all. Assuming you make too much is how people skip both.

## Layer three: one form that unlocks the rest

[paragraph] The Disability Tax Credit (DTC) is not a renovation program by itself. It is a certificate. Your doctor fills out a form confirming a serious, long-term health condition. And a surprising amount of the money in this course sits underneath it.

[paragraph] Being approved for the DTC opens the Home Accessibility Credit at any age, not just 65. It can make someone a qualifying person for the Multigenerational credit. It helps, or is required for, several provincial grants. It can even be backdated to past tax years, sometimes producing a refund large enough to help fund the renovation outright.

[paragraph] It also gets turned down the first time fairly often, then approved later once the doctor adds more detail. If anyone in the home has a long-term mobility, vision, hearing or memory problem and has never applied. That is the single best use of an hour in this whole course.

## Does this apply to me?

[paragraph] Six quick questions. If most of these are true, keep reading.

[chips]
  - Someone in the home is 65 or older, or has a long-term health condition.
  - The work is permanent and about safety or getting around, not decoration.
  - The home is the main residence of the person the work is for.
  - The work has not started yet, or if it has, every receipt has been kept.
  - I have checked what my province and my city offer, not just the federal credits.
  - I know who in the family will pay, and who will claim the credit.

## Do these in order, or the money disappears

[paragraph] Most of this money is lost by doing things in the wrong order. Grant programs usually will not pay for work that already started. Tax credits do not care about timing, but they care a great deal about receipts.

[ordered]
  - Apply for the DTC first, if it might fit — It can take months to hear back, and it changes what else you can get.
  - Find the rule about starting work — Read it before you accept a quote. If it says wait for written approval, that sentence matters more than any quote.
  - Get an assessment, if one is asked for — Some programs want an occupational therapist to sign off. It helps a tax claim too, even where it is not required.
  - Get written approval, then start — A phone call does not count. Neither does a friendly email from someone who is “pretty sure” it will go through.
  - Keep every receipt — It needs the company’s GST/HST number, the address, what was done, and proof you paid. A total on a business card is not a receipt.
  - Claim it at tax time — Tell whoever does your taxes that the work happened. They cannot claim what they do not know about.

[myths]
  - “The contractor said he would handle the paperwork.” — Some contractors handle it well. But you sign the tax return, and you are the one a program holds to the start-work rule. Ask to see the written approval yourself before anyone touches a wall.

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: DO NOT BORROW AGAINST YOUR HOUSE TO CLOSE THE GAP

[spotlight] Every program in this course pays you back later, but the contractor wants paying now. That gap tempts people toward the fastest loan in the room, and the fastest loan in the room is almost always the worst one. Contractor financing, rent-to-own equipment and high-rate second mortgages get marketed hardest to exactly the households these programs are meant to help. Cheaper options usually exist: a line of credit from your regular bank, a documented family loan, splitting the work into two phases across two tax years, or simply waiting for the next grant window.

[paragraph] Worth checking too: if a Veteran lives in the home, Module 1.1 covers the Veterans Independence Program, which can also help with home support and changes, on entirely separate money from anything in this course.

## Two sisters, one bathroom, and a rule in paragraph nine

[story] Two sisters, one bathroom
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ Bernice is eighty-two and has lived in the same house in Moncton since 1974. Her daughters, Colette and Anne-Marie, agreed the bathtub had to go. Colette got three quotes. Anne-Marie, who actually reads things, got the application forms.
  ¶ They nearly made the classic mistake. The contractor had a cancellation and could start Monday. That one sentence has cost Canadian families more grant money than any other. Anne-Marie went back to the guidelines and found, in paragraph nine, that work started before written approval simply would not count. They rebooked. It cost six weeks and one slightly annoyed contractor.
  ¶ They also got the arithmetic right. Bernice lives on Old Age Security, the Guaranteed Income Supplement and a small pension, so she pays almost no tax. A non-refundable credit in her name would have been close to worthless. So Colette paid for the work and claimed the Home Accessibility Credit herself, against her own tax bill. Anne-Marie applied for the provincial grant in Bernice’s name instead, where the income test actually worked in their favour.
  ¶ The walk-in shower went in that March. Between the grant and the credit, the family paid roughly half of the first quote. Nothing clever happened. Two people read the rules, split the paperwork, and decided who was paying before anyone bought tile.

[takeaway] Three layers, and you wear all three: a federal credit claimed at tax time, a provincial or city grant applied for before the work starts, and the Disability Tax Credit sitting underneath both. Decide who pays and who claims before the first invoice, and never let a contractor’s free Monday beat a program’s start-work rule.

## Glossary

[glossary]
  - HATC — Home Accessibility Tax Credit. A federal credit for permanent safety and accessibility changes to a home.
  - MHRTC — Multigenerational Home Renovation Tax Credit. A federal credit for building a self-contained secondary unit so a senior can live with family.
  - DTC — Disability Tax Credit. A certification of a severe, long-term impairment that unlocks several other programs.
  - Refundable credit — Paid to you even if you owe no tax. A non-refundable credit only lowers tax you already owe.
  - Forgivable loan — Money you do not have to pay back, as long as you stay in the home for a set number of years.
  - Service manager — The municipal or regional office that actually runs a provincial housing program and takes your application.
  - Self-contained unit — A living space with its own entrance, kitchen, bathroom and sleeping area — the test for the multigenerational credit.
  - Start-work rule — A program rule that the work must not begin before written approval arrives. Breaking it can cost you the funding.

[lists]
  - WHERE TO GET HELP — 211: dial 2-1-1 anywhere in Canada for your local list of home adaptation and repair programs.; Canada Revenue Agency: 1-800-959-8281 for questions about the accessibility credit, the multigenerational credit and the Disability Tax Credit.; Community Volunteer Income Tax Program: free tax clinics that can help file the claim if money is tight.; Your provincial housing authority or your city’s service manager, for grants and forgivable loans.

[paragraph] This module skips dollar figures on purpose. Credit rates, cost limits and provincial income cut-offs all change, sometimes every year. Check current numbers on canada.ca and your province’s program page before you rely on them.
