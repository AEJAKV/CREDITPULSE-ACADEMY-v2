## Courtroom brief

[paragraph] Old debt can arrive wearing a fresh envelope and the confidence of a brand-new invoice. The law is less interested in the envelope than in the timeline.

[paragraph] This masterclass helps you screen an older unsecured consumer debt before you pay, promise or ignore anything. Separate the court deadline from reporting retention, identify jurisdiction and build a dated evidence file for local advice. The result is a careful next step, not a homemade legal verdict.

[spotlight] A limitation period usually concerns time to start a court proceeding. It does not automatically erase a debt or remove accurate report information.

[lists]
  - Separate four clocks: court action, credit reporting, collection conduct and judgment enforcement. — Use a current Canadian jurisdiction map as a screening tool without treating it as legal advice. — Identify dates and documents that may affect when a claim was discovered or acknowledged. — Avoid accidental decisions while an old debt is still being investigated. — Complete an old-debt timeline and a legal-advice checkpoint.

[myths]
  - “When a limitation period ends, the debt disappears automatically.” — Limitation law usually restricts a court remedy; it is not a universal debt eraser. Legal effect varies, and Newfoundland and Labrador has distinctive extinguishment wording.

## Jordan and the Twenty-Dollar Shortcut

[story] Meet Jordan (DEDICATED STORY)

Jordan Chen, a warehouse dispatcher in Halifax, was preparing to apply for an apartment when a collector emailed about an old store-card balance. The account had been sold twice, the amount was larger than he remembered and the message offered a tidy solution: pay $20 today to “show good faith.”

Twenty dollars sounded harmless, but the message threatened legal action within forty-eight hours. Instead of clicking, Jordan opened an evidence folder: agreement, statements, last-payment record, default notice and an old email promising to “try to catch up.” He recorded where he lived when the account opened and when payments stopped. Then he requested the creditor, balance breakdown and dates in writing.

A community legal clinic explained that the ordinary period was only the beginning. Province, claim type, discovery, acknowledgement, part payment, transition rules and any claim or judgment could matter. A token payment was not a useful experiment.

Jordan did not declare the debt expired or ignore the collector. He preserved messages, avoided acknowledging liability, requested records and diarized the response date. When a formal court document arrived, he obtained help and responded on time.

The matter ended through a documented resolution based on evidence, not the collector’s countdown clock.

[spotlight] Jordan’s strongest move was the pause between pressure and payment. It turned an old account into a dated, reviewable case file.

[heading] The conclusion

[paragraph] The lesson is not “never pay old debt.” It is “do not make a legally meaningful move before you understand the clock, claim and documents.”

[transition] One account can be running four separate clocks at once, and only one of them is the court deadline.

## Exhibit A: One Account Can Carry Four Timelines

[paragraph] The most common mistake is treating every date as the same date. Put each clock on its own line.

[cards]
  - COURT-CLAIM LIMITATION — Deadline to start a civil proceeding, subject to law, discovery and exceptions. Which law applies, what claim is this and when was it discoverable? — 01
  - CREDIT-REPORT RETENTION — How long information may remain under reporting rules and bureau practices. What is the delinquency or reporting date, and is the entry accurate? — 02
  - COLLECTION CONDUCT — How a collector may contact you and which regulator handles complaints. Where do I live, who is collecting and which rules apply? — 03
  - JUDGMENT ENFORCEMENT — How an existing judgment may be enforced; a judgment is not an unlitigated account. Was a claim filed or judgment entered? What court, date and file number? — 04

[spotlight] A six-year credit-report entry does not create a six-year lawsuit deadline.

[myths]
  - “If an item is gone from my credit report, no one can sue.” — Report removal and civil limitation law are separate. A missing tradeline is not proof that a claim is barred or no judgment exists.

[heading] THE COURTROOM-DOCUMENT RULE

[paragraph] Never ignore a Statement of Claim or other court document because you believe time expired.

[paragraph] Limitation may be a defence that must be raised through the required process and on time. Verify the court and obtain help quickly.

## Exhibit B: Canada Screening Map

[paragraph] Ordinary civil-debt periods: a screening guide only.

[paragraph] This August 2026 first-pass guide is not a legal conclusion. Claim, contract, discovery, transition law, acknowledgement, payment, judgment, security or a special statute may change the answer.

[cards]
  - 2 YEARS — Alberta, British Columbia, Manitoba, New Brunswick, Nova Scotia, Ontario and Saskatchewan — Modern statutes generally use discovery-based basic periods. Ultimate periods and transition rules may matter. — 01
  - 3 YEARS — Quebec and Nunavut — Quebec generally uses three-year prescription for a personal action. Nunavut’s 2023 amendments use three years after discovery for recovery of money, with a ten-year ultimate period. — 02
  - 6 YEARS — Newfoundland and Labrador, Northwest Territories, Prince Edward Island and Yukon — Statutes contain six-year categories relevant to recovery of debt or money. Trigger, confirmation and other wording still require local analysis. — 03
  - SPECIAL TRACK — Judgments, secured claims, tax or government debt, student obligations, support orders and other statutory claims — Do not force these into the ordinary unsecured-debt table. Identify the special law and obtain qualified advice. — 04

[spotlight] Manitoba’s current general discovery-based period is two years. Nunavut’s recovery-of-money period changed to three years in 2023. Old charts can be confidently outdated.

[myths]
  - “Canada has one national limitation period for consumer debt.” — Limitation law is provincial or territorial, and claim type and facts can change the answer.

[heading] How to use the map

[ordered]
  - Locate the law that may govern; do not assume your current address decides everything.
  - Identify the legal claim and event said to start discovery or accrual.
  - Check transition rules if events happened before the current statute.
  - Ask a lawyer, clinic or consumer office before payment or acknowledgement.

[spotlight] In Quebec, limitation is commonly discussed as prescription. Different term, same need for dates, documents and local advice.

## Evidence Hearing: Build the Timeline Before Testing the Clock

[paragraph] Write “unknown” when a fact is missing; a guess dressed as a date is still a guess.

[cards]
  - Agreement and jurisdiction — Contract, opening location, governing-law wording and residence history. — 01
  - Payment history — Last payment date, amount, bank proof and first missed payment never cured. — 02
  - Default events — Statements, demand or acceleration notices, account closure and correspondence. — 03
  - Acknowledgements — Any signed or written recognition, part payment, exact wording, date and maker. — 04
  - Assignment history — Original creditor, purchasers or collectors and balance breakdown. Sale normally does not restart time itself. — 05
  - Court status — Claim, service documents, defence, order or judgment. Verify the court independently. — 06

[spotlight] A payment or qualifying acknowledgement can affect analysis in many jurisdictions. Form, timing and post-expiry effect are not uniform.

[myths]
  - “Selling a debt automatically starts a new limitation period.” — A transfer changes who claims the account; it does not normally create a fresh default date on its own. Other later events may matter.

## Quick Check: Choose the Best Next Move

[accordions]
  - A collector offers a token-payment plan on an eight-year-old account. What should you do before paying? — Preserve the offer, gather the timeline and obtain advice for the applicable jurisdiction before paying or acknowledging.
  - Why is credit-report removal not enough to calculate the court-claim period? — Reporting retention and civil limitation are separate clocks governed by different rules and dates.
  - A formal court document arrives after you think the period expired. What is safest? — Verify the court, obtain help immediately and file the required response on time; limitation may need to be raised as a defence.
  - Which facts can make a screening period unreliable by itself? — Claim type, jurisdiction, discovery, transition law, acknowledgement, part payment, judgment, security and special statutes.
  - Does a debt sale normally reset the clock on its own? — No. Assignment changes who claims the account; it does not normally create a new default date by itself.

[spotlight] The word “old” is not a legal date. A useful file contains events, evidence and the law that may apply.

## Glossary of Terms

[glossary]
  - Limitation period — A statutory time limit for starting a legal proceeding, subject to the law and facts.
  - Discovery — The point when a claimant knew or reasonably ought to have known key facts needed to bring a claim.
  - Acknowledgement — A statement recognizing a claim or debt that may affect timing if the applicable statute’s requirements are met.
  - Part payment — A payment of less than the full balance that may affect a limitation analysis in some jurisdictions.
  - Prescription — The term commonly used in Quebec for the legal effect of the passage of time on rights and remedies.
  - Judgment — A court’s formal decision; enforcement can follow rules different from an unlitigated account.
  - Ultimate period — A longer outside deadline that may apply even when discovery occurs later, subject to exceptions.
