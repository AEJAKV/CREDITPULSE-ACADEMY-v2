## Start here

[paragraph] Tax software is excellent at one thing: taking every T4, T5 and RRSP slip you upload and putting the numbers in the right boxes. It is considerably less good at knowing that you drove your father to dialysis fourteen times last year, or that a doctor recommended you install a stair lift.

[paragraph] None of that arrives on a slip. All of it can be worth real money on your return.

[heading] What this course does

[paragraph] The pattern behind almost everything here is the same: no document shows up in the mail to remind you, so the credit only gets claimed by people who already knew to go looking for it. This module is the looking.

## Medical expenses: wider than almost anyone assumes

[paragraph] Most people claim the obvious: prescriptions, dental work, glasses. The credit reaches much further, and it reaches across your household. You can claim eligible expenses for yourself, your spouse, and dependants including adult children, parents, grandparents, and in some cases siblings, aunts, uncles, nieces and nephews who rely on you for support.

[paragraph] Two categories people consistently miss. Travel: if you had to go at least 40 kilometres one way for medical services not available near home, the travel itself is claimable, and past 80 kilometres you can add meals and accommodation. And a wide list of items beyond prescriptions: orthodontic work, hearing aids, wigs following a medical condition, air conditioners recommended for a medical condition, and home modifications for a person with mobility limitations, several of which also connect to the Home Accessibility Tax Credit covered in Module 1.2.

[paragraph] The claim runs against a floor: you can only claim the amount above a threshold indexed each year, or three percent of net income, whichever is lower. That floor is exactly why whose return carries the claim matters.

[myths]
  - “Everyone should claim their own medical expenses on their own return.” — The claim can go on either spouse’s return, and putting it on the lower-income spouse’s return often clears the income threshold with a smaller pile of receipts, producing a larger credit. Add up every household member’s expenses, then decide together whose return carries the claim. Do not default to whoever happened to pay.

## The Canada Caregiver Credit: for people who never call themselves a caregiver

[paragraph] This credit exists for anyone supporting a spouse, partner, or dependant with a physical or mental impairment, and a great many people providing exactly that support do not think of themselves as caregivers. They think of themselves as someone whose mother lives with them, or whose adult son has a condition that means he will always need some support. The CRA’s definition is broader than the word feels.

[paragraph] It stacks with other things. A dependant eligible for this credit is very often also eligible for the Disability Tax Credit, which in turn can open the Home Accessibility Tax Credit, the Registered Disability Savings Plan, and a larger claim under this very credit. One eligibility form, completed once by a medical practitioner, can be the key to several benefits at once: the same door-opener pattern from Module 1.1, showing up again here.

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: THE FORM THAT UNLOCKS THE MOST, AND GETS FILED THE LEAST

[spotlight] The Disability Tax Credit, established with form T2201 completed by a medical practitioner, is the single highest-leverage document in this entire module. It is not only a credit in its own right. It is the eligibility gate for the caregiver credit at its higher amount, the Home Accessibility Tax Credit at any age, the Registered Disability Savings Plan and its matching government grants, and several provincial programs. It can also be backdated. If a practitioner confirms the impairment existed in prior years, the CRA can adjust up to ten years of past returns, which occasionally produces a refund large enough to fund a renovation, clear a debt, or simply arrive as a genuine surprise. It is also commonly declined on a first, thin application and approved on a second one with more specific medical detail about how the impairment restricts daily function. A decline is a data point about the paperwork, not a final verdict on the person.

## Working and raising a family: the quiet ones

[lists]
  - Child care expenses — Not only daycare. Camps, before-and-after-school programs and caregivers all qualify.; The receipt needs the provider’s SIN or business number, a detail that trips people up every spring.
  - Moving expenses — If you moved at least 40 kilometres closer to a new job, business or full-time study program, a long list of costs is deductible.; Movers, travel, temporary lodging, and the costs of selling the old home and buying the new one.
  - Tuition transfer — A student who cannot use all their tuition credit this year can transfer up to a set amount to a spouse, parent or grandparent.; Families routinely default to carry-forward without comparing which choice is worth more right now.
  - Home office expenses — For employees required to work from home, a share of costs like utilities, internet and rent is claimable with the right employer-signed form.; The pandemic-era flat rate is gone. The current process rewards keeping records through the year, not assembling them in April.
  - Canada Training Credit and Canada Workers Benefit — Covered in Module 1.4, and worth a second mention here.; Both are refundable, both are easy to miss because neither arrives with a slip, and both are worth checking every year regardless of what you claimed last.

[myths]
  - “Tax software catches everything I am entitled to automatically.” — Software is built to process what you enter, and it processes slips exceptionally well. Anything without a slip, caregiving, medical travel, a family member’s disability status, a mid-year move, has to be entered by you, because the software has no way to know it happened.

## Refundable versus non-refundable, one more time

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: REFUNDABLE VERSUS NON-REFUNDABLE, ONE MORE TIME

[spotlight] This distinction runs through the entire Platinum tier for a reason: it is the single fact most likely to change whether a credit is worth pursuing at all. A non-refundable credit only reduces tax you actually owe. If your income is low enough that your tax bill is already near zero, claiming more non-refundable credits does not put money in your pocket; it is arithmetic against a number that was already small. A refundable credit is paid to you regardless, which is why the Canada Training Credit, the Canada Workers Benefit and several caregiver-adjacent provincial credits matter disproportionately for lower-income households. Before assuming a credit is not worth the paperwork for someone on a fixed or modest income, check which kind it is. The paperwork is often identical. The payoff is not.

## Do not stop at the federal return

[paragraph] Every province and territory runs its own parallel credits: caregiver amounts, disability supplements, home renovation credits like the ones in Module 1.2, low-income tax reductions, and climate or carbon rebates. These sit on a completely separate schedule from the federal return, get less attention because there is no single national name for any of them, and are just as easy to miss. Check your province’s tax credit page once a year, the same way you would check the federal one.

[chips]
  - Medical expenses for every household member, not just yourself, totalled before deciding whose return carries the claim.
  - Whether anyone in the household might qualify for the Disability Tax Credit and has never applied.
  - Caregiving support you provide but have never labelled as caregiving.
  - A move, a new job, or a return to school in the past year that might unlock a deduction.
  - Whether last year’s return should be adjusted. The CRA generally allows amendments going back several years.
  - Your province’s own credits, checked separately from the federal list.

## Priyanka, her mother, and the form that had been sitting there for years

[story] Priyanka and her mother
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ Priyanka’s mother moved in with her three years ago after a stroke left her with significant mobility limitations. Priyanka drives her to two specialist appointments a month, manages her medications, and has never once used the word caregiver to describe any of it. It was just what a daughter does.
  ¶ Her accountant asked one question at their annual meeting that nobody had asked before: has your mother’s doctor ever completed a disability tax credit form? Priyanka said no, because it had never occurred to her that a stroke severe enough to need this much support might not automatically be disabled enough on paper without someone actually applying.
  ¶ The form took one appointment. The approval came back covering the current year and, once her accountant asked the specific follow-up question, three prior years as well, since the impairment had clearly existed since the stroke. That produced adjustments to three old returns, a materially larger caregiver credit going forward, and eligibility for programs Priyanka had never looked at because she had never known her mother qualified for the credit underneath them.
  ¶ Nothing about her mother’s situation changed. The only thing that changed was a form getting filled in by someone who finally thought to ask.

[takeaway] The credits that go unclaimed are the ones with no slip attached: medical travel, caregiving, a family member’s undocumented disability, a mid-year move. Total household medical expenses before deciding whose return carries them, ask whether anyone should have a Disability Tax Credit application on file, and check your province’s credits separately from the federal ones. None of it arrives by mail. All of it has to be asked for.

## Glossary

[glossary]
  - DTC — Disability Tax Credit. Certification of a severe and prolonged impairment, established with form T2201. The gateway to several other credits.
  - Canada Caregiver Credit — For a spouse, partner or dependant with a physical or mental impairment who relies on you for support.
  - Refundable vs non-refundable — A refundable credit is paid regardless of tax owed. A non-refundable credit only reduces tax you already owe.
  - T2201 — The Disability Tax Credit Certificate, completed by a medical practitioner.
  - Notice of Reassessment — The CRA’s response to an adjustment request, such as a retroactive disability credit claim on a prior year.
  - Net income threshold (medical expenses) — The floor below which medical expenses cannot be claimed, set at an indexed dollar amount or 3% of net income, whichever is lower.

[lists]
  - WHERE TO GET HELP — Canada Revenue Agency: 1-800-959-8281, for questions about any credit in this module.; Community Volunteer Income Tax Program: free tax preparation and filing help for modest-income households, including prior-year adjustment requests.; A tax professional or accountant: for household-level decisions, like whose return should carry a shared medical claim.

[paragraph] This module skips dollar amounts on purpose. Thresholds, credit rates and provincial amounts are indexed and revised regularly. Confirm current figures on canada.ca and your provincial tax credit page before filing.
