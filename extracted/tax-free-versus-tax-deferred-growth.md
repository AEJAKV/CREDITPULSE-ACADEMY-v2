## Start here

[paragraph] You’ve now met six registered accounts across this course. TFSA. RRSP. FHSA. RESP. RDSP. RRIF.

[paragraph] They look like six separate things to memorize. They aren’t. Underneath, almost every one of them is answering the same question in one of two ways.

[paragraph] When does the tax get paid — now, or later? That’s it. Get that distinction straight, and the whole system stops feeling like a pile of acronyms and starts looking like a set of tools with obvious uses.

## The two models, side by side

[paragraph] Tax-free: pay tax now, never again. You contribute money that’s already been taxed. No deduction on the way in. But from that moment on, the account is untouchable — growth isn’t taxed, and withdrawals aren’t taxed, ever. A TFSA works this way. So does the FHSA on qualifying withdrawals, which is unusual because it also gives you a deduction going in.

[paragraph] Tax-deferred: skip the tax now, pay it later. You get a deduction today, which lowers this year’s tax bill. The money grows untouched. But when it comes out, the withdrawal counts as income and gets taxed at whatever rate applies then. An RRSP works this way, and so does the RRIF it eventually becomes. The RDSP is also tax-deferred on its growth and government money.

[paragraph] Neither model is better in the abstract. Which one wins depends entirely on your tax rate now versus your tax rate later.

[cards]
  - TFSA — Account: No — Deduction going in?: No
  - RRSP / RRIF — Account: Yes — Deduction going in?: Yes — fully, as income
  - FHSA — Account: Yes — Deduction going in?: No, on a qualifying home
  - RESP — Account: No — Deduction going in?: Growth and grants taxed — to the student
  - RDSP — Account: No — Deduction going in?: Growth and government money taxed

[myths]
  - “Tax-free is always better than tax-deferred.” — Not automatically. A tax-deferred account gives you a deduction at today’s rate. If today’s rate is high and your retirement rate will be lower, that deferral is genuinely valuable. Tax-free wins when your rate today is low and likely to rise. It’s a comparison, not a ranking.

## The question that answers most of these decisions

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: THE QUESTION THAT ANSWERS MOST OF THESE DECISIONS

[spotlight] Ask yourself one thing: is my tax rate higher now, or will it be higher later? If your rate is high now — peak earning years, sitting on an upper shelf — a deduction is doing real work. Tax-deferred accounts like an RRSP shine here, because you’re skipping tax at a high rate and paying it later at a lower one. If your rate is low now — early career, part-time work, a parental leave year, semi-retirement — a deduction isn’t saving you much. Tax-free accounts make more sense, because you’re paying tax at a cheap rate today and never paying it again. This single question explains why the same advice can be right for one person and wrong for their neighbour. Nobody’s tax rate stays still for a whole career, which is why most Canadians end up using both kinds of account at different stages.

## The account that breaks the pattern

[paragraph] Most accounts pick one model. The FHSA takes both.

[paragraph] You get a deduction going in, like a tax-deferred account. And qualifying withdrawals come out completely untaxed, like a tax-free one. There’s no repayment obligation either, unlike the Home Buyers’ Plan.

[paragraph] That combination is genuinely rare. It’s why, if you’re eligible for an FHSA at all, it usually deserves attention before you weigh the other two against each other.

## A rough order of operations

[paragraph] There’s no universal answer here, and your own situation matters more than any general rule. But a common sequence looks something like this.

[chips]
  - Employer pension matching first. If your workplace matches contributions, that’s an immediate guaranteed return before any tax consideration enters the picture.
  - Government-matched accounts next. RESP grants and RDSP grants and bonds pay more per dollar than almost any tax strategy. If you’re eligible, these are hard to beat.
  - Then the tax-rate question. High shelf now? Lean RRSP. Low shelf now? Lean TFSA. Saving for a first home? The FHSA likely comes first.

[paragraph] Notice that the matched accounts come before the tax question entirely. A guaranteed 20% or more from a government grant beats a tax deduction in almost every scenario.

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: THE SECOND QUESTION, WHO INHERITS IT

[spotlight] Now-or-later isn’t the only question running underneath these accounts. There’s a second one, quieter, and it only comes up once: what happens to this account the day you die? A TFSA named to a spouse as successor holder, from Module 2.5, keeps its tax-free status without interruption. Named only as beneficiary, it collapses into ordinary taxable-going-forward assets instead. A RRIF named to a spouse as successor annuitant, from Module 2.10, simply continues; named only as beneficiary, its full value is generally added to your income in the year you die. An RESP can transfer between siblings. RDSP grants and bonds have their own repayment rules if the account closes early. Different accounts, same pattern: the difference between an account passing on intact and one collapsing into a tax bill often comes down to one line on one form, filled in correctly years before anyone needed it to matter.

[myths]
  - “I should pick one account type and put everything there.” — Most Canadians benefit from using several, at different life stages and for different goals. A TFSA for flexible medium-term savings, an RRSP in peak earning years, an FHSA while a first home is still ahead. They’re tools, not teams to pick.

## Choosing between accounts

[chips]
  - I know whether my tax rate is likely higher now or later.
  - I’ve checked whether an employer match or a government grant is available before optimizing for tax.
  - If I’m saving for a first home, I’ve considered the FHSA before anything else.
  - I understand that RRSP and RRIF withdrawals count as income, and can affect income-tested benefits later.
  - I’m not treating any one account as universally best — the right answer depends on my own situation.

## The same advice, three different people

[story] The same advice, three different people
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ Three coworkers at the same company got the same tip from the same well-meaning manager: put everything into your RRSP.
  ¶ For Ines, forty-eight and at the top of her earning years, it was excellent advice. Her deduction landed on a high shelf, saving real tax, and her retirement rate will almost certainly be lower.
  ¶ For Tomas, twenty-four and in his first year of work, it was mediocre advice. His shelf was already the lowest one — the deduction shielded income that was barely taxed. A TFSA would have given him more flexibility and cost him almost nothing in lost deduction value.
  ¶ For Grace, thirty-one and saving hard for a first condo, it was the wrong advice entirely. She was eligible for an FHSA, which would have given her the same deduction plus tax-free withdrawals for exactly the goal she was saving toward.
  ¶ Same sentence. Three different situations. The manager wasn’t being careless — he was describing what had worked for him. That’s usually what general financial advice actually is.

[takeaway] Almost every registered account answers one question: do you pay tax now, or later? Tax-free accounts cost you tax today and never again. Tax-deferred accounts give you a deduction today and tax you on withdrawal. Which wins depends on whether your tax rate is higher now or later — but government-matched accounts like the RESP and RDSP usually come first, since a guaranteed match beats a deduction.

## Glossary

[glossary]
  - Tax-free growth — Contributions are made with after-tax money; growth and withdrawals are never taxed.
  - Tax-deferred growth — Contributions are deducted now; growth is sheltered, and withdrawals are taxed as income later.
  - Marginal rate — The rate on your next dollar of income. The number that decides how much a deduction is worth.
  - Matched contribution — Money an employer or the government adds on top of what you contribute. Generally worth prioritizing over tax strategy.
