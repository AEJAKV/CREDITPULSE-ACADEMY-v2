## Start here

[paragraph] Tax-Free Savings Account. It sounds like a plain savings account with a nice tax perk attached.

[paragraph] It’s actually much more flexible than that. You can hold stocks, bonds, mutual funds, and cash inside a TFSA — not just a savings balance earning a small interest rate. And every dollar of growth inside it, forever, is untouched by tax.

[paragraph] The name is technically accurate. It’s also the reason a lot of Canadians underuse one of the best tools available to them.

## The core idea, in one paragraph

[paragraph] You contribute money that’s already been taxed — no deduction here, unlike an RRSP. But once it’s inside the account, everything that happens next is tax-free. Growth, dividends, interest, and every withdrawal. None of it gets taxed, ever, no matter how large the account grows.

[paragraph] That’s the whole mechanism. Simple to describe. The details that trip people up are all about room and timing.

## Contribution room: what it is, and how it builds

[paragraph] Every year, if you’re 18 or older and a Canadian resident, you get new TFSA room. For 2026, that’s $7,000.

[paragraph] Room builds whether or not you open an account. It builds whether or not you have any income at all — unlike an RRSP, a TFSA doesn’t care what you earned.

[paragraph] Room also carries forward. Never contributed before, and you’ve been eligible since the TFSA started in 2009? Your total room by 2026 sits at $109,000. Miss a year, or several, and that room just waits for you — it doesn’t expire, and it doesn’t shrink.

[myths]
  - “If I don’t use my TFSA room this year, I lose it.” — The opposite is true. Unused room carries forward forever, with no expiry. Someone who’s never contributed at all still has every dollar of room they’ve accumulated since they turned 18 or since 2009, whichever came later.

## Withdrawals: easy to take out, easy to mess up putting back

[paragraph] Take money out of a TFSA, and it’s yours — no tax, no penalty, no questions asked.

[paragraph] Here’s the part that catches people. That withdrawn amount does get added back to your room. But not immediately. Not even that same year. It comes back on January 1 of the following year.

[paragraph] Withdraw $5,000 in June this year, and you might assume you can put it right back in August. You can’t — not unless you have other unused room sitting there already. That $5,000 doesn’t reappear as available room until next January 1.

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: THE MISTAKE THAT COSTS PEOPLE REAL MONEY

[spotlight] This is, by a wide margin, the most common TFSA error — and it happens to people who are otherwise being careful with their money. Someone maxes out their TFSA. Then, mid-year, they withdraw a chunk for an emergency or a big purchase. A few months later, feeling flush again, they put that same amount back in — not realizing the room hasn’t actually returned yet. The result is an over-contribution. And the CRA charges a real penalty for that: 1% of the excess amount, every single month it stays over the limit. The fix is simple once you know it. Before recontributing anything you withdrew this calendar year, check your actual available room — not just what you remember withdrawing. Your CRA My Account shows your real, current number.

## TFSA or RRSP — the question everyone asks eventually

[paragraph] Both are excellent tools. They’re just built for different moments.

[paragraph] An RRSP gives you a deduction now, and you pay tax later, when you withdraw — usually in retirement, when your income and your tax shelf are often lower.

[paragraph] A TFSA gives you no deduction now, but you never pay tax on it again — not on the growth, not on the withdrawal, ever.

[paragraph] A rough rule of thumb: if you’re on a low shelf right now, a TFSA often makes more sense, since the RRSP deduction isn’t saving you much anyway. If you’re on a high shelf, the RRSP deduction is doing real work, and may be worth prioritizing. Many Canadians end up using both, just leaning toward one or the other depending on where they sit in a given year.

[myths]
  - “A TFSA is only useful for short-term savings, like an emergency fund.” — That’s one good use for it, but far from the only one. A TFSA can hold long-term investments — stocks, ETFs, mutual funds — and let decades of growth compound completely tax-free. Treating it only as a savings account is leaving a much bigger tool on the table.

## Before you contribute or withdraw

[chips]
  - I know my actual available TFSA room, checked in my CRA account, not estimated from memory.
  - If I withdrew money this year, I know it won’t count as available room again until next January 1.
  - I understand a TFSA can hold investments, not just cash sitting at a low interest rate.
  - I’ve thought about whether a TFSA or an RRSP fits my situation better this year, or whether both make sense.
  - I know contribution room builds every year regardless of income, so it’s worth tracking even in a low-earning year.

## Owen’s expensive August

[story] Owen’s expensive August
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ Owen had maxed out his TFSA every year since he opened it. Disciplined, consistent, proud of it. In June, he pulled fifteen thousand dollars out for a kitchen renovation — a completely reasonable use of the account.
  ¶ In August, a bonus landed, and Owen did what felt like the obvious thing: put fifteen thousand dollars back into the TFSA, restoring it to where it had been before the renovation.
  ¶ He got a letter from the CRA the following spring. The bonus contribution had been an over-contribution — his withdrawn room wasn’t due back until the following January 1, and he’d had no other room sitting unused. The penalty had been quietly accumulating, 1% a month, for the five months the excess sat in the account.
  ¶ It wasn’t a huge sum in the end, but it was money lost to a rule Owen had simply never been told about. He checks his actual available room in his CRA account now, every single time, before putting anything back in — no exceptions, no relying on memory.

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: NAME A SUCCESSOR HOLDER, NOT JUST A BENEFICIARY

[spotlight] One more mechanic worth setting up before you ever need it, and it costs nothing to do today. If you have a spouse or common-law partner, you can name them as your TFSA’s successor holder, not merely a beneficiary. The difference is bigger than it sounds. A beneficiary receives the money in your TFSA tax-free, but the account itself closes — the funds become ordinary cash or investments outside any TFSA, and none of your own contribution room transfers with it. A successor holder simply becomes the new owner of the account on the date you die. It keeps its TFSA status without interruption, the tax-free growth never stops, and it doesn’t touch or use up any of their own contribution room. Most people set a beneficiary and assume they’ve covered it. If you’re married or common-law, check your TFSA paperwork for the word successor — it’s usually one form, and it’s the difference between your spouse inheriting a TFSA and inheriting the bill that comes from losing one.

[takeaway] A TFSA lets your money grow and come out completely tax-free — no deduction going in, no tax ever again. Room builds every year regardless of income and never expires. But a withdrawal doesn’t restore your room until January 1 of the next year, and recontributing too soon is the single most common, and costly, TFSA mistake.

## Glossary

[glossary]
  - Contribution room — The amount you’re allowed to put into your TFSA, building up each year.
  - Over-contribution — Putting in more than your available room, triggering a 1% monthly penalty on the excess.
  - Qualified investment — The kinds of assets a TFSA can hold: cash, stocks, bonds, mutual funds and more.
  - Non-resident — Someone who has moved outside Canada. Room stops building while non-resident, and new contributions trigger their own penalty.
