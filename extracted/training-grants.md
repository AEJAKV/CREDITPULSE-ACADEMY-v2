## Start here

[paragraph] Most people who miss training money were never turned down. They asked the only organization they had heard of, got a no, and reasonably concluded the answer was no.

[paragraph] Training money in Canada comes from four separate places, and they barely acknowledge one another.

[heading] What this course does

[paragraph] The lane you are standing in decides who applies, who receives the money, and whether it arrives before or after you pay tuition. This module sorts the four lanes, in the order that keeps a claim alive.

## Four lanes: find yours before you do anything else

[paragraph] The first question is not what you want to study. It is which lane you are standing in.

[lists]
  - Employed, training for your current job — Job Grant programs pay a share of training an existing employee. The government covers the larger part, the employer the rest.; You cannot apply. Your employer can. Bring a costed proposal, not a request.; Provincially run, and not all running: Ontario’s has been paused for review since late 2025.
  - Out of work, or leaving a shrinking industry — The largest pot of money in this course, and the least visible, because it has no national brand.; Funded federally, spent provincially under names like Better Jobs Ontario or WorkBC.; Usually needs an eligibility interview. Applying takes weeks, so this lane rewards starting early.
  - Paying for your own training — Two federal tax tools: the Canada Training Credit and the Lifelong Learning Plan.; Covered in full just below, including the comparison that matters.
  - You are an apprentice — A separate system from the other three, and one that changed recently.; Covered in full just below, including what closed and what remains.

[paragraph] In lane one, the useful move is not a form. It is walking into a manager’s office with the program name, the training provider’s quote, and the employer’s share already worked out. Most managers say no to training because it feels like a vague expense. Fewer say no to a costed proposal with two-thirds of it already covered.

[paragraph] In lane two, provincial programs can fund tuition, books and a living allowance while you retrain. They are selective, usually want an eligibility interview, and tend to look for a labour-market reason rather than a personal interest. Applying takes weeks, so this lane rewards starting early too.

## Lane three: paying for your own training

[paragraph] Two federal tools live here, both run through the tax system, and most people who qualify for the first one do not know it exists.

[paragraph] The Canada Training Credit is a refundable credit that builds quietly in the background. If you file a return, fall within the age band and meet the income conditions, an amount is added to your training credit limit every year, up to a lifetime cap. Refundable matters here: you get it even if you owe no tax.

[paragraph] The Lifelong Learning Plan lets you withdraw from your own RRSP to fund full-time training for yourself or a spouse, tax-free at the time you take it out, with a repayment schedule afterward. It is not free money. It is your own money, borrowed from your future, with lost investment growth as the real cost.

[cards]
  - Whose money — Canada Training Credit: The government’s — Lifelong Learning Plan: Yours, withdrawn from your RRSP
  - Do you repay it — Canada Training Credit: No — Lifelong Learning Plan: Yes, on a set schedule, or it becomes taxable income
  - When you get it — Canada Training Credit: After filing, the spring after you pay tuition — Lifelong Learning Plan: Before you enrol, it is cash up front
  - Real cost — Canada Training Credit: None, beyond a reduced tuition credit — Lifelong Learning Plan: Lost investment growth, plus repayment pressure

[myths]
  - “Nobody is going to fund a course that is not a full diploma.” — The Canada Training Credit applies to eligible tuition and fees, including many short professional courses and licensing programs, not only degrees. Provincial job grants routinely fund a few days of certification. The bias toward long programs is in our heads, not in the rules.

## Lane four: you are an apprentice

[paragraph] This lane changed materially, and most of what you will read online is wrong about it. The Apprenticeship Incentive Grant and the Apprenticeship Completion Grant, the two federal cash grants paid directly to apprentices, both ended on 31 March 2025 and are no longer accepted.

[paragraph] What remains is real, and in some ways better. Apprentices can claim Employment Insurance while attending in-class technical training, and the Canada Apprentice Loan offers interest-free borrowing you can apply for months before training starts, with funds available from day one while an EI claim is still processing. Employers have their own incentive too: a federal tax credit tied to apprentice wages, worth knowing if you need to make the case to a hesitant sponsor.

[paragraph] New federal apprenticeship measures have been announced but not opened as of this writing. Announced is not open. Do not plan a year around a press release.

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: THE MONEY THAT IS ALREADY SITTING ON YOUR TAX RETURN

[spotlight] Log into CRA My Account and look at your most recent Notice of Assessment. If you are within the eligible age range and have been filing returns with working income, there is a Canada training credit limit sitting there, an amount that has been accumulating for years without anyone telling you. This is the rare government benefit that needs nothing from you until the moment you use it. It builds automatically, provided you file. Which is the catch worth stating plainly: people who stop filing during a low-income stretch stop accumulating, and those are often exactly the people who will later need to retrain. File every year, even a year with almost no income. It costs an hour and keeps this, and several other benefits, alive in the background.

## Does this apply to me?

[paragraph] Six quick questions. If most of these are true, move on to the order that keeps a claim alive.

[chips]
  - I know which of the four lanes I am in, and if I am in more than one, which pays first.
  - I have checked my Notice of Assessment for an accumulated training credit limit.
  - If my employer has to apply, I have a costed proposal rather than a request.
  - If I need provincial support, I have booked the eligibility appointment, not just read the page.
  - I have confirmed the program is currently open, not merely announced or historically famous.
  - I know whether the money arrives before I pay tuition or after.

## What to do, in what order

[ordered]
  - Check your Notice of Assessment first — It takes two minutes and tells you what you already have.
  - Establish whether the program is open today — Two federal apprenticeship grants closed in 2025, and at least one large provincial job grant is paused. Search the program name plus the current year and read the official page, not a summary.
  - Identify who has to apply — If it is your employer, your task is persuasion and paperwork preparation, not an application form.
  - Book the provincial appointment early if you need one — Eligibility assessments take weeks, and courses have start dates that do not wait.
  - Confirm the training provider qualifies before you pay a deposit — Most programs restrict funding to recognized institutions or approved provider lists. A deposit paid to an ineligible school is not recoverable.
  - Keep the receipts, and tell your tax preparer what you took — The training credit is claimed on the return, and nobody claims it for you.

[myths]
  - “I will just enrol now and sort out the funding once I have started.” — Provincial retraining programs generally will not fund a course you have already begun, and several require the eligibility assessment first. Tax credits do not care about sequence, but everything else in this module does.

## Marcus, the college website, and the question nobody asked him

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: THE NUMBER THAT DECIDES WHETHER RETRAINING WORKS

[spotlight] The tuition is rarely what sinks a career change. The months without full income are. Before enrolling, work out one figure: total months of reduced income, multiplied by the gap between what you will earn during training and what you need for fixed costs. Compare that to what you actually have available: savings, a partner’s income, a living allowance if your provincial program offers one, EI if you qualify while in approved training. If the number is uncomfortable, the answer is usually not to abandon the plan. It is to change its shape, a part-time program over eighteen months instead of full-time over eight, or a short certification that moves you sideways into a better-paying role first. People who take on high-interest debt to bridge a training period often finish the program and start the new job already behind. The credential was fine. The financing was the problem.

[story] Marcus and the college website
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ Marcus is forty-four and drove a delivery route in Regina for eleven years until his back stopped cooperating with the loading. He decided on a dispatch and logistics certificate, found the program at a local college, and looked at the tuition on a Sunday night. Then he did what most people do, which is nothing, for four months.
  ¶ When he finally called the college’s financial aid office they were helpful and told him about student loans. That is the answer a college gives, because it is the answer a college has. He filled in a loan application, felt worse about the whole thing, and stalled again.
  ¶ What changed it was a conversation at a walk-in employment centre, which he only visited to ask about something else. The counsellor’s first question was not what do you want to study. It was whether he was currently working, and whether he was leaving a physically demanding trade for medical reasons. Both answers put him in a lane he did not know existed: provincial retraining support, which in his case covered tuition and provided a modest allowance during the program.
  ¶ The second thing she told him was to look at his Notice of Assessment, where he found a training credit limit that had been accumulating since he was in his thirties. He had not asked the wrong question. He had asked the right question of an organization that could only ever give him one answer.

[takeaway] Four lanes, and the lane decides everything: employer-applied job grants, provincial retraining programs, tax-based tools you claim yourself, and the separate apprenticeship system. Check your Notice of Assessment before you do anything else, confirm the program is open today rather than famous, and never assume the first organization you called had the whole picture.

## Glossary

[glossary]
  - Canada Training Credit — A refundable federal credit that accumulates annually on your tax file and is claimed against eligible tuition and fees.
  - Lifelong Learning Plan — A tax-free RRSP withdrawal to fund full-time training, repaid on a set schedule.
  - Job Grant — The family of provincial programs that fund employer-purchased training for existing staff. The employer applies, not the employee.
  - Labour Market Transfer Agreements — The federal-provincial arrangements that fund most retraining programs. The reason the money is federal and the program name is provincial.
  - Canada Apprentice Loan — Interest-free borrowing for registered apprentices during technical training, available before an EI claim is processed.
  - Red Seal trade — A trade with a national standard and interprovincial certification. Many apprenticeship supports are limited to these.
  - Notice of Assessment — The CRA summary issued after you file. Where your training credit limit appears.

[lists]
  - WHERE TO GET HELP — CRA My Account: to check your Canada Training Credit limit, and to file the return that keeps it building.; Your province’s employment services office: for job grants and retraining eligibility assessments.; The Canada Apprentice Loan program: for apprenticeship financing, applied for separately from an EI claim.; Your training provider’s financial aid office: useful for provider questions, but not the place to search for funding.

[paragraph] This module skips dollar figures on purpose. Program status, credit amounts and provincial rules in this field change often. Confirm every figure on the program’s own page the day you need it.
