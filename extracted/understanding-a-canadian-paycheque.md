## Start here

[paragraph] Your pay stub is not lying to you. It just isn’t explaining itself.

[paragraph] You earn a number. A smaller number lands in your account. In between, five or six lines take a bite. Most of us just accept the final number and move on.

[paragraph] That’s fine, until something changes. A raise. A new job. A side gig. Suddenly the math on your stub doesn’t match what you expected, and nobody ever taught you why.

[paragraph] This module fixes that. Short version: every deduction on your pay stub has a name, a purpose, and a limit. Once you know all three, the stub stops being a mystery.

## The five lines on almost every stub

[paragraph] Different employers format this differently. But nearly every Canadian pay stub breaks down into the same five pieces.

[cards]
  - Gross pay — What you earned before anything comes off.
  - CPP — Canada Pension Plan. Goes toward your retirement.
  - EI — Employment Insurance. Covers you if you lose your job or take certain leaves.
  - Income tax — Federal and provincial, combined into one line or split into two.
  - Net pay — What actually lands in your account.

[paragraph] That’s the whole shape. Now let’s take each one apart.

## CPP: the one that builds your future

[paragraph] CPP is not a tax. It’s a forced savings plan.

[paragraph] Every dollar taken off today comes back to you later, as a monthly pension starting as early as age 60. Your employer matches it, dollar for dollar. Nobody gets to opt out.

[paragraph] In 2026, you pay 5.95% on your earnings, but not on all of them. The first $3,500 you earn each year is exempt. Above $74,600, CPP stops taking that rate entirely.

[paragraph] There’s a second, smaller layer called CPP2. It applies only to earnings between $74,600 and $85,000, at a lower rate of 4%. Most people never touch it. High earners do.

[paragraph] Put together, the most anyone pays into CPP in 2026 is $4,646.45. After that, your paycheque simply stops shrinking for this reason, for the rest of the year.

[myths]
  - “CPP is money the government keeps.” — It isn’t. It’s your money, held in a plan you can’t touch until retirement, that your employer is legally required to match. Think of it less like a tax and more like a locked savings account nobody can raid, including you, until you’re eligible.

## EI: the safety net you hope to never use

[paragraph] Employment Insurance is smaller than CPP. It’s simpler too.

[paragraph] You pay 1.63% of your earnings in 2026, up to a cap. Once you earn $68,900 in the year, EI stops taking anything more. The most anyone pays is $1,123.07.

[paragraph] Your employer pays a share too, about one and a half times what you pay. That’s their cost, not yours, but it’s worth knowing it exists.

[paragraph] What does EI actually buy you? Income support if you’re laid off. Paid time during parental leave. Coverage during some illnesses and caregiving situations. You hope to never need it. Most Canadians eventually do, at some point in their working life.

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: THE YEAR YOU SWITCH JOBS, WATCH FOR THIS

[spotlight] CPP and EI both have yearly caps. Once you hit them, deductions stop for the rest of the year. Here’s the catch. Each employer tracks your earnings separately. If you work two jobs, or switch jobs partway through the year, both employers may deduct CPP and EI from scratch, even if you’d already maxed out at your old job. The good news: you get that overpayment back. It shows up as a credit when you file your tax return. The bad news: you won’t see it until then, which means a year with a job change can mean smaller paycheques now and a bigger refund later. Knowing this in advance means it won’t feel like a mistake when it happens.

## Income tax: the part everyone assumes is one flat cut

[paragraph] It isn’t. Canada’s income tax is layered, like a set of shelves.

[paragraph] Your first chunk of income is taxed at the lowest rate. The next chunk, a little higher. And so on, climbing as your income climbs. Only the portion of your income sitting on a given shelf gets that shelf’s rate.

[paragraph] For 2026, the federal shelves look like this. Up to $58,523, taxed at 14%. From there to $117,045, taxed at 20.5%. From there to $181,440, taxed at 26%. Higher still, 29%, then 33% above $258,482.

[paragraph] Nobody pays 33% on their whole income. Only the slice above $258,482 gets that rate. Everything below still sits on its own, lower shelf.

[paragraph] On top of federal tax, your province adds its own layers, with its own shelves and its own rates. That’s why two people earning the same salary in different provinces can take home different amounts. We’ll unpack this fully in the next module, since the whole system deserves its own close look.

[myths]
  - “A raise that bumps me into a higher bracket means I’ll take home less money.” — This one is almost never true, and it stops more people from asking for raises than anything else in this module. Moving into a higher bracket only means your next dollars are taxed at the higher rate. Every dollar you already earn keeps its old, lower rate. You always come out ahead, just not by quite as much as the raise alone would suggest.

## The slice of your income that’s never taxed at all

[paragraph] Before any of those shelves apply, there’s a floor.

[paragraph] It’s called the Basic Personal Amount. In 2026, it’s $16,452 federally. Think of it as the first stretch of your income that gets a free pass, no federal tax touches it, no matter what you earn.

[paragraph] Every Canadian gets this. Higher earners get a slightly smaller version of it, but everyone gets some. It’s automatically built into your paycheque deductions, you don’t need to apply for it or ask for it.

[paragraph] Your province has its own version too, at its own amount.

[cards]
  - CPP (base) — Deduction: 5.95% — 2026 rate: $74,600 of earnings
  - CPP2 — Deduction: 4.00% — 2026 rate: $85,000 of earnings
  - EI — Deduction: 1.63% — 2026 rate: $68,900 of earnings
  - Income tax — Deduction: 14% to 33%, layered — 2026 rate: Never, rate climbs with income

## Reading your own stub

[chips]
  - I can find gross pay, and I know it’s the starting number, not the final one.
  - I can find CPP and EI as two separate lines, not one combined deduction.
  - I know whether my income tax line is federal only, or federal and provincial combined.
  - I know roughly where I sit on the federal tax shelves this year.
  - I know if I’ve changed jobs this year, since that can mean CPP and EI restart from zero.
  - I know net pay is what’s left after all of the above, not a separate deduction itself.

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: WHY YOUR FIRST PAYCHEQUE OF THE YEAR LOOKS DIFFERENT

[spotlight] Notice your take-home pay creeping up partway through the year, even with no raise? That’s CPP or EI hitting its yearly cap. Once you’ve paid the maximum for the year, that line disappears from your stub until January resets it. It works the other way too. Your very first paycheque each January can look smaller than your December one, even at the exact same salary, because CPP and EI are both starting fresh. Neither of these is an error. Both are the caps doing exactly what they’re built to do.

## Devon’s first real paycheque

[story] Devon’s first real paycheque
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ Devon got his first full-time offer at twenty-three. Forty-eight thousand a year, in Alberta. He did the math in his head on the drive home: forty-eight thousand, divided by twenty-six pay periods, felt like a number he could build a life around.
  ¶ His first stub landed with about four hundred dollars less than he’d calculated. He assumed payroll had made a mistake.
  ¶ He texted a friend who worked in accounting, mostly to vent. She asked him to read her the lines on the stub instead. CPP. EI. Federal tax. Provincial tax. She talked him through each one in under five minutes.
  ¶ Nothing was wrong. Devon had just never been taught that gross pay and take-home pay are two different numbers, separated by predictable, well-labelled pieces. Once he understood the shape of it, he stopped opening his stub with a small knot of dread every two weeks.

[takeaway] Your stub breaks into five predictable pieces: gross pay, CPP, EI, income tax, and net pay. CPP and EI both stop once you hit a yearly cap. Income tax is layered, not flat, a raise never shrinks your take-home pay. And a job change partway through the year can mean CPP and EI restart, which evens out at tax time.

## Glossary

[glossary]
  - Gross pay — What you earned, before any deductions.
  - Net pay — What actually lands in your account, after every deduction.
  - CPP — Canada Pension Plan. Forced retirement savings, matched by your employer.
  - CPP2 — A second, smaller CPP layer on earnings between two set thresholds.
  - EI — Employment Insurance. Covers job loss, parental leave, and some illness or caregiving leave.
  - Basic Personal Amount — The slice of income that is never taxed federally, given to every taxpayer.
  - Tax bracket — A range of income taxed at one specific rate. Higher brackets apply only to the income above them.

[paragraph] Educational only, not tax advice. Figures shown are 2026 federal rates. Confirm your own province and year before relying on any number here.
