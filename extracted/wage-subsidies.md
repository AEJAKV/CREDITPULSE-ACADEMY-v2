## Start here

[paragraph] Run the arithmetic on a summer position. You pay the wage every two weeks, plus the employer’s share of CPP and EI, plus vacation pay, plus workers’ compensation. You supervise, which costs you the time of someone more expensive than the hire.

[paragraph] A wage subsidy is a partial reimbursement arriving late. It is not a discount at the till.

[heading] What this course does

[paragraph] Small employers use these programs every year to hire people they could not otherwise afford, and they also get squeezed every July by employers who planned the subsidy as a discount instead of a rebate. This module sorts which program fits which hire, what actually gets reimbursed, and how to survive the gap between paying payroll and getting money back.

## Three questions before you post the job

[paragraph] Would you make this hire anyway? If the honest answer is no, stop. A subsidy that funds half the wage still leaves you paying the other half, plus the on-costs, plus the supervision, for work you did not need. The programs are built to make a marginal hire possible, not to make an unnecessary hire free. Most also carry a displacement rule: you cannot use a subsidised hire to replace existing staff, or to fill a spot someone was recently laid off from. Program officers do check.

[paragraph] Can you carry the full cost until the money arrives? Reimbursement timing is the whole game. Some programs pay after the placement ends. Some pay in instalments against interim claims. A few offer an advance. Find out which before you make an offer, and plan for the worst case: a claim with a documentation problem takes longer than a clean one.

[paragraph] Who is doing the paperwork? Every program wants records: signed agreements, timesheets, payroll registers, proof the employee qualifies, interim and final reports. In a business with no HR department, this lands on whoever is already busiest. Decide who that is before you apply, not in August when the report is due and the person who knew the file has gone back to school.

[myths]
  - “The subsidy covers the whole cost for a not-for-profit, so it is free.” — Not-for-profits can receive substantially more. In the summer jobs program, up to the full provincial minimum wage plus mandatory employment costs, where private employers receive up to half. But if you pay above minimum wage, you cover the difference yourself. Reimbursement is calculated against the minimum, not against what you chose to pay.

## The map: which program fits which hire

[paragraph] Federal wage support is organized around who you are hiring, not what your business does. Find the person, then the program.

[lists]
  - Hiring a young person for the summer — Canada Summer Jobs, for youth aged fifteen to thirty.; Open to not-for-profits, public sector, and private employers with fifty or fewer full-time staff.; Applications run months ahead of summer. The deadline disqualifies most first-time applicants, not the merit.
  - Hiring a post-secondary student for a placement or co-op — The Student Work Placement Program, delivered through sector-specific partner organizations rather than government directly.; Rolling intake, which makes it one of the more usable programs in the country.
  - Hiring a first-year apprentice — The federal apprenticeship hiring incentive is between intakes, with a successor announced but not yet open.; What remains today is the Apprenticeship Job Creation Tax Credit: a non-refundable credit against apprentice wages in Red Seal trades, worth nothing to a business with no tax payable.; Several provinces run their own apprentice incentives on top.
  - Hiring a young person into a technical or digital role — The Youth Employment and Skills Strategy funds a family of programs through delivery partners.; Research-focused placements run through Mitacs and the National Research Council’s youth programs.
  - Hiring a person with a disability — The Opportunities Fund supports wage subsidies and workplace accommodation costs, delivered through community organizations.; Accommodation funding is separate from the wage support, and frequently unclaimed.
  - Provincial and sectoral programs — Sit on top of all of this, and in several provinces are now larger than the federal equivalents.; Your provincial employment ministry and your industry association are the two places to check.

[paragraph] The distinction that matters most across all of them is when the money moves.

[cards]
  - Gross wages — When you pay it: Every pay period, from day one — What comes back: A defined share, after the claim is processed
  - Employer CPP, EI, vacation pay — When you pay it: Every pay period — What comes back: Covered in some programs, not others. Check.
  - Workers’ compensation premiums — When you pay it: On your usual schedule — What comes back: Sometimes included as a mandatory cost
  - Supervision and training time — When you pay it: Continuously — What comes back: Nothing

## The gap between payroll and reimbursement

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: THE GAP BETWEEN PAYROLL AND REIMBURSEMENT

[spotlight] This is where wage subsidies turn into a credit problem for small employers, and it is entirely predictable. Take a sixteen-week placement. You fund the full payroll for sixteen weeks, submit the claim within the window after it ends, and wait for processing. Depending on the program and your paperwork, the money can land four to twelve weeks after that. Call it five months of carrying the full cost, on a business that applied for the subsidy precisely because it did not have five months of spare payroll. Three ways through it. Ask whether the program offers advances or interim claims: several do, and almost nobody asks. Time the placement so the reimbursement lands before your next seasonal squeeze rather than during it. And if you must borrow, price an operating line against a business credit card before you use the card by default, because over five months on a full payroll the difference is not a rounding error. Then submit the claim the week the placement ends. Every week you delay is a week of financing cost you chose.

## Stacking, and the arithmetic nobody mentions

[paragraph] Programs can often be combined: a federal wage subsidy with a provincial training grant, or a subsidy on one hire and a different program on another. Two rules govern it, and both are easy to breach by accident.

[paragraph] First, total government assistance generally cannot exceed one hundred percent of the wages actually paid. Stacking two subsidies on the same employee’s same hours is the classic disqualifier, and it is usually discovered at audit rather than at application.

[paragraph] Second, government assistance reduces what you can claim elsewhere. If a subsidised employee’s work would otherwise qualify for research and development credits, only your net out-of-pocket wage cost counts toward that claim. The subsidy is still worth more than the credit it displaces almost every time, but a budget built on getting both in full is a wrong budget.

[paragraph] The subsidy itself is generally taxable income to the business, or it reduces the deductible wage expense, depending on how it is structured. Ask the program which, and tell your accountant before year-end rather than after.

[myths]
  - “Once the subsidy is approved, the position is funded.” — Approval is conditional on the placement running as described, the documentation being complete, and the claim arriving inside the deadline, often as short as thirty days after the job ends. Money already advanced can be clawed back when reporting is missed. The application is the beginning of the obligation, not the end of it.

## Before you make the offer

[chips]
  - I would make this hire even at full cost, or close to it.
  - I have confirmed in writing what is reimbursed: wages only, or wages plus mandatory employment costs.
  - I know the claim deadline after the placement ends, and who is responsible for filing it.
  - I have modelled carrying full payroll until the reimbursement lands.
  - The funding agreement is signed before I extend the offer.
  - I have checked that no other program is already funding these same hours.

[paragraph] That fifth line is not pedantry. Extending an offer before the agreement is signed is one of the most common ways employers disqualify themselves, and it is also how a small business ends up honouring a job offer with no subsidy behind it.

## Dev, the sixteen-week placement, and the June that nearly went wrong

[heading] CREDIT PULSE SPOTLIGHT: WHAT THE SUBSIDY IS ACTUALLY BUYING YOU

[spotlight] Treated as a discount, a wage subsidy saves you some money on a summer position and is forgotten by September. Treated as a recruiting instrument, it is the cheapest hiring pipeline available to a small Canadian employer. The people who use these programs well hire the same way every year, keep the good ones on afterward, and build a relationship with the delivery partner or the local employment office, which is how they hear about intakes early and how their applications get stronger each cycle. A student placement program in particular is a low-risk sixteen-week interview for a role you would otherwise fill by guessing from a resume. Weigh that against the cost of a bad permanent hire: the recruitment, the ramp-up, the severance, the six months spent hoping it improves. A subsidised placement is not a saving on wages. It is risk reduction with a rebate attached.

[story] Dev and the June that nearly went wrong
  detail: DEDICATED STORY
  ¶ Dev runs a six-person landscaping company outside Kamloops. He had used the summer jobs program twice before and treated the subsidy as a discount, which worked fine both times because both times he had a cushion. In the third year the cushion was gone. He had bought a second truck in April, and by mid-June he was covering two subsidised wages plus payroll costs, with the reimbursement not due until September.
  ¶ What saved it was a question he had never thought to ask. Chasing something else with the program contact, he mentioned the cash-flow squeeze, and she told him that many funded employers can request an advance, and that a surprising number never do. He asked. It was not the full amount and it did not arrive instantly, but it moved enough money forward to keep him off the business credit card for the summer.
  ¶ The second thing he changed was the calendar. The following year he submitted the final claim the same week the placements ended, rather than in the general September pile of things he intended to get to. The money arrived roughly a month earlier than it had the year before, for no reason other than that he had sent it earlier.
  ¶ He also kept one of the students. She had spent sixteen weeks demonstrating she could handle irrigation systems and difficult customers, which is more than any interview had ever told him about anyone. Her wage in year two was not subsidised at all, and Dev has since described that as the best return the program ever gave him.

[takeaway] A wage subsidy is a partial reimbursement arriving late, not a discount applied up front. Only hire someone you would hire anyway, sign the funding agreement before you extend the offer, model carrying full payroll until the money lands, ask about advances and interim claims, and file the claim the week the placement ends.

## Your action this week

[ordered]
  - Identify who you would hire in the next twelve months, student, youth, apprentice, someone needing accommodation, and find the program that matches that person.

  - Ask the program contact two questions: are mandatory employment-related costs reimbursed, and do you offer advances or interim claims? Almost nobody asks the second one.

## Glossary

[glossary]
  - MERCs — Mandatory employment-related costs. The employer’s share of CPP, EI, vacation pay and similar. Reimbursed by some programs and not others.
  - Displacement rule — The prohibition on using a subsidised hire to replace an existing or recently laid-off employee.
  - Interim claim — A partial reimbursement filed during a placement rather than after it. Where offered, it materially shortens the cash-flow gap.
  - Delivery partner — The sector organization that administers a federal program on the government’s behalf. For several programs you apply to them, not to Ottawa.
  - Stacking cap — The limit, usually one hundred percent of wages paid, on combined government assistance for the same hours.
  - AJCTC — Apprenticeship Job Creation Tax Credit. A non-refundable federal credit on apprentice wages in Red Seal trades. No tax payable means no benefit.
  - Clawback — Recovery of funds already paid, typically triggered by missed reporting or a placement that did not run as described.

[lists]
  - WHERE TO GET HELP — Employment and Social Development Canada: 1-800-622-6232, for Canada Summer Jobs and the youth employment programs.; Student Work Placement delivery partners: apply through the sector organization covering your industry, not through a central portal.; Your provincial employment ministry and your industry association, for provincial and sectoral subsidies that often exceed the federal ones.; Your accountant, before year-end: on whether a subsidy is taxable income or reduces wage expense, and what it does to other credits you claim.

[paragraph] This module skips dollar figures on purpose. Subsidy caps and per-placement maximums are revised most years, while cost-share ratios and eligibility categories are more durable. Confirm current amounts on the program’s page before you budget.
